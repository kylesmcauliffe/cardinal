## 02. The Orphan and the Mine

Narrator (Kore): The Orphan and the Mine.
Narrator (Kore): Jump back fifteen years, to a campus that is not finished.
Narrator (Kore): Herbert Hoover, the Iowa orphan last hour let into Stanford because he passed mathematics and failed the rest, arrived in the summer of eighteen ninety-one, before opening day, living on the farm while the sandstone was still going up. He ran a laundry agency and a lecture bureau. He wrote a friend that he was working awful hard and had schemes for making more. The hunger was not a joke.
Narrator (Kore): At the start of his second semester a geologist named John Casper Branner arrived to chair the department. Hoover could type. Branner hired him as office assistant at twenty-five dollars a month, and Hoover kept the job until he graduated. Twenty-five dollars in the eighteen nineties, roughly, is on the order of a thousand dollars a month in our grocery money. It was not a living. It was a door. Hoover's own rule, told to a classmate, was simple. Do your work so they notice it, and always be on the job.
Narrator (Kore): He graduated in the pioneer class of eighteen ninety-five, in geology, with a degree and almost no money. His first job was pushing ore carts in a Nevada mine. The degree had not delivered a career. It had delivered a person a London banker could be talked into trusting.
Narrator (Kore): Call him the engineer as a fixer. In the eighteen nineties, investors in the City of London, the financial district, not the city of churches, were putting money into holes in Western Australia, South Africa, and China. They could not see the holes. Somebody had to go and look, price the risk, buy the machinery, hire the men, keep books an auditor in London could read, and talk to whatever government claimed the ground. The scarce thing was not dynamite. It was a report a stranger would believe. A degree from a new university on a horse farm was, for a few years, a strange but usable proof.
Narrator (Kore): The recommendation that moved him was not an application he filled out. Louis Janin, a San Francisco mining engineer, passed him to the London firm of Bewick, Moreing and Company. In eighteen ninety-seven, at twenty-three, they sent him to Western Australia.
Narrator (Kore): Coolgardie and Kalgoorlie were inland, hot, short of water, and full of money. He told the firm which holes to buy. He recommended the Sons of Gwalia. The name is Welsh. The mine made his reputation.
Narrator (Kore): Jump to nineteen five, the year Jane Stanford dies in Honolulu, and eight years after Hoover first went down that mine. The parliament of Western Australia appointed a Royal Commission on the Ventilation and Sanitation of Mines. A royal commission is not a royal visit. It is a government inquiry with the power to compel testimony and write a judgment. This one was hard on most of the operators in the goldfields. Workers, the record of the inquiry said, were unduly exposed to dust and fumes and falling rock. At the Sons of Gwalia, the commission found the air notably bad.
Narrator (Kore): Kenneth Whyte, in his life of Hoover, notes that the same parliament was launching six or eight royal commissions a year, some of them on questions as small as stone or stucco for the new law courts. A government that inquires into everything will inquire into you. That takes some of the sting out. It does not take the finding out. The question of how a young engineer got his results followed him.
Narrator (Kore): He was twenty-three at the mine. The commission spoke when he was thirty-one. By then the firm had sent him to China. In February eighteen ninety-nine he married Lou Henry in Monterey, and they sailed the next day.
Narrator (Kore): Lou Henry is not a wife in the margin. A guest lecture by Branner in Monterey captured her. She talked her parents into geology, the only woman in the program, degree in eighteen ninety-eight. Whyte sets their first meeting in Branner's lab. She expected someone brawny. What walked in was speechless, hair uncombed, tanned from summer work. She did not look away.
Narrator (Kore): Two people who can read the same rock. When the war comes, the skill that matters is the mining engineer's list. Survey, account, price the risk, move food, talk to governments that hate each other. He had practiced it underground.
Narrator (Kore): By the time the guns started he was a London partner on London Wall, two doors from the rooms where he was about to try to feed a country. The orphan from Iowa had become a man the City used to look at the underside of the earth.
Narrator (Kore): Jump forward. Not to the war yet. To the boardroom. The mine boss is about to sit down at the table that owns the farm.

## 04. Bionomics
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: mozart40_2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: fs_42139.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Bionomics.
Narrator (Kore): Nineteen sixteen. A Stanford psychologist named Lewis Madison Terman published a book called The Measurement of Intelligence. Inside it was a test. He called the test the Stanford-Binet. He called the number it produced an intelligence quotient. The world shortened that to IQ.
Narrator (Kore): Say the word bionomics once, because David Starr Jordan loved it. He was a great ichthyologist, the first president of this university, a pacifist at real cost, and a eugenicist. Bionomics, in his mouth, was the claim that one science could cover heredity, history, economics, and ethics, and then tell a society what to do.
Narrator (Kore): Eugenics is the idea that a nation should breed human beings the way a farmer breeds livestock, encouraging the births it calls fit and discouraging or preventing the births it calls unfit. In those decades this was mainstream progressive science. University presidents held it. So did public health officers. The biology was wrong. The structure was useful. If poverty and what they called feeblemindedness are mostly inherited, then the hierarchy you see is nature, and a university endowed by a railroad monopoly is not defending a class. It is defending the species.
Narrator (Kore): California wrote it into law. In nineteen nine the state authorized the medical superintendents of state institutions to operate on inmates when, in the superintendent's judgment, it would improve the person's physical, mental, or moral condition. No consent from the patient. No court order. Over the following decades California sterilized more people under that authority than any other state. Later counts put the state's share at roughly a third of the recorded sterilizations in the United States. The counts do not agree with each other, because the people this was done to were not carefully counted. The disagreement is part of the harm.
Narrator (Kore): Lewis Terman arrived at Stanford in nineteen ten, with a wife, Anna, and two small children, Fred and Helen. Malcolm Harris notes why they were in California at all. Terman's young adulthood was marred by a long and debilitating course of tuberculosis, and the illness required the move to a mild climate. Fred, the son who will build the industry, came west because his father could not stay east and breathe. Then the father set out to measure minds.
Narrator (Kore): The test he adapted was not his invention. In nineteen five, in France, Alfred Binet had built a test to find children who were falling behind in school, so they could be helped. Binet warned against using it as a measure of inborn worth. Terman and his Stanford team did the thing Binet had warned against. They translated the items, reset the age norms for American schools, and forced the scores into a curve whose average would always be one hundred. Mental age divided by actual age, times one hundred. A single number for a whole child.
Narrator (Kore): Stanford Magazine, looking back, is blunt. Terman believed general intelligence was an original endowment. Genetics alone dictated it. School and hard work did not change it. In nineteen sixteen he put the test and the manifesto in the same book. A child could finish in about fifty minutes. The next year he helped design the Army's tests. More than one point seven million draftees took them. A campus instrument became a government instrument in a year.
Narrator (Kore): One point seven million soldiers, set against a classroom. His long study of gifted children began with six hundred forty-three California children at or above one hundred thirty-five. He called it Genetic Studies of Genius. He pledged not to publish their names. He enrolled his own son and daughter.
Narrator (Kore): And he missed one. Malcolm Harris records that the study passed over the only child of a Stanford engineering professor. William Shockley. Score, one hundred twenty-nine, just under the line Terman was using for genius. Harris's phrase is that the IQ ranked slightly subgenius.
Narrator (Kore): Remember the number. One hundred twenty-nine against a line at about one hundred thirty-five. Six points. The boy who missed the cut would share the Nobel Prize in Physics in nineteen fifty-six for the transistor, and then argue a eugenics of his own. Fred, already inside his father's study, would build the industry. We are not there yet. The measuring stick is about to walk out of the psychology department and into the world.
Narrator (Kore): A rank is a moral decision pretending to be a ruler. Once you have a single number, you can sort a school, an army, an immigration desk, a sterilization ward, and say you are only following the measurement. Binet wanted a tool for help. Terman built a tool for order.
Narrator (Kore): Jordan would leave the presidency in nineteen thirteen, but the science he had sheltered did not leave with him. Lewis Terman taught until nineteen forty-three and died in nineteen fifty-six, the year of Shockley's Nobel. The father ranks children. The son builds an industry. That sentence is the hinge of this hour. Fred Terman is the son. We will meet him in a garage, and then at a laboratory at Harvard, and then on a train home.
Narrator (Kore): Jump back two years from the book, to the summer the war began. The engineer is about to discover that logistics is a kind of power, and that paper is a kind of weapon.

## 05. Feeding Belgium
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms3_3 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: telegraph.mp3] [ERA: radio]

Narrator (Kore): Feeding Belgium.
Narrator (Kore): June twenty-eighth, nineteen fourteen. A Serbian nationalist killed the Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. Hoover, in London, told himself it was just another Balkan lapse into barbarism, and went back to work. He was wrong, and he was in the right city to find out how wrong.
Narrator (Kore): On the afternoon of August fourth, a few hours after German troops crossed into neutral Belgium, Hoover walked a little more than a city block to the American consulate. That walk is the scene. One block. A mining engineer with an office on London Wall becomes the man who will try to feed seven and a half million people.
Narrator (Kore): Learn Belgium in three facts. About seven and a half million people. It grew only about twenty percent of the food it ate. The German army took the harvest, and the British blockade, meant to starve Germany, also closed the occupied country. Between them, a nation that imported four pounds of every five it ate began to beg.
Narrator (Kore): His first errand was small. An American engineer named Millard Shaler had fifteen hundred tons of cereals stuck on a dock. Hoover saw that fifteen hundred tons would not touch a country. He built the Commission for the Relief of Belgium, the CRB. A private, neutral machine in London, buying food around the world, landing it at Rotterdam, moving it by canal, handing it to Belgians to distribute. He wanted, in his own words, the strongest centralization and an effective monopoly. Absolute command of his half. No knitting bee.
Narrator (Kore): He also ran a campaign he named himself.
QUOTE (Charon, flat documentary): enormous propaganda.
Narrator (Kore): That is Hoover's phrase, recorded by Whyte. He browbeat American newspapers and governors. He hinted to one state that another state had already given more. He told reporters to go see the famine and to play up what he called the detailed personal horror stuff. He sent a motion-picture crew to film the food lines in Brussels. He hired famous writers. George Bernard Shaw sent a sentence.
QUOTE (Charon, flat documentary): one Belgian goes without a full meal whilst thousands of lapdogs are living in luxury from Canterbury to San Francisco.
Narrator (Kore): A cable went out. I will read it the way a cable sounds.
LETTER (Algieba: Herbert Hoover, a clipped cable from London, autumn 1914, no adjectives): "Over one million people on bread line in Belgium at present moment with supplies estimated to last from one to three weeks. We shall require upwards of twenty thousand tons foodstuffs monthly."
Narrator (Kore): Twenty thousand tons a month. The first gift ship, thirty-five hundred tons of beans, bacon, flour, and rice, reached Holland on November twenty-first. One ship, a sixth of a month. He got two great British lines to carry the food free, and the piers and the telegraph too. He treated charity as a logistics firm.
Narrator (Kore): You cannot move twenty thousand tons a month on pity alone. The ships sail on a schedule, so the story has to sail on a schedule. A true story, repeated with discipline, is a kind of track. The food runs on it. He named the craft himself. When he later hates the stories told about him, remember that he had already called the method propaganda.
Narrator (Kore): When the United States entered the war, Wilson made him Food Administrator. His name became a verb. To Hooverize meant to clean your plate, to skip the meat, to waste nothing. Reporters expected a giant and found an unobtrusive man at a desk. The verb was larger than the body. A successful campaign replaces the person with an instruction.
Narrator (Kore): Now jump to nineteen nineteen. The war is over. Hoover is in Paris, advising Wilson at the peace conference, and he does something that will outlast the verb.
Narrator (Kore): He had conceived, while he was feeding Belgium, of gathering the paper of the war. In June nineteen nineteen he sent a telegram to Stanford's president, Ray Lyman Wilbur. The Hoover Institution still calls that telegram its founding document. Hoover offered fifty thousand dollars and told Wilbur to send Professor Ephraim D. Adams to Paris to start the collecting. Fifty thousand dollars in nineteen nineteen is roughly nine hundred sixty thousand dollars in our grocery money. For that, he bought the beginning of an archive.
Narrator (Kore): Adams went. So did Ralph Lutz and Frank Golder. They gathered pamphlets, newspapers, posters, and government documents across Europe. The founding collections include the files of the Commission for the Relief of Belgium, the United States Food Administration, and the American Relief Administration. In nineteen twenty-two the collection was renamed the Hoover War Library. That year it took in forty thousand documents, housed in the Stanford library.
Narrator (Kore): Forty thousand documents in one year. A library is a ledger. Whoever keeps the record owns the ground. He put the record of a war on his own campus, under his own name. After he lost the presidency he would watch the anti-Hoover literature pile up on shelves he had paid for. The archive does not love you. It keeps you.
Narrator (Kore): While he was feeding a country and filing its papers, another industry, down the coast, was learning to sell the West back to the people who lived in it. Jump to a tavern on a corner in Hollywood. Late October, nineteen eleven. We are between our anchor years on purpose. The myth machine does not wait for a round number.

Narrator (Kore): Not the first movie set in Los Angeles. The first permanent studio in Hollywood. Its first California picture was a western, The Law of the Range, out December thirteenth, nineteen eleven. One reel, about nine hundred fifty feet. The ad called it a rousing Western foto-drama, taken in the West. The review said it was full of struggles, a chase, a posse, a hold-up, and that the photographs were not very good.
NEWS (Sadachbia: a 1911 trade-paper reviewer, brisk, slightly disappointed): "The story is interesting, but the photographs are not very good."
Narrator (Kore): Start there. Not with a masterpiece. With a cheap chase, shot in the sun, by men who had left New Jersey because Thomas Edison's lawyers were winning.
Narrator (Kore): The Motion Picture Patents Company was the Trust. Edison and a handful of licensed companies held the patents on cameras and projectors. Pay them, or fight them. A trust, here, is a combination that uses patent law as a gate. We have seen this gate. Huntington's railroad. The Associated Press franchise. A rule that looks like property and behaves like a toll. The independents went west in part because California was far from the process servers.
Narrator (Kore): The sun was a product before it was a backdrop. On January thirty-first, eighteen seventy-four, Thomas Balch Elliott and his Indiana neighbors formed the colony that became Pasadena. Many of them had come for a milder climate, sick with asthma or tuberculosis. Lyra Kilston, following John Baur's history of those health seekers, records the house that joked about the cure. In eighteen eighty-two Emma Bangs kept a Pasadena boarding house the town called the busted lung brigade. A parrot there had learned to cough exactly like a lunger. Hollywood inherited that migration. A cure, then a chase. The wage is the part with a number.
Narrator (Kore): Los Angeles was an open-shop town. Open shop means you can work without joining a union, and the courts helped employers keep it that way. When the studios needed carpenters and electricians to build a street that looked like Wyoming, the wage was the point. On The Squaw Man, Cecil B. DeMille noted that carpenters and other hands cost twenty-five to fifty percent less than on the East Coast. Public television's Lost LA records the note.
Narrator (Kore): Twenty-five to fifty percent. That is the number. A carpenter in Los Angeles, nineteen thirteen, at three-quarters of a New York wage, or at half. Myth-making locates itself where the payroll is cheaper and the lawyers are farther away.
Narrator (Kore): The Squaw Man is the feature people mean when they say Hollywood grew up. December nineteen thirteen. DeMille got on a train. The plan was Flagstaff, Arizona. The story he later told is the one you have heard. It was colder than when he left New York. Everybody back on the train, all the way to California.
Narrator (Kore): The story goes. Public television says that version is widely taken to be apocryphal. Shooting started December twenty-ninth. To save three dollars, he played a faro dealer himself.
Narrator (Kore): The cold-train story is the legend, and the people who love it call it shaky. The Christmas Eve lease is a document. The wage gap is a document. Hollywood's origin is a budget and a patent fight, wearing a cowboy hat.
Narrator (Kore): And the prehistory of the screen was shot on this farm. In eighteen seventy-eight, on Leland Stanford's stock farm, Eadweard Muybridge set a row of cameras to see whether a horse ever had all four hooves off the ground. It did. Those frames are the ancestor of the motion picture, paid for by the railroad president whose laurel tie would burn in nineteen six. Cinema begins here as a tool for proving something about a rich man's horse, and becomes a factory that sells his West back to the country at a discount on the carpenters.
Narrator (Kore): The banker and the myth machine meet next, in a name on a building and a column in a square. The story of our streets.

## 07. The Column in the Square

Narrator (Kore): The Column in the Square.
Narrator (Kore): If you have stood in Union Square, you have stood under a granite shaft with a bronze Victory on top, wreath in one hand, trident in the other. A lot of people think that column is for General MacArthur. It is not.
Narrator (Kore): On May first, eighteen ninety-eight, Commodore George Dewey's squadron destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay in a morning. The war was short. The empire was not.
