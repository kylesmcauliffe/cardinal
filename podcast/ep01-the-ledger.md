Narrator (Kore): Let me teach this one properly, because a California mission was four institutions in one.
Narrator (Kore): One: a church. Its stated purpose was conversion, to bring Native people to Catholic Christianity. A baptized Native person was called a neófito, a neophyte, a new believer. An unbaptized one was a gentil, a gentile.
Narrator (Kore): Two: an economy. A mission was a farm, a ranch, a workshop, and a warehouse. And at their height they controlled roughly one-sixth of all the land in Alta California.
Narrator (Kore): Three: a military partner. Every mission sat in the district of a presidio, a fort with a small garrison of soldiers. The soldiers protected the friars, and they also went out to bring back people who left.
Narrator (Kore): Four: a labor system. This is the part the sugar-cube models leave out. Native people from dozens of communities were brought to live together at the mission, and,

Narrator (Kore): Secularization.
Narrator (Kore): Now jump back from Dana's voyage to 1821. Mexico wins its independence from Spain, and with it, the far-off province of Alta California becomes Mexican.
Narrator (Kore): The new Mexican republic had a problem with the missions. They were run by Spanish-born friars loyal to a church still tied to Spain, and they controlled the best land in California, along with most of its labor. Mexican liberals believed in separating church power from the state. Ambitious soldiers and settlers in California wanted that land. And some officials genuinely believed the neophytes ought to be free citizens.
Narrator (Kore): The word is secularization, from the Latin saeculum, the world, as opposed to the cloister. A secular priest serves a parish under a bishop. A regular priest, like a Franciscan, lives under a religious order. To secularize a mission meant, on paper, to turn it into an ordinary parish and to release its lands, herds, and people.
Narrator (Kore): It came in steps. On July 25, 1826, California's first Mexican-born governor, José María de Echeandía, issued a proclamation of emancipation that let qualified neophytes in the southern districts leave mission rule and become Mexican citizens. Five years later, in 1831, about eighteen thousand six hundred eighty-three Native people were still under mission control, compared with four thousand three hundred forty-two soldiers, settlers, and what the count called other classes. Four to one.
Narrator (Kore): Then, on August 17, 1833, the Mexican Congress passed the decree for the secularization of the missions of California. And on August 9, 1834, Governor José Figueroa issued the rules for how it would actually work. The promise, in substance, was this. Each head of a family, and everyone over twenty, would receive a lot of mission land, not more than four hundred varas square and not less than a hundred. A vara is a Spanish yard, about thirty-three inches, so that plot came to somewhere between about seven and twenty-eight acres. Half the livestock, shared out. Half the tools, the seed, and the movable goods, or less.
Narrator (Kore): On paper, it is one of the most sweeping land redistributions in North American history.
Narrator (Kore): On paper.
Narrator (Kore): Here is a second concept, and it is the key to this whole episode. Trust versus title. Quote (Charon, flat documentary): the harpies of the civil power,

Narrator (Kore): The Rancho.
Narrator (Kore): 1839. Narrator (Kore): That piece of ground, today, is the northern part of the Stanford University campus.
Narrator (Kore): Here is how you got a rancho, because the process becomes a disaster in the next episode. You petitioned the governor. If you could not write, a neighbor wrote it. You attached a diseño, the drawing, a map with no surveyor behind it: a creek bend, a big oak, a hill. The governor asked the local officials and the mission whether the land was free and whether you owned cattle. If they said yes, he issued a título, a title. Then a local official walked the bounds with you and put you in juridical possession. The oak was the corner. The neighbors were the survey.
Narrator (Kore): What is missing is a precise line. A grant might say two leagues, more or less. Under Mexican law a governor could grant as little as a third of an acre or as much as eleven square leagues, about fifty thousand acres. Two leagues was most common. In a country with plenty of land and few people, more or less was fine. Your neighbors knew where the oak was.
Narrator (Kore): That is our concept. A property system built on local knowledge and custom, versus one built on survey and record. Mexican ranchos worked on memory, on neighbors, on the oak tree. Narrator (Kore): Fourteen hundred seventy-one acres is almost six square kilometers, nearly three times Monaco, about one and three-quarter times Central Park, about eight hundred and thirty full-size football pitches.
