Narrator (Kore): Call the pattern a cluster. Firms sit next to the laboratory that trained their people, and next to each other, because the next hire, the next part, and the next idea are a short drive, not a letter to Boston. Economists have a longer name for it, agglomeration, the simple fact that specialists get more done when they can see each other's doors. You only need the picture. A park is a neighborhood of specialists. The university is the landlord of the neighborhood and the school of its children. A landlord who also writes the syllabus is not a neutral host. He is choosing which problems count as an education, because those are the problems the tenant will pay to have taught.

Narrator (Kore): Look at the town he chose, because the legend will later pretend the valley was waiting for him. The supply chain will be the people in the building. He recruits them one by one, from laboratories that already work, and they come because the man who wrote the textbook asked. A cluster, before anyone is using the word, assembled by a son who wanted to live near his mother. The world's chip business sits on this peninsula in substantial part because of that want. Let the contingency sit before the legend of destiny arrives to tidy it.
Narrator (Kore): In November of that same year the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Shockley, John Bardeen, and Walter Brattain, for the transistor. Learn the object, because the rest of the hour is people fighting over who gets to make it. A transistor is a small solid switch. A vacuum tube, the thing in the radios and the radar sets of the last war, is glass, heat, and a filament that burns out. A transistor does the switching inside a crystal. Less heat. No glass to break. A size that can shrink again and again until a building's worth of calculating fits in a pocket. The Nobel was for the effect. The industry was for the shrinking.
Narrator (Kore): Shockley's book on electrons and holes was the field's textbook. That matters more than the medal for the young people who got on trains. You move to a town with no semiconductor industry because the man who wrote the book asked you, and because the book is what every serious laboratory is teaching. Bardeen and Brattain stayed identified with the first, point-contact device. Shockley's name sat on the junction transistor that could be manufactured. The prize made them a trio. The company made him a boss. Young physicists moved because he asked them to. They did not yet know they were moving into a management problem disguised as a laboratory.
Narrator (Kore): A field can be right and a boss can still empty the building. Shockley had the standing to recruit the best young people in the trade. He did not have the temperament to keep them. Jay Last, who was one of them, said later that Shockley had no business sense at all, which was not surprising, until the moment the work required it. Relationships with Beckman deteriorated. Then relationships inside the lab deteriorated. Individually, Last remembers, most of them decided they were going to leave.
Narrator (Kore): They did not leave because the science was boring. They left because they wanted to make a transistor, a device the market could use, and the man who had recruited them would not run a laboratory they could stand. The IQ test had missed him by six points. The management test, which nobody had written down, he failed in public.
Narrator (Kore): Remember who ranked him. Lewis Terman, the father, had come west because tuberculosis required a mild climate. His son Fred leased the farm. The boy the father scored at one hundred twenty-nine came home to his own mother and built the laboratory the son of the tester would have wanted in the neighborhood. Three Termans and one Shockley, and only one of them can keep a staff.
Narrator (Kore): By the summer of nineteen fifty-seven a group of the young men had decided the way out was not eight separate jobs. It was one exit, together.

Narrator (Kore): A research office in the park is a tenant of the university. A factory in Sunnyvale, and a test range in the Santa Cruz mountains, are not slices of the founding grant.
Narrator (Kore): What the valley felt was the payroll, and the payroll had a number. Accounts of the missiles division, Steve Blank's history of it among them, put Lockheed's local employment at zero in nineteen fifty-six and at more than twenty-eight thousand people by nineteen sixty-five. Twenty-eight thousand. Set that against Hewlett-Packard and Fairchild, which were famous and still small beside a missile factory. Set it against the other number Harris records. Thirty thousand California engineers were laid off in nineteen sixty-three and sixty-four when a defense cycle turned. One company's hiring, at its peak, was on the order of the statewide layoff. A cluster that lives on contracts can fill a county and then empty a profession in the same decade. People bought houses and drove to work on missiles. Some of those houses, within a few years, belonged to people who no longer had the job. The park is not a promise of permanent work. It is a promise that the work, while it lasts, will be near the campus.
Narrator (Kore): Learn the product the payroll was for, without pretending to a tour of the factory. A missile division is not a clever gadget in a garage. It is a prime contractor, a company the Navy or the Air Force pays to assemble a weapon out of everyone else's parts. Guidance, airframe, fuel, the warhead that belongs to someone else. The contractor's job is the system. Stanford's job, if Terman has done it right, is to be where the system's engineers were trained, and where a research office can sit on a ground lease while the factory itself stands on private land in Sunnyvale. The university gets the prestige and the rent. The county gets the traffic. The contract gets a return address that sounds like a campus.
Narrator (Kore): On January seventeenth, nineteen sixty-one, leaving office, Dwight Eisenhower warned the country about the arrangement in a phrase that stuck.

In grocery money, using that year's index, about one hundred sixty-seven billion to about seven hundred forty-seven billion. Harris writes that while bombers were leveling cities in North Korea, military support for electronics research at Stanford tripled. He had to build a park where the three could share a parking lot.

Narrator (Kore): San Francisquito Creek is a ditch with a county line in it. Palo Alto, in Santa Clara County, on the Stanford side. East Palo Alto, in San Mateo County, on the other. The name says east. The map says north. A later newspaper would have to remind readers that the poorer city is not east of the rich one. It is across the water, and across Highway 101.
Narrator (Kore): The name itself was a compromise, and it has a date. The city's own general plan records an informal election on December twelfth, nineteen twenty-five. The area had been called Ravenswood, and Runnymede. East Palo Alto won the vote. There was talk, even then, of annexing to Palo Alto. The enabling law never arrived. The place kept a name that sounds like a neighborhood of the rich city, and a government that was not that city's.
Narrator (Kore): Richard Rothstein, in The Color of Law, uses Palo Alto as a case, and Harris follows him onto the ground. After the war the federal government, through the Federal Housing Administration, became a superbank for private mortgages. The loans were designed to cost the Treasury nothing, which meant the government only backed borrowers and neighborhoods it called solid. The old Home Owners' Loan Corporation maps colored districts by investability. Red was the color for black neighborhoods. The practice took the name redlining. A red line on a map is a refusal of a loan, drawn in advance of the applicant.
Narrator (Kore): Restrictive covenants did the same work in the deed. A covenant is a promise written into the title. These promises said a buyer could sell later only to white buyers. In nineteen forty-eight the Supreme Court said courts would not enforce racial covenants. The FHA did not therefore start insuring black buyers. Harris is blunt. The agency did not lend to black buyers under any circumstances. It brought the red line along.
Narrator (Kore): Here is the scene where liberal intentions met the form. In the late nineteen forties the Peninsula Housing Association of Palo Alto, middle-class and working-class residents, bought an undeveloped ranch near Stanford and planned a cooperative subdivision. Hundreds of houses. Common spaces. Of the first one hundred fifty families, Harris writes, two percent were black. That was enough. The federal insurers would not back the construction loans. Banks, including Bank of America, the bank from the North Beach plank, did not make the loans the FHA refused. The association gave up and sold the land to a developer who accepted the condition. Whites only. The subdivision is called Ladera. It is still there. The co-op that tried to include black families is not.
Narrator (Kore): Two percent of one hundred fifty is three families. Three families, and a two hundred sixty acre ranch next to Stanford in nineteen forty-eight loses its insurance. That is the number. A policy can be triggered by a handful of people and still cover a landscape.
Narrator (Kore): Blockbusting was the other tool, and it used a sale rather than a refusal. Narrator (Kore): Eighty-two percent, against a starting point of nearly none, in six years. Not because a law ordered black families into one town. Narrator (Kore): The eighty-two percent is the number. The creek is the border you can walk.

Narrator (Kore): The Demo and the Contract.
Narrator (Kore): December ninth, nineteen sixty-eight. San Francisco. The Fall Joint Computer Conference, in Brooks Hall. Douglas Engelbart, of the Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park, sits at a console and shows a room what a computer can be if it is a tool for a mind instead of a batch of cards.
Narrator (Kore): The name Mother of All Demos comes later. On the day, the Smithsonian's account of it, he had asked for the slot in March and been given about ninety minutes in a hall that could hold two thousand. He said later he was nervous as hell. The stage lights were bright enough that he could not see the audience. His terminal was tied, over telephone lines, to a computer thirty miles away at SRI, and to colleagues in Menlo Park who joined the picture on the screen. A mouse under his hand. The prototype, the museum notes, he and Bill English had built at SRI in nineteen sixty-four. Text that could be edited, pointed at, rearranged, copied. He typed a word, then the same word again, and showed that a mistake could be backed up without a card punch. The Defense Department's research agency and the Air Force had paid for the work. A contract cover from the following spring lists an ARPA order and a sum of about one point five million dollars for the study of computer-augmented management. Narrator (Kore): The man on the stage had been a Navy radio man. He shipped for the Philippines on V-J Day and, on the way, read Vannevar Bush, the Atlantic piece reprinted in Life. Pretty much everyone, Harris notes, thought it was nuts. SRI hired him. A small Air Force grant let him write it down. The demo is that idea, in public, twenty-three years later.
SPOKEN (Rasalgethi: Douglas Engelbart, on a stage, December 1968, curious, not sales): "If in your office, you, as an intellectual worker, were supplied with a computer display backed up by a computer that was alive for you all day, and was instantly responsive to every action you have, how much value could you derive from that?"

Narrator (Kore): The day had a second audience, and it was not the Pentagon's. Stewart Brand, who had been around the acid tests, stood with the camera. Bill Paxton, who worked the demo, remembered him behind the lens, and remembered the feedback as psychedelic.

Narrator (Kore): April third, nineteen sixty-nine. Students meet and take the date as a name. The university's news service, writing the next day, records what they opposed. All classified research. Narrator (Kore): They are asking the trustees to govern an institute the trustees already control. SRI's history says the mood had shifted. Narrator (Kore): On April tenth the news service reports a second meeting. Students who packed Dinkelspiel Auditorium voted the trustees' responses unacceptable, and decided to sit in.
