## 01. Done

Narrator (Kore): The ceremony is two days late. Leland Stanford, governor of California turned railroad president, is the only one of his four Sacramento partners who came. Reporters have been telling the country that the blow will be heard farther than any sound a mortal man has made. The telegraph operator has warned every office on the line. Narrator (Kore): Stanford swings.
Narrator (Kore): [SILENCE] [HARD] He misses. The maul hits the rail.
Narrator (Kore): [HARD] It does not matter. The operator closes the circuit anyway. The wire goes out across the continent.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): done
Narrator (Kore): The officials will say that the two railroads together have laid one thousand seven hundred seventy-six miles of track. As in the year of independence. A nation that had proclaimed itself in 1776 was, ninety-three years later, announcing that it had nailed itself together, and it wanted the number to rhyme.
Narrator (Kore): Hold the miss. The ceremony is a machine for making a myth, and the myth is that a spike finished a country. The rail was already graded. The holes were pre-drilled. The man with the maul missed, and the telegraph told the truth the organizers wanted, which was not quite the truth of the morning. Chinese workers in clean blue frocks had carried one of the last rails into the gap. The photograph of the day will not quite show them.
Narrator (Kore): We will come back to that flat. First we go backward twenty-one years, to a place that is not yet a state, where the money is not yet in the railroad because there is no railroad. There is gold in a mill race, a treaty on a table, and a passage so hard that the passage itself is the business.
Narrator (Kore): This is Cardinal. Episode two: The Toll.

## 02. The Fastest State
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms4_2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: horses.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Fastest State.
Narrator (Kore): September 1, 1849. Monterey. Forty-eight delegates meet in Colton Hall, a two-story schoolhouse of local stone above the old Spanish presidio, to decide what California is. The United States has owned the country, on paper, since the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo the year before. It has not organized it. There is no territorial governor sent from Washington. There is a military man, a flood of gold miners, and a question.
Narrator (Kore): Here is the concept, because the speed is famous and the machinery is not. In the American system a territory is a waiting room. Congress governs it. The president appoints the governor. The people do not elect senators, and they do not vote for president. A state is the room after that. It writes a constitution, elects its own officers, and gets two seats in the Senate no matter how large or small it is. Most of the West sat in the waiting room for decades. California walked past it.
Narrator (Kore): On September 5 the delegates vote on whether to ask Congress for a territorial government or to write a state constitution and demand admission. The clerk's count is twenty-eight to eight, for a state. They sign the constitution on October 13. On November 13, 1849, the voters ratify it, twelve thousand sixty-one to eight hundred eleven. Millard Fillmore, who became president when Zachary Taylor died, signs the admission bill on September 9, 1850.
Narrator (Kore): From the treaty, February 2, 1848, to that signature is two years and seven months. Put the neighbors beside it. Oregon, acquired by treaty with Britain in 1846, waited about twelve years and eight months, and it had a territorial government from 1848. Nevada, admitted during the Civil War on October 31, 1864, waited about sixteen years and nine months. Utah waited almost forty-eight years, until January 4, 1896. New Mexico, January 6, 1912, and Arizona, February 14, 1912, waited about sixty-four years, and Arizona's southern strip was not even American until the Gadsden Purchase of 1854. Texas is the odd one. It came in as a republic, in 1845, and skipped the waiting room for a different reason. It had already been a country.
Narrator (Kore): Why California? Three forces, and they arrive together. Gold, which by the end of 1849 has dumped a population into a place that had almost no American government. The refusal, at Monterey, to wait. And the Compromise of 1850, the national bargain that let California in as a free state, with no slavery, and postponed the breakup of the Union for a decade. A free state on the Pacific was a prize both sides of that fight thought they understood. They did not yet understand what it would do to a railroad.
Narrator (Kore): Remember the free state. In a few years the question of where to lay the tracks will be the same question as where slavery is allowed to grow. California's speed is not only a gold-rush story. It is the reason a transcontinental railroad becomes possible the moment the slave states walk out of Congress.

## 03. Panama
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: borodin from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: surf.mp3, wind.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Panama.
Narrator (Kore): 1849. Collis Potter Huntington is twenty-eight, a hardware merchant from upstate New York, and he wants California. There is no railroad across the continent and no practical wagon road for a shopkeeper in a hurry. The sea route around Cape Horn is eighteen thousand miles. One army officer's ship, William Tecumseh Sherman's, takes two hundred two days. Stephen Ambrose, looking back at that isolation, writes that it must be doubted whether so desirable a place had ever been so cut off.
Narrator (Kore): Huntington takes the other way, the isthmus of Panama. His steerage ticket costs more than two hundred fifty dollars. On the price index we are using, the Minneapolis Fed's consumer prices, two hundred fifty dollars in 1849 is roughly ten thousand dollars today. The ship that should meet him on the Pacific side is not there. For two months he is stranded in the mud and the rain. He writes to his brother.
LETTER (Algenib: Collis Huntington in 1849, young, writing from Panama to his brother, grim and plain): Death is carrying off the Americans most fearfully. There are eight to ten deaths a day.
Narrator (Kore): Cholera, dysentery, malaria, yellow fever. Huntington does not die. He goes into business on the beach, selling medicines and food to men who cannot leave. He later said he made three thousand dollars at it. Roughly a hundred and twenty thousand dollars now, earned because other people were stuck. He remembered the crossing as twenty-four miles, and he said he walked it.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): It was only twenty-four miles. I walked it.
Narrator (Kore): He walked it at least twenty times, buying and selling, while the fever worked through the camps. Accounts differ on the exact miles of one tramp. They do not differ on the trade. The man who will later buy Congress, as his own letters will boast, learns his California fortune at a bottleneck. The gold is in the mountains. The price is on the path.
Narrator (Kore): That is the concept under this whole hour. When a place is hard to reach, the money is not in the thing at the end of the path. It is in the passage. Shovels, flour, mule teams, a toll wagon road, and at last a railroad that the government pays you to build, and that you then own, so that everyone behind you has to pay you to move. Huntington does not know the last step yet. He knows the first. Do not dig. Sell to the people who are digging, and charge them for the crossing.

## 04. The Store
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms3_3 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: horses.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Store.
Narrator (Kore): Sacramento, in the early eighteen-fifties, is a levee town that floods, burns, and rebuilds, selling tools to men on their way to the gravel. Four shopkeepers find each other. Huntington and Mark Hopkins, the quiet partner with the ledger, are neighbors at 52 K Street, in the hardware trade. Charles Crocker is a big man, around two hundred fifty pounds, dry goods and a freighting business, who will become the foreman. Leland Stanford, the lawyer who burned out of a practice in Wisconsin, sells groceries. Shovels and flour. The miner's lottery is the customer's problem. The margin on the shovel is the store's.
Narrator (Kore): June 1850, on the trail west, before Crocker is a railroad man. At the Green River crossing, another party moves its animals onto ground Crocker's men have picketed. Crocker drives the animals off.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): That ground is ours.
Narrator (Kore): They threaten to pull his stakes. He pulls a pistol.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): You do, if you dare.
Narrator (Kore): They back down. Ambrose records this as one of several times Crocker used the pistol, and he quotes Crocker's own rule. A man well assured of his own position, who shows a bold front, need not fear anybody. On that same journey Crocker found his horse-guards playing cards, called for the deck, burned it, and told them it would not do. They had to have their wits about them. The weapon the record gives him, on a riverbank in 1850, is the pistol. An ax handle will show up later, in another man's hand, used to discipline workers, not to hire them.
Narrator (Kore): The four merchants incorporate the Central Pacific Railroad on June 28, 1861. Their combined wealth, Ambrose says, is a hundred and fifty-nine thousand dollars. In 1861 prices that is roughly six million dollars now. Here is the comparison that matters more than the modern price. The federal subsidy they are about to chase will pay sixteen thousand dollars a mile on flat land. Ten flat miles is a hundred and sixty thousand dollars. Their entire pooled fortune is about what the government will loan them for ten miles of easy track. They are not rich enough to build a railroad. They are rich enough to hold a franchise while the United States builds it with them.
Narrator (Kore): Last episode I promised a second way to count a fortune, and I would not invent it. Here is the method, and here is why there is still no fake number beside it. A consumer-price conversion asks what the dollars would buy in a grocery basket. A share-of-the-economy conversion asks what fraction of the whole country's output those dollars were. For a fortune, or for a federal subsidy, the second number is usually much larger, because the United States of 1861 was a small economy next to this one. The research for this series left that second figure blank rather than guess it. So we will not guess it. When I say roughly six million, that is the basket. The slice of the country was bigger than the basket makes it sound. That is the lesson. One method on the air, and a warning about what the method leaves out.
Narrator (Kore): Theodore Judah is the engineer who brings them the map. He has been up the ridge between the American River and the Bear, through Dutch Flat toward Donner Pass, with a druggist named Daniel Strong. He tells the storekeepers, in his wife's later account, that they are tradesmen in Sacramento, that their property is there, and that if they will help him survey, he will make them the Central Pacific. With a congressional bill passed, he says, they will have the control of business that makes a fortune. If nothing more, they can own a wagon road from Dutch Flat over the mountains. They take both. Quietly they form the Dutch Flat and Donner Lake Wagon Road, a toll company on the line the railroad will follow. Crocker said later that none of them knew anything about railroad building. They were enterprising men, and they wanted the road to come to Sacramento.
Narrator (Kore): Before any of them, there is a story the Stanford family liked to tell, and it has to be marked as a story. According to a biographical sketch in the Senate's memorial addresses after Leland's death, a Mr. Whitney spent a night at Josiah Stanford's farm, Elm Grove, on the road between Albany and Schenectady, when Leland was thirteen, and talked about a railroad to the Pacific. The boy never forgot it. Leland was born on March 9, 1824, so thirteen is about 1837. The record problem is the name. There were two Asa Whitneys. One made car wheels and worked on the Mohawk and Hudson Railroad. The other was a China merchant who came home convinced that the United States should be the road to Asia, went to China only in 1842, and began his public campaign in 1844, years after a thirteen-year-old Leland would have sat at that table. The memorial says only Mr. Whitney. Chang's history of the Chinese railroad workers identifies the visitor as the China merchant. The dates argue against it. Treat Elm Grove as family legend, not as the night the Pacific railroad was handed to a boy.
Narrator (Kore): What is not legend is the merchant's own book. In 1849 Asa Whitney, the promoter, published A Project for a Railroad to the Pacific. He wrote that the change of the route for the commerce with Asia had, since before the time of Solomon, changed the destinies of empires, and that its march had always been westward.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): Through us must be the route to Asia, and the change to our continent will be the last, the final change.
Narrator (Kore): Senator Thomas Hart Benton, in the same season of prophecy, imagined Chinese workers building that West, and said they must talk together, and trade together, and marry together. Hold that sentence against the inaugural we are about to hear. The China dream and the China panic are the same decade. The storekeepers will get rich on both.

## 05. Three Signs
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: scottish3 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: wind.mp3, horses.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Three Signs.
Narrator (Kore): A sidebar, for anyone who walks these streets, and for anyone who has never seen them. Place names are arguments that won.
Narrator (Kore): Union Square, in San Francisco, is a shopping square now, a column in the middle, Geary and Powell at the northeast corner. California Historical Landmark 623 records where the name came from. It originated in 1860, from public meetings held on that ground in support of the Union, in the year before the Civil War broke. Not a labor union. The federal Union, the one California's gold was helping to pay for, the one a Pacific railroad was supposed to pin to the coast. If you have stood in that square waiting for a cable car, you have stood in a war meeting.
Narrator (Kore): Sanchez Street runs south through the Mission and Noe Valley. The old directory of street names, in the appendix to The Beginnings of San Francisco, says it was named for the family of José Antonio Sánchez, a soldier of Juan Bautista de Anza's company, the expedition of 1776. Last episode we stood on a ranch at the airport that also belonged to a Sánchez, the Buri Buri grant. Do not flatten those into one man because the spellings match. The street is the family. The ranch was a Sánchez grant. The records blur which man a sign intends. A local hears a surname and thinks he knows the story. The papers are less sure than the sign.
Narrator (Kore): Judah Street is the third. Same appendix. Named for Theodore D. Judah. The engineer who found the Donner route, sold it to four storekeepers, and then watched them use it in a way he came to hate. Most people who turn onto Judah are going to the beach, the N-Judah streetcar, the sunset. The name is a man who died at thirty-seven, on November 2, 1863, before the tracks met, having brought a franchise to a door and decided the men inside would beggar it. We will hear him say that. The street does not say it. Streets rarely do.
Narrator (Kore): Three signs, one war. A square for the Union. A street for a Spanish soldier's family, easy to confuse with a land grant. A street for the engineer the merchants outgrew. The map looks neutral. It is a minute of the meeting.

## 06. Vigilance
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: tragic from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: crowd.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Vigilance.
Narrator (Kore): May 14, 1856. The corner of Montgomery and Washington, San Francisco. James King of William, editor of the Evening Bulletin, is shot by James P. Casey, a city supervisor whose criminal record King has been printing. King dies on May 20. On May 22 the Committee of Vigilance hangs Casey, and with him a gambler named Charles Cora, from the windows of its headquarters at 41 Sacramento Street. The building has sandbags piled against the walls. People call it Fort Gunnybags.
Narrator (Kore): This is the second committee. The first, in 1851, was merchants and vigilantes hanging men in a gold-rush city that did not yet have a working court. The 1856 committee is larger, more military, and more sure that it is the government. Its members turn out under arms. A fort on a commercial street. California has been a state for less than six years. Executions after a newspaper editor dies. The concept has a plain name. Private governance. When the official state is new, slow, or captured, the people with warehouses decide they will be the police and the court. Sometimes they are punishing a murderer. Sometimes they are deciding who counts as the public.
Narrator (Kore): California has been a state for six years. It already has a habit of building institutions beside the law and then asking the law to bless them. The vigilance committee is one. The railroad company, chartered by the state and subsidized by Congress, will be the lasting one. The committee hangs two men and disbands. The company stays, and it charges rent on the only modern road out.

## 07. The Governor
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: aria from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: crowd.mp3, wind.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Governor.
Narrator (Kore): September 4, 1861. Leland Stanford is elected governor of California. The Democrats are split, and a Republican who was a grocer a few years ago wins with a plurality. He takes the oath on January 10, 1862, in the middle of the Great Flood. Sacramento is under water. The record of that day is that the new governor reached his inauguration by boat, through the streets. The capitol they are using is a temporary one. The water is the fact. The rowboat is how he arrived.
Narrator (Kore): He serves one term, to December 10, 1863. For that whole term he is also president of the Central Pacific, an office he will hold from 1861 to 1890. His house is the governor's office and the railroad's office. That is the conflict of interest, said without a metaphor. The man who signs state paper for the railroad is the railroad's president. His partners will call him lazy and vain. He will insist, for the rest of his life, on being called Governor. He is good at being the face.
Narrator (Kore): In the assembly chamber he reads his inaugural from a text, and he tells the state what kind of settler he wants. Gordon Chang records the words.
SPEECH (Iapetus: a young Leland Stanford, governor in 1862, reading an inaugural from a prepared text): The settlement among us of an inferior race is to be discouraged.
Narrator (Kore): He says that Asia, with her numberless millions, sends to our shores the dregs of her population. He calls the Chinese a degraded and distinct people, a deleterious influence upon what he calls the superior race. The chamber applauds. These remain, Chang notes, his best-known words about the Chinese. Ambrose, writing about the campaign rather than this speech, has the shorter snarl. The dregs of Asia. That degraded race. Do not mash the two into one sentence. The inaugural is the formal address. The campaign line is the stump. They agree with each other.
Narrator (Kore): The same year, in the governor's house, Jane Stanford is seriously ill with a lung infection. Western doctors do not help her. The family's cook, Moy Jin Kee, brings a doctor from Sacramento's Chinese quarter, Yee Fung Cheung, who treats her with herbs. She recovers. Jane becomes so attached to Jin Kee's teenage brother, Jin Mun, that she talks of adopting him. When the boy leaves their service she gives him a gold ring engraved with his name. He wears it for seventy years.
Narrator (Kore): Public speech, private kitchen. The governor who wants the Chinese discouraged owes his wife's recovery to a Chinese physician, and his company is about to decide it cannot lay the track without Chinese crews. California's gold and Nevada's silver are helping pay for the war just begun. There is loose talk that the state might follow the South out of the Union. The railroad directors, one of them the governor, will have none of secession. They want the coast held, and they want the subsidy that holding it will justify.
Narrator (Kore): Washington is about to give it to them, because the men who had blocked a northern railroad are no longer in the room.

## 08. The Porch
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: mozart40_2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: wind.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Porch.
Narrator (Kore): August 13, 1859. A hot day in Council Bluffs, Iowa, on the east bank of the Missouri, looking at Nebraska. Abraham Lincoln is in town. He is not yet the nominee. He is a full-time politician, a year past the debates with Stephen Douglas, trying to become the Republican candidate for president. That evening he speaks at Concert Hall. The crowd is large. The local paper, the Nonpareil, has told the town to go and hear old Abe.
Narrator (Kore): He did not take the easy way in. At St. Joseph, Missouri, he could have ridden the only railroad across the state and gone home to Illinois. Instead he boarded a stern-wheel steamboat and came nearly two hundred miles up the Missouri to this bluff. The morning before the speech, he walked to the top with the Puseys, the family he was staying with. Lewis and Clark had stood on that same bluff in 1804. Lincoln looked at the river and the opposite shore and told his friend Pusey that not one, but many railroads, would center here.
Narrator (Kore): The next day a group of citizens gathers on the big porch of the Pacific House, a hotel, to hear him answer questions. When he finishes, the crowd thins. W. H. M. Pusey recognizes a young man still standing there. Grenville Mellen Dodge is twenty-eight, a railroad engineer. Pusey points him out and says Dodge knows more about railroads than any two men in the country.
Narrator (Kore): Lincoln's head comes around. He studies Dodge for a moment.
SPEECH (Alnilam: Abraham Lincoln in August 1859, on a hotel porch, unhurried, curious): Let's go meet.
Narrator (Kore): He sits beside Dodge, crosses his long legs, swings a foot, puts his hand on the younger man's forearm, and goes straight at it.
SPEECH (Alnilam: Abraham Lincoln, 1859, intent, the campaign voice dropped): Dodge, what's the best route for a Pacific railroad to the West?
Narrator (Kore): Dodge answers at once. From this town, out the Platte Valley. Lincoln asks why. Dodge says the forty-second parallel is the most practical and economic route, which makes Council Bluffs the logical place to begin, because of the railroads already building west from Chicago to this point. Lincoln keeps asking. Years later Dodge will describe the conversation in a soldier's phrase.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): He shelled my woods completely and got all the information I'd collected.
Narrator (Kore): There is a money angle under the geography, and it is small, and it is real. Lincoln is about to accept seventeen lots in Council Bluffs as collateral on a loan of three thousand five hundred dollars to a fellow lawyer, Norman Judd. The lots are in the Riddle Tract, on the floodplain, the same tract where Dodge is buying. Three thousand five hundred dollars in 1859 is roughly a hundred and thirty thousand dollars now. The future president is also looking at his collateral. A photograph caption in Ambrose's book puts the two men as they were that year. Lincoln was a politician and a railroad lawyer running for president.
Narrator (Kore): The lawyer part is not a compliment added later. At twenty-three, in his first campaign, for the Illinois legislature, Lincoln told voters that no other improvement could equal the utility of the railroad. He finished eighth of thirteen. He lost, and he kept the subject. In a tax case for the Illinois Central he billed two thousand dollars. The railroad answered that this was as much as Daniel Webster himself would have charged. Lincoln re-billed five thousand, sued, and won. Ambrose calls him, on a scholar's verdict, one of the foremost railroad lawyers in the West. In 1857 he defended the first railroad bridge across the Mississippi, the Rock Island bridge, after a steamboat called the Effie Afton hit it and burned. Lincoln went to the river and measured the currents himself. He argued for travel from East to West, whose demands, he said, are not less important than those of the river. The jury hung. A hung jury was a win for the bridge. The boats did not get to own the crossing. Richard Rayner, in a later life of the associates, quotes him in that case on the manifest destiny of the people to move westward. The destiny was a legal argument about a bridge.
Narrator (Kore): The Iowa railroad men do not forget the porch. At the 1860 convention they help nominate him, and they get John Kasson to write the railroad plank of the Republican platform. It says that a railroad to the Pacific Ocean is imperatively demanded by the interests of the whole country, and that the federal government ought to render immediate and efficient aid in its construction. On the train east to his inauguration, Lincoln takes a turn at driving the locomotive. Dodge, in the audience for the address, writes to his wife that old Abe has delivered the greatest speech of the age. It is backbone all over. Then Dodge adds the practical line. It looks as though we can get all our measures through.
Narrator (Kore): The route he gave Lincoln on that porch was never only a survey. Ambrose's sentence is the one to keep. Lincoln's question was, next to slavery, the foremost question in his mind, and it was inevitably part of the question of slavery's future in the Republic. A northern railroad and a southern railroad were two different countries.

## 09. The Elephant
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms1_1 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: telegraph.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Elephant.
Narrator (Kore): Through the eighteen-fifties the line could not be chosen, because choosing it chose slavery's direction. Jefferson Davis, secretary of war, sent out the Pacific railroad surveys of 1853 and wanted the thirty-second parallel, from New Orleans to San Diego. No free-state politician was ready to charter or fund a road that would help slavery expand. Free-soil men wanted Chicago, or St. Louis, or Minneapolis. No slave-state politician was willing to give the road to them. The United States paid ten million dollars in the Gadsden Purchase for the strip that made Davis's southern route possible on American soil, and the Army experimented with camels for the desert. The camels are a detail. The blockade is the fact.
Narrator (Kore): Judah put it in a pamphlet in 1857, reaching for the Bible a year before Lincoln's House Divided speech made the verse famous. The Pacific railroad, he wrote, is a house divided against itself. It cannot be done until the route is defined, and if the route is defined, the opposing interest is powerful enough to defeat it. In January 1859 he wrote that President Buchanan favored the extreme southern route and would veto a bill for any other. In 1860 he reported that the debate on slavery, and on other matters, had left Congress no time to consider the Pacific railroad bill.
Narrator (Kore): Then the Deep South leaves the Union, in the spring of 1861, and its members leave Congress. The votes that had blocked every northern route are no longer in the chamber. Chang's history says it in one turn. A divided Congress could not agree. Secession let Washington authorize a northern route the South had opposed. The historian Robert Russell adds the cold institutional point. Because the Southerners were gone, Congress could pass a Pacific railway law several years earlier than it otherwise would have. Dodge's account of Lincoln's motive is military and political at once. The road had to be built not only as a military necessity, but as a means of holding the Pacific Coast to the Union. California's gold was paying for the war. The railroad was how you kept California.
Narrator (Kore): Judah goes back to Washington and, at his wife Anna's suggestion, turns a room in the Capitol into what he calls the Pacific Railroad Museum. Maps, surveys, and Anna's paintings of the Sierra on the walls. Members come to look. Many of them ask the obvious question. How do you get across those mountains? Aaron Sargent, a California congressman and Judah's ally, speaks for the bill on January 21, 1862. He calls it a military necessity. He says trade with Japan and China will jump, that California's loyalty will be assured, that no foreign army will dare attack the coast. Judah's complaint is that Sargent spoke before an empty hall. Judah himself is everywhere the bill is. He serves as secretary of the Senate's Pacific railroad committee and as clerk of the House subcommittee, with the privilege of the floor in both houses. The engineer is also the staff.
Narrator (Kore): Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania, who owns a foundry, writes in a rule that the iron be American-made. He says the Southerners will be back someday, with the same arrogant, insolent dictation which the North has cringed to for twenty years, forbidding any road that does not run along the southern border. Justin Morrill of Vermont grumbles that the nation can hardly afford both guns and railroads. The House passes the bill on May 6, 1862, seventy-nine to forty-nine. The Senate passes it on June 20, thirty-five to five. The House agrees to the Senate's changes.
Narrator (Kore): Lincoln signs it on July 1, 1862, the same day as Malvern Hill, the last battle of the Seven Days on the Virginia Peninsula. Ambrose's account gives McClellan's army about a thousand dead and three thousand wounded in that fight, and a country depressed by it. In Washington, a pen. In Virginia, a battle the Union wins and does not feel like a victory. The law that results is, in Ambrose's phrase, complicated to an almost incomprehensible degree. The outline is simple enough to teach.
Narrator (Kore): Two corporations build toward each other. The Union Pacific west from the Missouri. The Central Pacific east from Sacramento. Each gets a right of way two hundred feet on either side of the track. Each gets land, in a checkerboard. A section is one square mile, six hundred forty acres. The grant is five alternate sections per mile on each side of the line. Alternate means every other square. Ten sections, sixty-four hundred acres, for each mile of track. The squares the government keeps, in between, become valuable because the railroad is there. That argument, about who gets the rise in the land, will have a name later. Not this hour.
Narrator (Kore): And the government loans its credit, in bonds. Sixteen thousand dollars a mile on flat ground. Thirty-two thousand in the foothills. Forty-eight thousand in the mountains. A thirty-year loan at six percent. Say the next sentence slowly, because the politics depends on it. A loan, not a gift. The bonds are a debt the companies are supposed to repay. In practice the debt will outlive the builders and land on their heirs, and the land grant is the part that behaves like a gift. On the 1862 price index, those three rates are roughly five hundred thirty-six thousand, one million one hundred thousand, and one million six hundred thousand dollars a mile today. The Central Pacific has to build fifty miles within two years, and finish its line by July 1, 1876, a few days before the nation's hundredth birthday. It will beat that deadline by seven years. The catch is already in the rates. A mountain mile pays triple a flat mile. So it matters, enormously, where someone says the mountains begin.
Narrator (Kore): One warning on the dollars. From 1861 to 1864 the price index we use jumps, from twenty-seven to forty-seven, because the Union is printing paper money, the greenbacks, and prices in the East rise. California's economy still runs on gold coin. Sacramento's dollar and Washington's dollar are not the same dollar in those years. We will not invent the premium between them. Roughly, on the index, is the honest word.
Narrator (Kore): Judah wires his partners in Sacramento.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): [HARD] We have drawn the elephant. Now let us see if we can harness him.
Narrator (Kore): An elephant, in the American phrase of that century, is a prize you have won and may not be able to feed. They have the franchise. The Sierra is still there.
Narrator (Kore): The next day, July 2, 1862, Lincoln signs a second land law. Justin Morrill's act, the one he wanted even while he doubted the country could afford guns and railroads both. It grants each loyal state thirty thousand acres of federal land for every senator and representative it has, to endow at least one college of agriculture and the mechanic arts. The count follows the census of 1860. New York had thirty-one representatives and two senators, thirty-three seats. Thirty-three times thirty thousand is nine hundred ninety thousand acres. Stanford University's campus today is eight thousand three hundred fifty-three acres. New York's land-grant pile was on the order of a hundred and twenty of those campuses. Cornell University, chartered in 1865, is the school New York built with that idea. Stanford is not a land-grant college. It will be founded later, privately, by a railroad man, on a fortune this week's other law helps create. Same week. Same president. Public land turned into a college by a statute, and public land turned into a railroad subsidy. Do not mix them up. The confusion flatters the railroad.
Narrator (Kore): In the spring of 1863 the war and the railroad sit in the same room again. General Grant orders Grenville Dodge to Washington. Dodge is alarmed. He thinks he is about to be called to account. Lincoln wants to talk about the Missouri River, about towns fifty miles up and down the bank that all want to be the place the Union Pacific starts, even as Robert E. Lee is preparing to march into Pennsylvania. Lincoln remembers the porch in 1859. Dodge's verdict is short. He found Mr. Lincoln well posted.
Narrator (Kore): A year later the companies come back for more. Lincoln signs the amendment on July 2, 1864. The land grant doubles, to ten alternate sections on each side, about twelve thousand eight hundred acres a mile. Set that against the campus again. One mile of finished track can now earn land equal to about one and a half Stanford campuses. The companies may issue their own first-mortgage bonds, equal to the government bonds, which pushes the United States into second place in line if the railroad fails. The government will advance bonds after twenty miles of track instead of forty. Representative Elihu Washburne described the galleries, when it passed, as a tempest of the wildest disorder, lobbyists male and female. The Union Pacific's man, Thomas Durant, had a lobbyist, Joseph P. Stewart, spreading two hundred fifty thousand dollars in Union Pacific bonds, twenty thousand of it to Charles T. Sherman, the general's brother. The gauge of the track, the width between the rails, is a fight Ambrose's own pages date two different ways. We will leave the inches out rather than pick a page.

## 10. Moving the Mountains

Narrator (Kore): Moving the Mountains.
Narrator (Kore): The triple rate for mountain miles is an invitation, and Charles Crocker accepts it. He drives the state geologist, Josiah Whitney, out to Arcade Creek, about seven miles east of Sacramento. This is not Asa Whitney the promoter, and not the car-wheel Whitney. This Whitney maps mountains for a living. The ground at the creek is flat. What Crocker shows him is a fan of reddish earth washed down from the hills. Whitney agrees that the Sierra Nevada, for the purposes of the subsidy, begins here. On September 29, 1862, Governor Stanford telegraphs Washington about evidence to fix the western base of the Sierra Nevada. Aaron Sargent, no longer in Congress but still in the capital, carries the geologist's report to Lincoln. Lincoln says it seems about right.
Narrator (Kore): The mountains move about fifteen miles west, on paper. Foothill miles pay thirty-two thousand dollars. Mountain miles pay forty-eight thousand. The difference is sixteen thousand a mile. Sixteen thousand times fifteen miles is two hundred forty thousand dollars in extra bonds. In 1863 prices, roughly six and a half million dollars now. A Chinese worker on this road will be paid thirty-five dollars a month. Two hundred forty thousand dollars is about five hundred seventy years of that wage. The United States has been talked into paying mountain rates for a fan of red dirt on a valley floor.
Narrator (Kore): Sargent's line, as Ambrose records it, is a joke at Lincoln's expense and at his own.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): [SILENCE] Here you see, my pertinacity and Abraham's faith moved mountains.
Narrator (Kore): Another report, which Ambrose prints and does not adopt, puts a version in Lincoln's mouth. Here is a case in which Abraham's faith has moved mountains. ## 11. The Patent and the Furnace
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: schubert2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: church.mp3, telegraph.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Patent and the Furnace.
Narrator (Kore): March 18, 1865. The war is not over. Lee has not surrendered. In Washington, Abraham Lincoln signs patents that convey Mission San Juan Capistrano and Mission San Luis Rey to Bishop Joseph Sadoc Alemany of California. The patents confirm a decree the Land Commission issued in 1855. Alemany had filed the church's claim in 1853. A later Supreme Court case, Foster against Mora, measures the San Juan Capistrano parcel, the buildings and the gardens, at forty-six and seventy-four hundredths of an acre.
Narrator (Kore): Here is the misconception, and it is common enough to correct out loud. Lincoln did not return all the missions. He signed these two, on this date. Other mission parcels move on other days. San Diego's patent is May 23, 1862. San Gabriel's is November 19, 1859. La Purísima waits until 1874. A press release that says one document gave them all back is wrong. What Lincoln did was narrower and more interesting.
Narrator (Kore): Last episode, a Mexican governor sold the buildings at San Juan Capistrano for seven hundred ten dollars. The American patent is how the church gets its parcel back, through the same federal title machine that is confirming rancho grants, and that, in this same decade, is giving railroads millions of acres. One office, three kinds of land. A mission church. A Mexican ranch. A checkerboard along a track. If you can get your paper in front of the United States and survive the lawyers, the United States will draw the line. The railroad is simply the applicant that arrives with a statute written for it.
Narrator (Kore): And the men who hold that statute decide they should also hold the contract to build. The Big Four award the construction to Charles Crocker and Company, a firm they own. Ambrose calls it an almost identical device to the Crédit Mobilier. In the East, Thomas Durant and a promoter named George Francis Train buy a sleepy Pennsylvania corporation, the Pennsylvania Fiscal Agency, and rename it the Crédit Mobilier of America. The Union Pacific pays that company, which the Union Pacific's own directors own, to build the Union Pacific. The profit is in the building, whether or not a ticket is ever sold. Among the insiders is Congressman Oakes Ames of Massachusetts. His family made the shovels that Huntington, Hopkins, and Crocker had sold to miners. The store and the subsidy have the same supplier. One eastern beat, and then we leave it. The investigation that will make Crédit Mobilier a scandal is still years away.
Narrator (Kore): So is the fire. When a congressional committee later demands the Central Pacific's construction books, Mark Hopkins carries fifteen leather-bound volumes down to the basement and feeds them into the furnace. Huntington tells the committee his partner is a peculiar man. He considered the paper no longer worth saving. That is not a scene from 1869. It is where this arrangement ends. The books that would show what the elephant cost are burned by the man who kept them. For now the tracks are still climbing, and the men on them are about to stop.

## 12. The Summit
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms4_2 from 300s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: wind.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Summit.
Narrator (Kore): The winter of 1866 into 1867. Snow so deep, and so bright, that men go snow-blind. E. B. Crocker, Charles's brother and the company's lawyer, writes to Huntington that they have had to buy up all the cheap goggles in the market. Think of that, he says. What an item in railroad building. On Christmas Day, 1866, the Dutch Flat Enquirer reports that a gang of Chinese workers was covered by a snow slide and that four or five died before they could be dug out. Near the summit tunnel, Ambrose writes, an avalanche carried away some twenty Chinese, whose bodies were found after the spring thaw. How many died on the line, the company did not publish. The historian Thomas Chinn's judgment, which Ambrose quotes, is that the loss of life was heavy. In June 1870 a train will pass through Sacramento carrying, by one newspaper report, the bones of perhaps twelve hundred Chinese workers, boxed to be sent home. One report. Not a census. It is the number we have.
Narrator (Kore): James Harvey Strobridge is the construction boss. Irish, over six feet, thirty-seven when he takes the work. At Bloomer Cut a black-powder blast costs him his right eye. The Chinese who learn English call him the one-eyed bossy man. Foremen clap a hand over an eye to make a crew think he is coming. He says that men generally earn their money when they work for him. When there is trouble among the Chinese crews, Ambrose writes, Strobridge can pick out the leaders with a glance. He confronts them, usually with an ax handle, and they give way. The ax handle is discipline. It is not a recruiting story, and it is not Crocker's. Crocker, for his part, later claimed he quarreled with Strobridge about the way he spoke to the men. They are human creatures, Crocker said. Strobridge answered that you have got to do it, and that they are about as near brutes as they can get. Crocker said he found out that it was true. Put that next to the pistol at Green River. The bold front, turned on his own workforce.
Narrator (Kore): Strobridge had not wanted them hired. His line, in Ambrose, is plain. I will not boss Chinese. He said they could not do the work. Then he did boss them, because the white crews were leaving for the mines and the mountain was not going to wait. The story goes that Crocker answered him with the Great Wall of China. Treat that as legend. The payroll is the record, and it complicates the usual telling. Ambrose has the decision in 1865. Payrolls that later historians have read show Chinese crews, under contractors including Hung Wah and Ah Toy, already on the Central Pacific's books in January and February of 1864. Hung Wah is a labor contractor. At the end, Strobridge will seat his Chinese foreman, most likely Hung Wah, at the head of a dinner table, and a paper will run the scene under the heading Chinese Laborers at Table. The honor is private. The pay packets are the public fact. Strobridge's wife, Hanna Maria, lives in a passenger car fitted out as a house at the end of track, with an awning and a caged canary by the door. The men call her the heroine of the Central Pacific. Ambrose calls her the only white woman who saw the work through from beginning to end. The canary is real. It does not soften the ax handle.
Narrator (Kore): By Chang's account the company needs a workforce it can barely imagine, and it prints thousands of copies of a woodcut flyer to circulate in California and in China. No copy survives. Crocker admits to Huntington that no one on the company side has translated the flyer back into English. The men who believe they are buying labor cannot read their own advertisement. Chinese workers become about ninety percent of the Central Pacific's labor. Chang calls them, for those years, by far the largest single workforce in American industry to that date.
Narrator (Kore): June 24, 1867. A Monday, near the solstice, the middle of the building season. Three thousand Chinese workers put down their tools at once, from Cisco to Truckee, nearly thirty miles of mountain. The demand, as the company understands it, is parity with white workers. Forty dollars a month instead of thirty-five, and a day of ten hours instead of eleven. Thirty-five dollars in 1867 is roughly eight hundred forty dollars a month now, about ten thousand dollars a year, roughly two-thirds of a full year at the federal minimum wage of our own time. The strike is not a demand to get rich. It is a demand to be paid like the other men on the same rock. A newspaper called it the greatest strike the country had known. The masthead is not in hand, so that sentence stays a paraphrase and not a performed clipping.
Narrator (Kore): Mark Hopkins writes to Huntington with the fear of a bookkeeper. If they succeed in this demand, then they control. E. B. Crocker writes the most honest line. The truth is they are getting smart. His remedy is to inundate California and Nevada with laborers. Freedmen, Chinese, Japanese, all kinds. Charles Crocker goes up the mountain. The strike leaders tell him, in words he later reports as theirs, eight hours a day is good for a white man, all the same good for a Chinaman. In that telling they ask for eight hours, not the ten the company wire had named. The number shifts with the teller. The pay does not. He answers in a pidgin of his own making. Hear it as his rendering, not as their language.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): [HARD] John Chinaman no make laws for me. I make laws for Chinaman. You sell for thirty-five dollars a month, me buy. You sell for forty dollars and eight hours a day, me no buy.
Narrator (Kore): Then the food stops. Crocker will claim he cut it off. The labor agents stop supplying provisions. E. B. Crocker writes that none of them went near the strikers for a week. They did not want to exhibit anxiety. After eight days most of the men go back to work. Histories that follow Charles Crocker's version call it a defeat. Chang reads the partners' own letters and sees men who were shaken, and he notes that pay for skilled Chinese workers rises over the following months. What the principals want next is not a negotiation. It is a flood.
LETTER (Orus: E. B. Crocker, matter-of-fact, in a business letter): [HARD] We want a hundred thousand chinamen here, so as to bring the price of labor down.
Narrator (Kore): Huntington goes further. It would be all the better for us and the state, he writes, if half a million came over in 1868. The governor who had called them the dregs now loves the supply. The economist's word for a market with one big buyer is monopsony. The Central Pacific is that buyer on the mountain. A strike is the workers' attempt to be a seller with a price. Cutting off the food is a lockout. Wanting a hundred thousand more men is the plan to make the price fall. On August 3, 1867, Chinese crews hole through the summit tunnel and let the air in.
