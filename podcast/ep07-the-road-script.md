Narrator (Kore): by Kyle McAuliffe.
Narrator (Kore): Nineteen seventy-two. Menlo Park, California. A suburban road runs west from the freeway, past the edge of a university, and up toward the coastal hills. The buildings are low. They sit back among oak trees, with more parking than a visitor can explain. There is nothing on the road that looks like power. That is the point of a gate that wants to be mistaken for geography.
Narrator (Kore): At number three thousand, a partnership is opening its doors. The partners are Eugene Kleiner and Tom Perkins. Kleiner had a private office in the building before the firm even had a name. The historian Leslie Berlin, who wrote the history of these years, says the partnership began in nineteen seventy-two with an eight-million-dollar fund, one of the largest in a business that was still small enough to fit in a handful of rooms.
Narrator (Kore): Hold the number. Eight million dollars in nineteen seventy-two. Using the Minneapolis Fed's consumer price index, the same method this series uses throughout, that is about sixty-four million dollars in twenty twenty-six grocery money. A person working full time at the United States federal minimum wage, seven dollars and twenty-five cents an hour, unchanged since two thousand nine, earns about fifteen thousand dollars a year. The fund is more than four thousand of those years. And half of it came from one man, Henry Hillman, a Pittsburgh industrialist. The other half came from individuals, trust funds, and institutions, among them an insurance company and Rockefeller University.
Narrator (Kore): Here is the concept, and it is the whole hour. A gatekeeper is not the person who does the work. A gatekeeper is the person who decides who is allowed to pass. A century earlier, the railroad through this same valley decided which towns could ship their wheat. The charge was called a freight rate, and the men who set it said it was whatever the traffic would bear. Sand Hill Road is about to do a quieter version of the same job. The thing being moved is no longer wheat. It is a company that does not exist yet.
Narrator (Kore): Kleiner was not a banker. He was one of the eight young researchers who had walked out of William Shockley's laboratory in nineteen fifty-seven and started Fairchild Semiconductor. As a boy in Vienna he had watched Nazis force his father to open the family safe. A police officer who knew the family pulled the father out of a line bound for the camps. Kleiner never finished high school. He volunteered for the American army, he said, because he had a personal thing to settle, and the G.I. Bill put him through engineering school. Perkins was different. He had made Hewlett-Packard's computer division the biggest in that company, and he had the polish of a man who had studied how a businessman was supposed to look. At Harvard Business School he had sat in the class of Georges Doriot, the French professor who taught a generation of Americans that venture investing was a craft.
Narrator (Kore): The same year, nineteen seventy-two, Don Valentine founded a firm called Sequoia Capital. Valentine had run marketing at Fairchild, and then at National Semiconductor. Berlin notes that Sequoia was moving onto Sand Hill Road while other people's money was still learning the address. The operators had become the financiers. They already knew the people worth funding, because they had been those people's bosses.
Narrator (Kore): Then Perkins did something that complicates the postcard. Shortly before a young associate named Bob Swanson joined the firm, Kleiner and Perkins left three thousand Sand Hill Road. They moved to the twenty-ninth floor of Two Embarcadero Center, in San Francisco, looking out at the bay. Perkins did not want the neighbors to see whom he took to lunch.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): We didn't want to be considered part of the flock. Eagles don't flock, was our joke.
Narrator (Kore): The joke lost. The other firms stayed on the road. Within a decade the low buildings among the oaks were the largest concentration of venture capitalists in the valley, and, Berlin writes, perhaps in the world. You can move your own office to a skyscraper. You cannot move the gate, once everyone else has agreed that the gate is a road.
Narrator (Kore): One more fact, because the money did not come only from rich families. In nineteen seventy-four Congress passed a pension law with a stern name, the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, and a sterner idea inside it: a prudent person does not gamble with workers' retirement. Fund managers read that as a ban on anything speculative. Venture capital is speculative by design. Most of the companies fail. In nineteen seventy-eight Congress cut the tax on capital gains and eased those rules, and the next year the Labor Department clarified the point that mattered. Prudence would be judged on the whole portfolio, not on each wild bet inside it. A pension fund could put a small slice into companies that might go to zero.
Narrator (Kore): Berlin's number: within a year, venture capitalists had more than half a billion new dollars to invest. In twenty twenty-six grocery money, that is about two point three billion dollars. The retirement savings of teachers, truck drivers, and clerks had become the raw material of a road in Menlo Park. The beneficiaries were not asked. That is the public-money leg of a private gate, and it is the part the origin stories usually leave on the cutting-room floor.


## 02. The Ticket Booth
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms3_3 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: hum.mp3] [ERA: hum]

Narrator (Kore): The Ticket Booth.
Narrator (Kore): January eleventh, nineteen seventy-one. A weekly trade paper called Electronic News lands on desks from Boston to Sunnyvale. The headline, over a series by a columnist named Don C. Hoefler, reads "Silicon Valley U.S.A." It is the first time those two words appear in print as the name of a place. Hoefler was writing about semiconductors, the chips cut from silicon, the second most common element in the earth's crust. He was not writing about social networks or search engines. Those did not exist. He was naming a factory district. The name outlived the factories.
Narrator (Kore): By the time Hoefler published the name, Stanford had already made a small office permanent. The story of the office begins with a man named Niels Reimers. In the late nineteen sixties he ran a pilot: take what a professor invents, file a patent, and license the patent to a company, in exchange for a royalty. By the summer of nineteen seventy-four, Berlin writes, the pilot was a four-year-old official operation called the Office of Technology Licensing. Reimers and an assistant worked out of a trailer behind Encina Hall. On the wall, in capital letters, a sign said MAKE IT HAPPEN. On Friday afternoons they filed the week's paper over popcorn and a beer called Henry Weinhard's. They called the ritual Management by Beer.
Narrator (Kore): Teach the words, because the rest of the hour is built out of them. A patent is a temporary monopoly. The government gives you the exclusive right to an invention for a term of years, and in exchange you publish how it works, so that when the term ends anyone can use it. A license is the permission slip. The university keeps the patent and rents the right to use it. A royalty is the rent, usually a percentage of sales. Equity is different. Equity is a slice of the company itself. Reimers would spend the nineteen eighties arguing that Stanford should take stock, not only cash, because a fast company might design the licensed invention out of its product before a royalty check ever arrived. He estimated that by refusing equity the office had left more than fifty million dollars on the table. The university let him start taking stock in nineteen eighty-nine.
Narrator (Kore): The scale, when it arrived, was not modest. Berlin, writing about the office as of twenty sixteen, says cumulative income from technology licensing was nearly two billion dollars. An Office of Technology Licensing overview a year later puts the cumulative figure at about one point nine billion, through fiscal year twenty seventeen. Three inventions, out of roughly twelve thousand the office had evaluated, accounted for the fortune: recombinant DNA, a set of antibody patents, and Google. The rest was a long tail. That is the shape of this business. You run a ticket booth for decades. A few tickets pay for the building.
Narrator (Kore): Compare it with the year the booth first beat everyone else. In nineteen eighty-three Stanford earned more from technology licensing than any other university in the country: two point five million dollars, about eight million in twenty twenty-six grocery money. Almost sixty percent of that year's income came from a single patent, the one Reimers had filed on a way of moving genes from one organism into another. We are going to that patent now, because it is where the university claimed ownership of a piece of life, and where a man who had just been fired decided to start a company anyway.


## 03. Patenting Life
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: mozart40_2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: hum.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Patenting Life.
Narrator (Kore): Summer, nineteen seventy-four. Niels Reimers is at his desk in the trailer, going through the mail. Two scientists have done something that will split the century. Stanley Cohen, at Stanford, and Herbert Boyer, at the University of California, San Francisco, have found a way to cut a gene out of one organism and stitch it into the DNA of another. DNA is the molecule that carries heredity, the long instruction sheet inside a cell. Their method let a person rewrite a line of that sheet. Reimers decided the method should be patented, and he began the fight to file.
Narrator (Kore): The fight started immediately. Berlin writes that the application was criticized for its potential to undermine the university's basic mission, which was to increase the world's stock of knowledge, not to fence it. Faculty said the claim reached too far. The press ran nightmares. The mayor of Cambridge, Massachusetts, asked whether a strange orange-eyed creature, supposedly sighted nearby, could be connected to the experiments, and said he would be damned if his city was going to be diseased over academic freedom. In nineteen seventy-five scientists met at Asilomar, on the Monterey coast, and agreed to regulate themselves. Berlin's dry observation is that the gesture advertised the fear instead of calming it. By the end of nineteen seventy-seven Reimers's clipping file was three inches thick. He kept going. His own line, later, was that he kept thinking about the potential for good: drugs, hormones, vaccines that did not depend on a weakened virus.
Narrator (Kore): Now the fired kid. Robert Swanson was twenty-eight. He had talked his way through an MIT chemistry degree and a management degree in five years, opened a Citicorp venture office in San Francisco, and been hired by Eugene Kleiner, who saw a version of his own nerve. Swanson lasted less than two years. The break came over a company called Cetus. At a long lunch, a Nobel laureate mentioned recombinant DNA as something Cetus might explore. Nobody else at the table wanted it, including Tom Perkins, who had come to the lunch to show how worried the firm was. Swanson still thought he had a job. Perkins told him the partners would like to have just the two of them working there. Swanson could stay on the payroll until the end of nineteen seventy-five.
SPOKEN (Puck: Bob Swanson, twenty-eight, just fired, quietly to himself): "Maybe I'm not very good."
Narrator (Kore): He had time, a Ping-Pong table that doubled as a desk, and a stack of cheap meals. He cold-called the scientists who had been at Asilomar. He had never heard of Cohen, Boyer, or Reimers. Boyer, unusually for a biologist in nineteen seventy-six, was willing to talk about a company. His cats were named Watson and Crick. Most of his colleagues would not have taken the call on a university phone. Arthur Levinson, who would later run Genentech and sit on the boards of Apple and Google, remembered that as a young biochemist he walked down the street to a pay phone if he needed to speak to a company. A venture capitalist named Brook Byers compared a scientist thinking about business to Bob Dylan going electric. People wondered what was up, and whether bad would come of it.
Narrator (Kore): Swanson asked Kleiner and Perkins to pay him a salary while he incubated the company, the way they had done for the founders of Tandem Computers. They said there was no room. The fund was barely making money. So Swanson and Boyer started it themselves. Swanson's first proposed name was Herbob, a mash of Herbert and Bob. Boyer countered with a word built from genetic engineering technology. Genentech.
Narrator (Kore): The record of what happened next is a set of dates and a stock table. Genentech was founded in nineteen seventy-six. On October fourteenth, nineteen eighty, it began trading on the NASDAQ exchange under the symbol GENE. The opening price was thirty-five dollars a share. In the first twenty minutes it hit eighty-nine, the fastest first-day gain Wall Street had seen. At the close the company was valued at five hundred thirty-two million dollars. In twenty twenty-six grocery money, that is about two point two billion. Swanson and Boyer had each put in five hundred dollars at the start. Berlin says each of those stakes was worth about sixty-five million dollars by the closing bell, about two hundred sixty million in today's grocery dollars. The slice Kleiner and Perkins had eventually bought, for a hundred thousand dollars and then another hundred thousand, was worth the same. The firm that had fired Swanson was now rich because of him.
Narrator (Kore): Sebastian Mallaby, in The Power Law, uses that fund as the picture of the math. As of nineteen eighty-four, the fourteen investments in Kleiner Perkins's first fund showed a combined profit of two hundred eight million dollars, about six hundred seventy million today. Ninety-five percent of the profit came from two companies, Tandem and Genentech. Without those two, the fund would still have beaten the stock market. With them, it became the story the industry tells itself.
Narrator (Kore): That story has a name. A power law is a distribution in which the winners do not win a little more than the losers. They win so much, and so increasingly, that the middle barely matters. Most venture bets go to zero. A handful return the fund. The rational response, if you believe the math, is to swing only at companies that could be enormous, and to ignore the company that might decently double. A decent double is what an ordinary investor calls a success. On Sand Hill Road it is a waste of a seat.
Narrator (Kore): And the patent? Stanford and the University of California licensed it widely, on purpose, because an exclusive deal would have been impossible. Cohen was consulting for a company that would compete with Genentech. Over the life of the patents, which expired in December nineteen ninety-seven, four hundred sixty-eight companies took a license. Maryann Feldman and her colleagues added up the money: about two hundred fifty-five million dollars in licensing revenue to Stanford and the University of California by the end of two thousand one. In twenty twenty-six grocery money, that is about four hundred eighty million dollars. Ninety percent of it was royalties on actual products. The other ten percent was the cover charge, the fees companies paid just to be allowed in the room. A license, for a young biotech firm, was also a permission slip. It said a famous university had looked at your science and not thrown you out.
Narrator (Kore): In two thousand nine the Swiss drug company Roche bought the rest of Genentech for forty-six point eight billion dollars. Every share that had cost thirty-five dollars at the opening bell in nineteen eighty was worth four thousand five hundred sixty dollars. Swanson did not live to see that sale. He stepped down as chief executive in nineteen ninety, after Roche bought a majority, and he died of brain cancer in nineteen ninety-nine, at fifty-two. Levinson's line is the one worth keeping. The man who spent his life turning this technology into drugs that saved other people did not get a drug that saved him.


## 04. Pong to Apple

Narrator (Kore): Pong to Apple.
Narrator (Kore): Nineteen eighty. A meeting at Atari, the video-game company that had taught a generation to lose quarters in a bar. Al Alcorn, the engineer whose work had helped launch the company in nineteen seventy-two, wants to build a new kind of cartridge system, one that would throw a hologram behind the game. The chief executive, Ray Kassar, tells him to talk to marketing. The marketing manager tells him to write a business plan.
SPOKEN (Zubenelgenubi: Al Alcorn, an Atari engineer, dry and annoyed): "We didn't need a business plan for Pong or the VCS."
Narrator (Kore): He needed one now. The line is Berlin's, from Alcorn's mouth, and it is a eulogy for a decade. Pong was the table-tennis game that made Atari. The VCS was the box that brought the games into the living room. Both had been built by people who shipped first and wrote the memo second. By nineteen eighty the memo had won. Kassar nodded at prototypes and released almost none of them. Alcorn's hologram machine, called Cosmos, drew thousands of orders at a trade show and was never sold. He had four words for what had happened to the company. All creativity had ceased.
Narrator (Kore): Across the valley, a different company was turning the memo into a religion. Apple had been started by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak, and saved as a business by Mike Markkula, a former Intel manager who wrote the checks, hired the grown-ups, and cared about how the thing looked. In December nineteen seventy-nine he wrote a one-page creed called "The Apple Marketing Philosophy." It had three words at the top: Empathy, Focus, Impute. Under the third, he wrote this.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): Our image is the combined result of everything the customer sees, hears, or feels from Apple.
Narrator (Kore): Read it again, slowly, because it is also a description of a university. The sandstone, the red tile, the church at the center of the Quad, the tour guide walking backward: those are an image, and the image is doing work. Markkula called the work "impute." If you present a product in a slipshod way, he wrote, people will perceive it as slipshod. If you present it in a creative, professional way, you impute the qualities you want them to feel. You do not argue them into it. You surround them.
Narrator (Kore): This is the cult energy of the Protestant fortune, and it is worth naming for a listener who has never stood on this peninsula. Catholic California put saints on the land. San Francisco, Santa Clara, the missions, the royal road. The fortunes that arrived after the gold rush were largely Protestant, and then, in the late twentieth century, frankly secular. They did not build missions. They built companies that asked for the feelings a mission used to ask for: belief, pilgrimage, a story about the future in which you are already late. Apple is the clearest example, and it did not happen by accident. Markkula wrote the instructions down.
Narrator (Kore): The money that made the instructions matter came, in part, from the East, and this is the one eastern beat of the hour. In the fall of nineteen seventy-seven Markkula and Jobs flew overnight to New York. They changed into new blue suits in a men's room at Thirty Rockefeller Plaza and rode the elevator to the fifty-sixth floor, to the offices of Venrock. Venrock is the venture firm of the Rockefeller family. The partner they had to convince was Peter Crisp. Jobs talked about a computer in every living room. The partners, by their own later account, barely heard the specifics. The whole territory was too speculative to take literally. They stepped into the hall.
SPOKEN (Algieba: Peter Crisp, a Rockefeller venture partner, remembering a shrug): "We went out in the hall, four or five of us, we looked at each other, and we shrugged our shoulders and said, What the hell?"
Narrator (Kore): They put in three hundred thousand dollars for ten percent of Apple. The deal valued the company at three million dollars. In twenty twenty-six grocery money, the check is about one point seven million dollars, and the company, on that day, is about sixteen million. Crisp later said people gave Venrock too much credit for being smart. Arthur Rock, the financier who had backed Intel, took a fifty-thousand-dollar slice of Venrock's own allocation, because Crisp owed him a favor from nineteen sixty-eight. Old eastern money, a shrug in a hallway, and a company that would spend the next forty years imputing a feeling to anyone who held its objects.
Narrator (Kore): The feeling is the product. That is Markkula's sentence, and it is why a machine for playing Pong and a machine for sending email can both be sold as a way of life. The university had been practicing the same sale since the day it opened, with a chapel in the middle and a railroad fortune underneath. Apple just had the honesty to put it in a memo.


## 05. Myth Machines

Narrator (Kore): Myth Machines.
Narrator (Kore): December twelfth, nineteen sixty-nine. San Francisco. A warehouse at eight twenty-seven Folsom Street, south of Market. Francis Ford Coppola and George Lucas open a film company and call it American Zoetrope. The word is Greek before it is English. Zoe, life. Tropos, a turn. A zoetrope is a nineteenth-century toy, a drum with slits, spun so that a strip of still drawings looks alive. They were naming a machine that turns still pictures into a world.
Narrator (Kore): They had driven up from Los Angeles with a crew of film-school friends, among them the editor and sound designer Walter Murch. None of them, Murch said, had family in the industry. They wanted American movies made under the influence of the films they loved from everywhere else. In nineteen seventy-two the company moved into the Sentinel Building, at nine sixteen Kearny Street, in North Beach, the old Italian neighborhood where A. P. Giannini had opened a bank in a remodeled saloon in nineteen oh four. Hold that address in your ear. A bank, a film company, and later a tunnel will all turn out to be stories about who gets to tell California what California is.
Narrator (Kore): Lucasfilm, the company Lucas built after he left the Folsom Street experiment, started a computer division in nineteen seventy-nine to drag high technology into movies. By the early nineteen eighties Lucas needed cash. He put the division up for sale. Steve Jobs, pushed out of Apple and looking for a next act, bought it. The popular sentence is "Jobs bought Pixar." The record is more precise, and Alvy Ray Smith, who was there, has kept the paperwork. On January thirtieth, nineteen eighty-six, Jobs wrote a certified check for five million dollars. Smith and Ed Catmull signed it over to Lucasfilm. Jobs then put another five million into the new company to capitalize it. Ten million dollars in all. About thirty million in twenty twenty-six grocery money, roughly two thousand years of a federal minimum-wage life.
Narrator (Kore): The first press release, dated February tenth, nineteen eighty-six, was coy. It said Jobs had paid an undisclosed sum in the millions. It also said the new firm would sell a graphics computer for about a hundred twenty-five thousand dollars, a machine faster at pictures than a six-million-dollar supercomputer. About forty employees came along, John Lasseter among them. They owned thirty percent. Jobs owned seventy, and he was chairman, not the founder in the way the brochures later implied. Catmull was president. Smith was vice president. Smith is blunt about the title: Jobs was the investor.
Narrator (Kore): The computer was not the fortune. The fortune was the toy, scaled up. In November nineteen ninety-five Pixar released Toy Story. A week later the company went public, in what Variety called the largest initial public offering of the year. In January two thousand six the Walt Disney Company bought Pixar. The announced price was about seven point four billion dollars, about twelve billion in twenty twenty-six grocery money. Smith's rueful arithmetic, looking back, was that Disney could have had the group for five million from Lucasfilm, and ended up paying billions. Four hundred times the grocery-dollar cost of Jobs's check, for a drum that makes drawings look alive. Hollywood had not moved to the Bay Area. The Bay Area had learned to manufacture myth in software, and then sold the factory back to Hollywood.
Narrator (Kore): There is a third Coppola, and he is the one with a theater named after him. August Coppola, Francis's brother and Nicolas Cage's father, was dean of the College of Creative Arts at San Francisco State from nineteen eighty-four to nineteen ninety-two. He persuaded the state legislature to put up fifteen million dollars for a Fine Arts Building, in the middle of a budget crunch. It opened in nineteen ninety-three: labs, a sound stage, and a hundred-and-fifty-seat theater with his name on it. In nineteen ninety-one, instead of a groundbreaking, he held what he called a skybreaking. Alumni tossed beans into a hole. He set a glass model of the building in the hole, poured wine over it, and said they would see how the dreams grew. Then he explained the ceremony.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): The idea is to look up, rather than down. Look up to the sky, the clouds, teach young people to dream.
Narrator (Kore): Same impulse as Markkula's memo and the zoetrope: the product is a direction for the eyes. Fifteen million public dollars is six times what Stanford earned from all its licenses in nineteen eighty-three. A state college's new arts building cost more to house than the country's most successful technology office was then earning from owning ideas. The myth machine was never only a startup. It was also a dean, a legislature, and a room with a hundred and fifty seats.

Narrator (Kore): The Story of Our Streets.
Narrator (Kore): Drive north out of the valley, across the city, toward the Golden Gate, and the road ducks under a hill in the Presidio. For years the official name of that tunnel was the Presidio Tunnel. Before that, locals called it the Funston Avenue tunnel, after the approach. On November fifth, nineteen eighty-six, the state gave it another name. Senate Concurrent Resolution eighty-six, introduced by San Francisco's state senator Milton Marks, added the words General Douglas MacArthur Tunnel. Caltrans, the state transportation department, still carries both names on its list of structures.
Narrator (Kore): Say the first name carefully. Douglas. Not Arthur. Arthur MacArthur was the father, a general who commanded the Department of the Pacific, and who, on the night in nineteen oh three when Theodore Roosevelt dedicated the column in Union Square, stood up and toasted the Army. That column is not his. It belongs to Admiral George Dewey and the battle of Manila Bay. The son is the one on the tunnel: Douglas MacArthur, who commanded in the Philippines, who left Corregidor in nineteen forty-two and said he would return, who ran the occupation of Japan, and whom President Truman fired in nineteen fifty-one for refusing to keep a war inside the lines a president had drawn. The Peninsula and the city have a habit of welding a famous last name to the wrong man. The tunnel is the son's. The monument in the square is an admiral's. The father is a toast at a dinner, and almost nobody orders the right one.
Narrator (Kore): Sand Hill Road is the other sign, and it does not commemorate a general. It commemorates a function. The name is plain English. A hill, some sand, a road. What it means, if you can read a gate, is that a company which cannot get a meeting in one of those low buildings does not merely lack money. It lacks the introductions to customers, the executives the partners know by their first names, and the next round of capital, which tends to follow the first round's letterhead. Proximity is the product. Judgment about people travels badly. You have to be in the room, and the room is four minutes from the campus that supplies the people being judged.
Narrator (Kore): And one departure, so the map stays honest. In nineteen ninety-eight NationsBank, of Charlotte, North Carolina, bought the San Francisco company that still carried A. P. Giannini's name. The merged bank kept the name Bank of America and put the headquarters in Charlotte. The bank that had lent on a face and a signature from a North Beach wharf, after the nineteen oh six fire, is run from North Carolina. Giannini Hall is still at Berkeley. His bank is not in his city.
Narrator (Kore): The jobs stayed. The bedrooms did not keep up. A gate that lets companies through, and does not let roofs through, bids up the ground. Henry George, watching the railroad do this to wheat towns, had a name for who captured the gain: the person who held the land. Stanford, by its founding grant, could not sell the farm. It could lease it. The road at the edge of the farm became the permission slip, and the rent on a bedroom followed.


## 07. Bayh-Dole
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: aase from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: fs_42139.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Bayh-Dole.
Narrator (Kore): December second, nineteen eighty. The United States Patent and Trademark Office issues patent number four two three seven two two four, "Process for producing biologically functional molecular chimeras." The application that issued had been filed on January fourth, nineteen seventy-nine, after years of argument. A chimera, in the old stories, is an animal sewn together from different animals. In the patent, it is Cohen and Boyer's method. Stanford and the University of California own it. Ten days later, on December twelfth, President Jimmy Carter signs a law with a dull name, the Patent and Trademark Law Amendments Act. Everyone calls it Bayh-Dole, after the two senators, Birch Bayh of Indiana and Bob Dole of Kansas.
Narrator (Kore): Here is what it did, in plain words. If a university, or a small business, or a nonprofit made an invention using federal research money, the institution could elect to own the patent. Before this, the federal agency that had paid for the work generally kept the rights, and a great many inventions sat on a shelf because a company would not spend a decade turning an idea into a product it was not allowed to own. Bayh-Dole flipped the default. The university takes title. The university has to file for the patent, has to try to license it, has to share the royalties with the human inventor, and has to give the government a license to use the invention for government purposes. The taxpayer funded the science. The university owns the paper. A company rents the paper and builds the product.
Narrator (Kore): Stanford did not wait for the law. Reimers had been running the office for a decade, and he had already fought the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare for the right to hold title on particular inventions. What Bayh-Dole did was make the Stanford arrangement the national rule, for every campus that took federal money. A local habit became a statute. That is how a model spreads when it is too useful to leave as a local habit. You do not argue the other universities into it. You write it into the condition of their funding.
Narrator (Kore): The number to put next to the statute is the one from nineteen eighty-three. Two point five million dollars in licensing income, first in the country, sixty percent of it from the chimera patent. The law did not create that patent. The patent was already filed, and the company built on it had already gone public. The law nationalized the booth. Within a generation, an office like Reimers's was normal equipment at a research university, the way a library was. Berlin's count of the change is the right one. When Reimers started, there were a handful of such offices in the country. After the recombinant DNA patent and the statute that followed it, they were simply how a university did business.
Narrator (Kore): Hold the sequence, because it is the sequence. A trailer behind Encina Hall. A patent on a method for rewriting a cell. A fired associate and a scientist with two cats. Narrator (Kore): The middle of the nineteen nineties. A dormitory room at Stanford is an operations center. Narrator (Kore): The problem was mechanical before it was philosophical. The campus network into the Gates building could carry about ten megabits a second. SPOKEN (Rasalgethi: Elon Musk, in a 2003 talk at Stanford, matter of fact): "Money is low bandwidth. You don't need some sort of big infrastructure improvement to do things with it. It's really just an entry in a database."
Narrator (Kore): Low bandwidth means the message is small. A payment is not a barrel of wheat and it is not a reel of film. The custom house at Monterey, in the hide trade, had the same job. So did the railroad's rate desk. The difference is speed, and the difference is that the ledger can now sit in a company that did not exist when the card was printed.
Narrator (Kore): X.com collided with another Palo Alto company, Confinity, where Peter Thiel and a Ukrainian-born engineer named Max Levchin were trying to beam money between handheld computers. Thiel's circle, several of them alumni of the Stanford Review, resented the rebranding and the software rewrites. Fraud leaked through the system faster than they could patch it. None of that is the concept. The concept is a network effect, and it is simple enough to teach in a minute.
Narrator (Kore): A network effect is what happens when a service becomes more useful to each person because other people are already using it. A telephone is worthless alone. A telephone is valuable in a city where everyone has one. A payment system works the same way. If the person you are buying from does not accept it, the system is a hobby. If the marketplace where both of you already are accepts it, the system is a road. PayPal found that road on eBay, the auction site, where strangers were already trading with strangers and needed a way to pay a person they would never meet. By the summer of two thousand two, eBay's own press release said, about sixty percent of PayPal's business was happening on eBay. eBay had forty-six million users. PayPal had the ledger those users were choosing, even though eBay was pushing a payment service of its own, called Billpoint.
Narrator (Kore): The story about Musk and Stanford belongs here, and it has to be labeled. The story goes that he enrolled in a Stanford doctorate program in physics and materials science, and dropped out after two days because the internet was more interesting. Ashlee Vance records it that way, with the phrase "as the story goes." Vance also records what happened when a lawyer later asked the university for the file. The graduate admissions office wrote back that, based on the information given, it was unable to locate a record for Elon Musk. Professors who were teaching physics there in nineteen ninety-five did not remember him. Some of his early enemies think the admission itself was a story told to decorate a young entrepreneur. The record, on the enrollment, is thinner than the legend. What the record does show is the work: Zip2, then X.com, then the card in the machine, then the companies after.
Narrator (Kore): July eighth, two thousand two. eBay announces it will buy PayPal. The document is a merger agreement dated the day before. Each share of PayPal becomes zero point three nine shares of eBay. On eBay's stock price of July fifth, the release says, the deal is worth one point five billion dollars. About two point eight billion in twenty twenty-six grocery money. Something like a hundred eighty-five thousand years of the federal minimum wage, for a ledger. Narrator (Kore): It closed on October third, two thousand two. PayPal's stockholders approved it. The same day, Peter Thiel resigned as chief executive. An eBay executive took the payments job.
