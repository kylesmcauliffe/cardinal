Narrator (Kore): March thirteenth, 1884. Leland Stanford Junior was born on May fourteenth, 1868, in Sacramento. He is two months short of sixteen. Narrator (Kore): His bedroom in the house on Nob Hill sat across the hall from his mother's. Jane turned the tutor's room next door into a study where she worked with Bertha Berner. She kept the bedroom as it was when he died. She described what she did there.

## 04. The Founding Grant

Narrator (Kore): The Founding Grant.
Narrator (Kore): On March ninth, 1885, the legislature passed an enabling act so a private deed of trust could found a university. The title begins, an act to advance learning, the arts and sciences, and to promote the public welfare. It was written as a general law. It had one institution in mind.
Narrator (Kore): On November eleventh, 1885, Leland Stanford and Jane Lathrop Stanford signed the grant. By then he was a United States senator. Senators were still chosen by legislatures, and he had taken the seat that March. The deed says they desire to found, on their estate known as the Palo Alto Farm, in San Mateo and Santa Clara counties, a university for both sexes. Colleges, schools, museums, galleries, everything necessary to a university of high degree. They convey the land to a board of trustees and to their successors forever.
Narrator (Kore): Read two clauses, because they are the machine.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): "the principal thereof shall forever remain intact, and that the rents, issues, and profits thereof shall be devoted to the foundation and maintenance of the University hereby founded and endowed"
Narrator (Kore): And, inside the rights the founders kept for themselves while they lived:
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): "but this reservation does not include the right or power to sell or encumber any of the real property granted."
Narrator (Kore): Here is the concept. A trust is property held by people who do not own it for themselves. The trustees spend the income on the purpose in the deed. The principal stays. Lawyers call a clause that keeps ruling after the giver is dead a dead hand. The same lock can freeze a mistake. Jane will later try to cap the women at a university the deed had opened to both sexes. The clause that stopped a sale is the clause that stopped her from turning the school into a boys' college.
Narrator (Kore): One name on the trustee list is a callback. Stephen J. Field, a justice of the Supreme Court of the United States. The last episode sat in his courtroom, where a railroad fence case grew a caption that treated corporations as persons. The same man is now on the board of the railroad president's university. The deed does not blush.
Narrator (Kore): The 1885 grant does not forbid tuition. It lets the trustees decide. The promise that California students would not pay comes later, in a constitutional amendment, with an escape we will reach. What the founders told Jordan was a fortune. The ultimate endowment, they said, would be about thirty million dollars. Jordan had fought to get Indiana's budget to fifty thousand. He was offered thirty times that, someday.
Narrator (Kore): Someday was not a deposit. Before Leland died he gave three ranches, eighty-five thousand acres. Vina never turned a profit, and the vines died of phylloxera. Gridley's grain was in decline. The Palo Alto stables usually lost seven thousand dollars a year, most of Jordan's ten-thousand-dollar salary. George Crothers said the ranches brought nothing but taxes. White says taxes did exceed the revenue, and the papers prohibited a sale. The cornerstone went down on May fourteenth, 1887, the day the boy would have turned nineteen. The doors opened on October first, 1891.

## 05. A Supermission

Narrator (Kore): A Supermission.
Narrator (Kore): Frederick Law Olmsted, the landscape architect who had made Central Park, was hired to site the campus. He wanted the buildings in the foothills. The Stanfords wanted flat land. They were building a monument you could march through. Olmsted suggested, and Charles A. Coolidge designed, the low buildings of the inner quadrangle, Encina Hall for the men, and a long drive from the railroad station planted in Mexican fan palms and Canary date palms. Palm Drive. The successor firm of the architect H. H. Richardson, Shepley, Rutan and Coolidge, carried the buildings. Flat land. A quad. An arch. A church at the center.
Narrator (Kore): Gray Brechin, in his history of imperial San Francisco, describes the result. A symmetrical composition of sandstone, continuous arcades, a Romanesque chapel loosely modeled on Trinity Church in Boston, the whole roofed in red tile, because the Stanfords wanted the buildings to resemble California's missions. Brechin's word for the place is a supermission. At the entrance stood a triumphal arch. The frieze was called The Progress of Civilization. The donors put themselves in it, on horseback, ahead of a locomotive entering the Sierra. Jane suggested the outline of that story and gave herself and her husband featured roles. Augustus Saint-Gaudens, the most famous sculptor in the country, turned her suggestions into the program. White adds a measurement. The arch was San Jose sandstone, more than a hundred feet tall, second in size only to the Arc de Triomphe, a Babylonian eruption, he writes, in the middle of the mission-style quiet.
Narrator (Kore): Leland wrote to Jordan a sentence Brechin quotes with brackets, meaning the biographer is supplying a word so the grammar holds. Our graduates are to be missionaries to spread correct ideas of civilization. Hold that next to the sentence from Florence. The children of California shall be my children. And hold both next to the missions the quad was dressed up to recall.
Narrator (Kore): The missions sat on crown land the friars held, in the official story, for the people they baptized. Secularization in the eighteen thirties scattered that claim. The Stanford grant says the real property may not be sold or encumbered. Adobe and red tile became sandstone and red tile. Under the missions, Native labor. Under the quad, a railroad fortune graded in large part by Chinese workers paid less than the white crews, which the Central Pacific's own managers admitted under oath.
Narrator (Kore): A mission was a discipline. The university, on the face of the grant, was an invitation. Both sexes. Come if you can pass the exam. Hoover, in a few minutes, is the price of that invitation: two suits and a bicycle.
Narrator (Kore): Today most of the twenty-one missions are Catholic parishes. La Purísima and Sonoma are state historic parks. The farm is still a private university on land the grant would not let anyone sell. The families who passed through the baptismal books at Mission Dolores and Mission Santa Clara are the Muwekma Ohlone. The campus sits in their watershed.

Narrator (Kore): Two Widows.
Narrator (Kore): A few miles from the farm, in the same Santa Clara Valley, another widow was building. The tourist story has the two of them backward.
Narrator (Kore): Sarah Lockwood Pardee Winchester signed her name Sallie. She was heir to a rifle. Her husband, William Wirt Winchester, was the son of Oliver Winchester, the New Haven man who turned a shirt business into the Winchester repeating rifle. William died of tuberculosis in New Haven on March seventh, 1881. In 1886 Sarah bought an eight-room farmhouse near San Jose and named it Llanada Villa. Mary Jo Ignoffo, the historian who went through the house's papers, is the source for that purchase. The Stanfords laid their cornerstone on May fourteenth, 1887, the birthday the boy did not reach. Two grieving households, both rich, started monuments about fifteen miles apart, inside roughly a year of each other. The timing is a fact. A shared motive is not. There is no evidence Jane Stanford and Sarah Winchester ever met, or wrote to each other.
Narrator (Kore): The house on Winchester Boulevard, 525 South Winchester, is famous for a ghost. The story goes that a Boston medium told Sarah the rifles had stacked up so many dead that the spirits would haunt her unless the hammers never stopped. Stairs to nowhere. A window in a floor. The record does not show that medium. The name later books give him, Adam Coons, has no trace in her lifetime. It first appears in 1967, in Susy Smith's Prominent American Ghosts. Her companion, Henrietta Servers, said she was not superstitious. She had been raised Baptist, and after 1908 she sat in St. Paul's, an Episcopal church in Burlingame. Call the medium a legend. The broken stairs have a plainer cause. The 1906 earthquake damaged the house, and she largely stopped building. A carpenter added the number thirteens after she was dead. The first printed mention of them is 1929.
Narrator (Kore): The séances the documents describe are Jane's. White puts them in the Nob Hill mansion, with the Newmans, friends of the Grants, trying to reach the boy. Mrs. Newman was the chief medium. The boy did not appear. A different child did, a spirit the Newmans called Ensign, and the medium reported that heaven rejoiced in the decision to dedicate the fortune to God and the church. That is a séance claim. It is not a deed. The wills were already signed in Paris. Berner later wrote that after Australia, where the dead did not answer, Jane never again attended a séance. The documented spiritualist in this valley is the railroad widow.
Narrator (Kore): What Jane said about Yale is an insult, and it is on the page. While they were bringing the boy's body home, the Stanfords looked at Cornell, Harvard, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and Yale. She said graduates of Harvard and Yale were of benefit to no one, including themselves. Her husband, she said, employed
DRAMA (Sulafat: Jane Stanford, dry, dismissive, remembering the men on her husband's payroll): "six of them as car-conductors on the Market Street Line now."
Narrator (Kore): There is no evidence that ties Leland or Jane Stanford to Skull and Bones. In 1871 Oliver Winchester gave Yale the land for an observatory. The building went up in 1882.
Narrator (Kore): The person who connects the two widows is not a medium. He is a lawyer in San Jose, Samuel Franklin Leib. He was a Stanford trustee. In 1899, afraid a constitutional campaign might fail and pull her gift down with it, Jane deeded the entire university endowment, twenty-five million dollars, with no reservations and no trust clauses, to Leib alone. ## 09. A Legacy of Debt
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: eroica2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: telegraph.mp3] [MOTIF: railroad stinger]

Narrator (Kore): A Legacy of Debt.
Narrator (Kore): June twenty-first, 1893. Leland Stanford dies. The Panic of 1893 is already on, the start of the deepest depression the country had been through. The Southern Pacific, the basis of the fortune, is missing dividends and cannot pay full interest on its bonds. Within two years of the opening, White writes, the new college is in danger of collapsing. The will leaves the university two and a half million dollars in cash. It leaves about seven million, largely, to his relatives, not to Jane's. The bulk, whatever it is actually worth, goes to Jane, and the university lives on her largesse.
Narrator (Kore): Two and a half million, in 1893 grocery dollars, is roughly ninety-three million now. Seven million is roughly two hundred sixty million. The relatives' cash is nearly three times the cash left to the school. The ranches could not be sold.
Narrator (Kore): Then the federal government sued. In 1894 it went after the estate for fifteen million dollars and froze the assets, including the bequest to the university. The money was the old government loans to the Central Pacific, the construction debt from the last episodes, come due. Here is the mechanism, because it is the whole Gilded Age in one lawsuit. The Central Pacific was a California corporation, and California law made stockholders personally liable for debts the company did not pay. Stanford and his partners had drained money out of the railroad into their own pockets, sold the hollowed-out Central Pacific to British investors, and had the Southern Pacific, which they still controlled, lease it back. The British were afraid of that stockholder-liability law, so the partners kept the stock in their own names. In 1894 the government came to the names.
Narrator (Kore): Fifteen million dollars. A congressman still earned five thousand a year, so the claim is three thousand years of a member's pay. In 1894 grocery dollars it is roughly five hundred eighty million now. At the federal minimum wage, seven dollars and twenty-five cents an hour since 2009, that is about thirty-eight thousand years of full-time work.
DRAMA (Sulafat: Jane Stanford, to a visitor, the boast gone out of her voice): "People think that Governor Stanford left me a very rich woman. I thought so myself, but it now seems that I was left a legacy of debt, trouble and worry."
Narrator (Kore): She kept the doors open on the household allowance the court allowed her, on an insurance policy, and on the sale of belongings. She cut salaries ten percent. ## 11. The Dowager

Narrator (Kore): The Dowager.
Narrator (Kore): Richard White, telling the Ross firing, gives Jane Stanford a title. She played the dowager queen. It was her university. The dismissal was her due.
Narrator (Kore): There is another dowager in this hour. Western reports said the Empress Dowager of China, Cixi, had the soul of a tiger in the body of a woman. Pronounce her tsz-shee. She had held the real power of the Qing throne since 1861. In 1898 she put the reforming emperor under house arrest and closed the door on foreign capital. In the summer of 1900 she backed the Boxers, the martial, magical movement that meant to drive the foreigners out.
Narrator (Kore): June 1900. Tianjin. Herbert Hoover is twenty-five, the orphan who came to Encina with two suits and a bicycle, now a mining engineer in the foreign settlement. Kenneth Whyte, his biographer, sets the month down as hot wind and banks of black and purple cloud. On June fourteenth, Hoover helps build barricades at the gates out of sacks of sugar and grain. A Boxer crowd burns the French cathedral in the Chinese city next door. Imperial troops sent to scatter them join them, and turn their artillery on the compound. Hoover fights fires and carries food on his bicycle, along the brick walls, out of the gunfire. His wife, Lou, will not wait in the basement. She runs her own errands with a .38 Mauser on her hip, stands a night watch, and works in a makeshift hospital.
Narrator (Kore): Rail and telegraph are cut. At Stanford, faculty and students read that some of their best alumni are probably dead.
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): "It is almost certain that they have fallen victims to the savage ferocity of the Mongolian hosts, unless they managed to make their escape previous to the bloody massacre, which is not likely."
Narrator (Kore): They had not. Whyte's account says the casualties inside the foreign compound were light.
Narrator (Kore): The coal was the point. Hoover considered the Kaiping mines, northeast of Tianjin, the only successful mining venture in China by Western standards. Nine thousand men worked three collieries. His employer, Chang Yen-mao, directed them. Chinese law kept the mines with the emperor, and Chang could not sell them to foreigners. During the siege the Boxers stopped the coal, the share price fell, and Russian and Japanese troops were said to be planting flags on the pits. The foreigners in the settlement arrested Chang. They thought he was negotiating his way out. Whyte notes there was no firm evidence. They locked him up anyway. From that confinement he signed a power of attorney and told Gustav Detring to put the company under the British flag on the best terms he could get.
Narrator (Kore): Hoover and Detring wrote the deal and backdated the papers, so the reorganization would look as if it had been agreed before the uprising. A million shares, one pound each. Existing Chinese shareholders would receive three hundred seventy-five thousand of them. What the London promoters would take for themselves was not written down. Hoover would not let the deed go straight to his boss, Charles Algernon Moreing. He had it made out to himself, as trustee. He had already told Moreing that without absolute control the game was not worth the candle.
Narrator (Kore): Hold the fraction. Three hundred seventy-five thousand shares out of a million stay with the Chinese owners on the page of that deal. The rest is the blank the engineers left for London. A rescue, in the wording. A takeover, in the handwriting.

## 14. The Expanding Peoples
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: brahms1_1 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: crowd.mp3, telegraph.mp3] [MOTIF: railroad stinger]

Narrator (Kore): The Expanding Peoples.
Narrator (Kore): February twenty-fifth, 1898. Washington. The Secretary of the Navy, John D. Long, has gone home for the afternoon. Theodore Roosevelt, the assistant secretary, is acting secretary for those hours. He cables Commodore George Dewey, in Hong Kong. The cable is marked secret and confidential. Keep full of coal. In the event of declaration of war Spain, Dewey's duty will be to see that the Spanish squadron does not leave the Asiatic coast, and then offensive operations in the Philippine Islands. The next day Long writes that the very devil seemed to possess Roosevelt yesterday afternoon. Long leaves the order standing.
Narrator (Kore): Roosevelt did not start this war, and he did not blow up a ship. The battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor on February fifteenth, killing two hundred sixty-six men. Navy inquiries in 1898 and 1911 blamed a mine. A 1976 study led by Admiral Hyman Rickover concluded the likeliest cause was a coal-bunker fire next to a magazine. It is still debated. President William McKinley asked Congress for the war. Congress declared it, in April. The Navy's own historians say the story that Roosevelt engineered the war by himself has largely been refuted. A war plan from 1896 already pointed at Manila. Gray Brechin puts it plainly. The Navy Department had planned the seizure of the Philippines two years before Gridley was ready to fire. What Roosevelt did, on one afternoon, was aim the fleet and refuse to whisper.
Narrator (Kore): May first, 1898. Dewey's squadron slides past the old fort at Corregidor into Manila Bay. He tells his captain, you may fire when you are ready, Gridley. Six hours of shelling sinks the Spanish fleet.
Narrator (Kore): The peace is a purchase. The Treaty of Paris, signed December tenth, 1898. Spain cedes Puerto Rico and Guam, gives up Cuba, and the United States pays twenty million dollars for the Philippines. In 1898 grocery dollars that is roughly eight hundred million now. The Senate ratifies the treaty on February sixth, 1899, fifty-seven to twenty-seven, barely the two-thirds a treaty needs, and two days after the shooting has started in Manila. The money went to Spain. It did not go to the Filipinos.
Narrator (Kore): Hawaii comes in the same summer, by joint resolution, the Newlands Resolution, on July seventh, 1898, while the war is still on. Francis G. Newlands, whose name is on it, was an heir by marriage to the Sharon silver fortune, and he laid out Burlingame and Hillsborough, the suburbs just up the peninsula from the farm. In October his brother-in-law, Hall McAllister Jr., writes that keeping the entire Philippine group would prove of incalculable benefit to San Francisco. A colony, argued from a peninsula law office.
Narrator (Kore): The war after the treaty begins February fourth, 1899. Roosevelt, president by then, will declare it over on July fourth, 1902. Fighting continues after the speech. The State Department's figures: more than forty-two hundred American combatants dead, more than twenty thousand Filipino combatants, and as many as two hundred thousand Filipino civilians dead of violence, famine, and disease. At the peak, seventy-five thousand American troops are in the islands. Americans called it an insurrection. The word keeps the other side from having a war.
Narrator (Kore): Both sides commit atrocities, and the government's own historians say so. American forces at times burned villages, reconcentrated civilians, and used torture. Filipino fighters tortured captured soldiers and terrorized civilians who cooperated with the Americans. One American method has a name, the water cure. On November twenty-seventh, 1900, Major Edwin F. Glenn used it on Tobeniano Ealdama, the town president of Igbaras, on Panay. Glenn was court-martialed, convicted, and given a one-month suspension and a fifty-dollar fine. General William Shafter told a Chicago paper it might be necessary to kill half the Filipinos so the remaining half could be advanced to a higher plane of life than their present semi-barbarous state. On September twenty-eighth, 1901, at Balangiga on Samar, townspeople attacked a company of the Ninth Infantry. Dozens of the soldiers died. The published counts of the American dead do not agree. About twenty-eight villagers were killed in the attack. Afterward, Brigadier General Jacob Smith told a major he wanted no prisoners. Kill and burn. The more you kill and burn, the more you please me. Asked the age limit, he said everything over ten, and he wanted Samar turned into a howling wilderness where even the birds could not live. Major Littleton Waller partly countermanded him. We are not making war on women and children. Smith was court-martialed for conduct to the prejudice of good order, sentenced to be admonished, and Roosevelt retired him.
Narrator (Kore): David Starr Jordan campaigned in public against annexing the Philippines. His reason was not a sermon on consent. Harris records the argument. The colonies were incapable of civilized self-government. They were not habitable by Anglo-Saxon people. The home cannot endure in the climate of the tropics. Anti-imperial, and racist. Both are in the record. Mark Twain, a vice president of the Anti-Imperialist League from 1901, asked whether we would go on conferring our civilization upon the peoples that sit in darkness.
Narrator (Kore): May thirteenth, 1903. Union Square, San Francisco. Roosevelt dedicates the Dewey Monument, a Roman column of the kind the old empire built for victories at sea. The ships' names are cut in the base. Olympia, Dewey's flagship, is first. He tells the crowd that the proper place of Americans is with the great expanding peoples, with the people that dare to be great. The civilian dead are already in the ground.
