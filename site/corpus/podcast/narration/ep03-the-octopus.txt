The Octopus.

This is Cardinal. Episode three.

May tenth, eighteen eighty. Hanford, in the San Joaquin Valley of California. The valley is the long flat trough that runs down the middle of the state, between the coast mountains and the Sierra Nevada. In spring the wheat stands high enough to hide a man on a horse.

United States Marshal Poole went to Hanford that day to put purchasers of railroad land in possession. He was resisted. A county history of Tulare reprints the Visalia Times of May fifteenth.

"As has long been anticipated, the conflict between settlers in the Mussel Slough country and purchasers of railroad lands has been inaugurated."

The paper, as that history quotes it, says Poole went with Clark, Crow, and Hartt. They first visited the ranch of W. B. Broden. He was not home. His household goods were set in the street. Then they met a number of settlers. The two sides, the paper says, do not agree on who fired first.

The dead, in the paper's list: settlers James Harris, Iver Knutson, J. W. Henderson, Archibald McGregor, printed there as McGregory, and Dan Kelly. Walter J. Crow and M. D. Hartt, the purchasers. E. Haymaker, a settler, slightly wounded in the head. The history's own summary counts eight dead. The paper it prints names seven, and one man wounded.

Families in Hanford had come for a picnic. Their own resolution, reprinted in the same history, says the news reached them in the middle of it. A later account in that book says that about four hours afterward, settlers killed Crow. The gunfight at the O.K. Corral, a year and a half after this, killed three. The movies kept Tombstone. The wheat field is the one that tells you how California actually worked.

Here is the fight under the fight. This was not empty land, and it was not a picnic that went wrong for no reason. In 1877 the Southern Pacific finally received its federal patents to land in this part of the valley, along a branch line toward the Mussel Slough of the Kings River. Settlers were already on it. Many of them had brought water to ground the county histories call barren, and had made a crop. Railroad agents then announced they would sell at prices from seventeen dollars an acre to forty.

Hold the price up to the company's own advertisement. The company itself had advertised the land at two dollars and fifty cents an acre and, in the company's own weasel word, upwards. Seventeen dollars is almost seven times that advertised floor. Forty dollars is sixteen times it. The difference is not the dirt. The difference is who gets paid for the work. The Pacific Railway Act of 1862, the law that gave the railroad its land in the first place, had named a different ceiling: grant land still unsold three years after the road was finished was to be open to settlers at not more than one dollar and twenty-five cents an acre, paid to the company.

A San Francisco newspaperman had just written the most famous economics book of the century to explain the rise in the land. Henry George published Progress and Poverty in 1879. His argument was that a railroad, a ditch, a town, all of that improvement, raises the value of the land around it, and the rise flows to whoever holds the title, not to the people who did the digging. He did not need a wheat field to draw the picture. He already had the railroad.

A railroad company approaches a small town as a highwayman approaches his victim.

George wrote.

The threat, "If you do not accede to our terms we will leave your town two or three miles to one side!" is as efficacious as the "Stand and deliver," when backed by a cocked pistol.

Build through the town, or build past it and let it die. After the road is built, charge what the town cannot refuse. The settlers at Mussel Slough had improved the ground. The railroad held the patent. The patent won. In the end the courts sided with the company. Some families left. Some paid the higher price. The Southern Pacific kept the land.

The story goes that this was honest farmers against a monster. The record is meaner. In eighteen seventy-eight, the same county history says, a band of masked men burned a homestead near Hanford. The next year a military company was organized in town. The railroad wrote the price. The men in the field pulled the triggers.

As has long been anticipated, the conflict. Violence had become weather. A toll road had finished its tracks and started acting like a government. It set prices. It bought newspapers and lawmakers. When the public got angry, it found a scapegoat who could not vote. And when a court finally wrote the story down, a caption did more work than the ruling. Come up the hill and meet the men who send the bill.

Nob Hill.

Jump back four years, to 1876, and up a steep street in San Francisco. The hill did not yet have its fame. It had a view, wind, and sand. The men who had just finished a railroad decided the view was the point.

Leland Stanford built first, at California and Powell. The house was finished in 1876. Richard Rayner describes a mansion of more than fifty rooms, on about two acres, inside a wall thirty feet high. The front doors were mahogany and rosewood, seven inches thick. A glass dome over the hall sat seventy feet up. The carved framework of the conservatory cost thirty thousand dollars. A custom library ladder, three rungs, cost a hundred and fifty. Rayner puts the whole production at two million dollars in the money of the eighteen seventies.

Two million dollars. In grocery dollars, at 1876 prices, using the Minneapolis Fed's price index and its estimate for 2026, two million then is roughly sixty-three million now.

In 1899 an economist named Thorstein Veblen gave this a name: conspicuous consumption, spending whose job is to be seen. The mansions came first. The theory arrived to describe them. Stanford's house was not a machine for living. It was a machine for being looked at, from the bay and from the offices of men who paid the railroad's rates.

Getting up the hill was its own advertisement. Cable cars already climbed Clay Street. That line, Andrew Hallidie's, had started in 1873, and it stopped two blocks short of Stanford's door. So Stanford and his railroad friends built another one, up California Street itself, past the new palaces. It opened on April tenth, 1878. It is still running. If you have stood on a San Francisco cable car and felt the floor bite as the grip takes the cable, you are on a vehicle a railroad president built so the public could reach his front steps, and so he would not have to walk the last two blocks.

Mark Hopkins let his wife talk him into a house a block up the hill, at California and Mason. He was the quiet partner, the ledger. Rayner says that even after he was a multimillionaire he kept a small cottage, kept no carriage, and took no vacations. The palace went up anyway. He never lived in it. We will come to the night he died.

Charles Crocker wanted a whole block. California, Sacramento, Taylor, and Jones. One lot on it belonged to a man named Nicholas Yung, an undertaker, comfortable but not rich, who liked the view and would not sell at Crocker's price. The offers climbed. Six thousand, then more, and at some point Yung is said to have named twelve thousand. Crocker stopped bargaining and built a fence.

The story goes that the fence was thirty feet high, and that Henry George called it a veritable coffin. The San Francisco Chronicle, looking back on November first, nineteen oh two, called it a spite fence. The paper said it went up in eighteen seventy-six, that it cost about three thousand dollars, and that after the Yungs left, the Crockers lowered it. What remained was about twenty-five feet. The New York Times, in nineteen oh four, called it twenty feet.

About three thousand dollars. Roughly a hundred thousand dollars in our grocery money. Crocker could lose that sum the way another man loses a cufflink. What he bought was a lesson. The price of refusing the railroad was darkness at noon.

The story goes that Yung answered by putting a coffin on his roof, skull and crossbones turned toward Crocker's windows. What a Chronicle report preserves is the threat: a coffin above the fence, as an advertisement for the undertaking business and a reminder of mortality. Threat or object, the picture stuck. The man who buried the dead, boxed in by the railroad.

Yung moved the house to another lot he owned, on Broderick Street. He would not sell the empty ground. He died in 1880. The fence outlived him. The heirs kept the feud for years. The wind worried the boards. Eventually the lot was sold, and the fence came down, and Crocker, by then dead himself, got the block in the only way that counts to an estate: on paper.

Stand on that pavement now and the houses are gone. The fire after the earthquake of 1906 took the hill in one night. What replaced them is the city's memory, pretending to be an address. The Stanford Court Hotel sits at 905 California, on the footprint of Stanford's mansion. The Mark Hopkins Hotel sits on Hopkins's corner, and the bar at the top looks down on the same bay the dome looked down on. Huntington Park is the lot of the house that had been David Colton's, where Collis Huntington later lived. His widow gave the ground to the city for a park in 1915. Grace Cathedral rises on ground the Crocker family turned over to the Episcopal church after the fire. The palaces lasted thirty years. The names are still on the hotel awnings.

The Story of Our Streets.

This series keeps a short ledger of names, because California wrote its history on signposts and then forgot the ink. Nob Hill is one of those names.

Nob is not a saint. The Spanish named towns for Francis, Clare, Joseph, and the rest, and those names are still the mission chain. Nob is short for nabob. And nabob is an English mangling of a word from India, nawab, a deputy of the Mughal emperor, a man who governed in someone else's name and got rich doing it. The British brought the insult home for merchants who made a fortune in the East and spent it loudly in London. San Francisco picked it up for a different east: Sacramento grocers and Nevada silver men who hauled their money up one steep ridge and built houses the rest of the city had to crane its neck to see.

So the hill is a joke that froze. Catholic California named places for heaven. Railroad California named a hill for showing off. You can still ride the California Street cable car, the line Stanford opened in 1878, step off at a hotel named for his bookkeeper, and look across a park named for the partner who stayed in New York. Then go west through Golden Gate Park to a museum that still carries the name de Young.

Michael Henry de Young, M. H. de Young, was the brother who lived. We are about to meet the brother who did not. In 1894 Michael organized a fair in Golden Gate Park, the California Midwinter International Exposition, a world's fair staged in a California winter to tell the rest of the country the sun still worked. The art building was left standing when the fair came down. The museum that grew out of it carries his name. The de Young. If you have been in that building, you were in a room descended from a newspaper family that spent the eighteen seventies as a political weapon. The marble does not mention the weapon. That is what a name is for.

Across the bay, the name is Hearst. Hearst Avenue runs along the north side of the University of California at Berkeley. The Hearst Memorial Mining Building is there because Phoebe Apperson Hearst paid for a great deal of that campus. She was George Hearst's widow, and from 1897 until she died in 1919 she was the first woman on the university's board of regents. George had bought the ranch at San Simeon, on the coast, in 1865. The castle tourists drive up to see is his son's later building, on the father's ground. Mine, newspaper, ranch, campus, castle. One family, five ways of making a name feel like geography.

Spanish California put saints on the land and made the church the landlord. Railroad California put nabobs on a hill and made a corporation the landlord. It built houses like altars and bought papers like pulpits. When a boy died, it would build a university that looked like a mission. That building is the next episode. The papers come first.

Friends.

The partners did not particularly like each other, and they liked Stanford least. Friendship, in the Gilded Age, was not a feeling. Richard White, in his history of the transcontinental railroads, treats it as an arrangement. Measures in California lived or died on who would stand with whom when the vote was called.

Some of the arrangements were with Congress, and we can hear them, because a man named David Colton saved his mail. Colton was the partners' man in San Francisco, a junior associate who wrote constantly to Collis Huntington in New York. Huntington wrote back in his own voice, dry, busy, sure of the price of things. Between the middle of the eighteen seventies and Colton's death in 1878, the letters piled up. In 1883, during a lawsuit by Colton's widow, they became public. They were reprinted in a pamphlet with a title that does not bother to whisper: How Members of Congress Are Bribed.

Read Huntington in his own words. November thirtieth, 1874, on the Congress about to sit.

January seventeenth, 1876. A rival, Tom Scott, was pushing a bill to help his own Pacific line, the Texas and Pacific. Huntington took the night train to Washington.

And the sentence he used whenever he wanted to sound clean. January twenty-ninth, 1876, and again that March.

The second time he finished the thought. He kept on high ground so that no investigation could hurt them.

Two hundred thousand dollars, in a letter dated January seventeenth, 1876. In grocery dollars that is roughly six million three hundred thousand now, as the cost of one bill.

Lobbying is arguing for a bill in public, with your name on the argument. Bribery is paying an official to use the office as a lever. Huntington's letters live in the gap. He does not write that he handed a senator an envelope. He writes that two senators were switched this week and can be switched back with the proper arrangements. The high ground is not innocence. It is a method. Pay in ways that look like friendship, so that if a committee asks, the paper smells like business.

White's point is that nineteenth-century Americans were not naive about this. They were shocked less by a bought newspaper or a greedy congressman than by what it added up to: a republic whose decisions were for sale. The word they used was corruption, and they meant it the old way, as rot in the structure, not as one man's bad manners.

This was not a California invention. While Huntington priced a bill, Tom Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad was asking Congress to back the Texas and Pacific, a southern route to the coast. The Colton letters are in large part Huntington fighting Scott. Farther north, Jay Gould was gathering lines. Charles Perkins of the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy said the Union Pacific's life depended on high rates more than any other company's. John Murray Forbes, whose Boston family had made its first fortune in the China trade, said Gould had as many aliases as a London professional thief. Stanford's face was Western. The technique was national.

Colton himself did not live to see the letters read aloud. On the evening of October eighth, 1878, a carriage stopped outside his house on Nob Hill. He was unconscious inside it. The company said a horse had thrown him. He died two days later. The rumor in the street was a stabbing. The truth of that night stayed dark. What the letters did, five years on, was turn the private voice of the railroad into a public document. Huntington's defense, for the rest of his life, was sarcasm and a bad memory.

The Bought Press.

Friendship with a newspaper worked the same way as friendship with a senator. You did not always buy the printing press. You bought the pipe the news came through.

In the eighteen seventies the Associated Press was that pipe. A paper that was not in the association did not get the national wire, which meant it did not get the country, not in time to print it. The wire was the pass, the same shape as a port or a mountain. Whoever stood at the gate decided which stories moved.

Huntington understood the gate. He wrote to Hopkins that if the Associated Press agent in Washington were seen to, a great deal of hostile copy could be kept back. His lobbyist, Richard Franchot, once got that Washington reporter onto the Central Pacific payroll. It worked until the Associated Press editor, James Simonton, noticed that the tone of the stories had changed, and suspected why.

In 1875 the partners tried something larger. They wanted an exclusive Associated Press membership for the San Francisco Chronicle, a paper they then had influence over, and they offered double the going rate. Double. The point of paying double was not generosity. It was to cut two other papers, the Bulletin and the Call, off the wire. Papers the railroad hated. The attempt failed. A news monopoly is worth more than a fair price, because a news monopoly decides what a fair price sounds like in print.

Jump to 1886, and a memo prepared for the president of the Union Pacific, which ran down the New York papers one at a time, the way a quartermaster runs down boots. One sheet existed to blackmail. One would print what it was handed. And the Star, the memo said, did as Huntington directs. White copies the verdict in a sentence that belongs at the center of this story.

Nineteenth-century Americans were not shocked by the corruption of the press.

It was how the trade worked. What frightened people was the use. A railroad that can raise a rate and then rent the description of the rate is not only a carrier. It is an editor.

There is a cartoon that puts the arrangement on one page. February nineteenth, eighteen eighty-one, in the San Francisco Wasp. George Frederick Keller's drawing is titled In the Hollow of His Hand. Two men stand on locomotives and hold Stanford up. Their names are not on the drawing. In Stanford's hands are two small figures. The copy of that issue identifies them as railroad commissioners Charles Beerstecher and Cone. The commission was new. Voters had adopted a fresh state constitution in 1879, over the railroad's opposition, and that constitution created a railroad commission, three elected men whose job was to watch the rates. Two years later a humor magazine drew the watchdogs sitting in the palm of the man they were supposed to watch.

Lawyers now call this regulatory capture. It sounds like jargon. It is a simple motion. A public creates a guard to stand between a monopoly and its customers. The monopoly then hires the guard, or elects him, or tires him out, until the guard's reports describe the monopoly's wishes as the public's safety. You do not have to repeal the law. You inhabit it. The Wasp did not have the phrase. It had the picture. Stanford's hand, and inside it, the commission.

The Sandlot.

July, 1877. The country was in the long depression that had started in 1873. In San Francisco, unemployed men gathered on a vacant lot near the city hall, a sandlot, and the meeting that was called against the railroads turned, fast, against the Chinese. The historian Malcolm Harris, who grew up in Palo Alto, follows the crowd up the hill.

They marched on Leland Stanford's mansion. The man at the front of the Workingmen's Party of California was Denis Kearney, an Irish-born drayman, a man who hauled freight for a living, and a speaker who had found the sentence that would move a crowd.

"I will give the Central Pacific just three months to discharge their Chinamen, and if that is not done, Stanford and his crowd will have to take the consequences."

Three months. That is the number in the threat. Now put a census next to it, even though the count comes three years later, in 1880, because the people Kearney was pointing at were not a rumor. The 1880 census counted 75,132 Chinese people in California, in a state of about 865,000. Nearly one Californian in twelve. And of all the Chinese people in the United States, about 105,000, seven in ten lived in that one state.

Kearney's slogan, the one painted on banners, was four words. The Chinese must go. Harris's judgment of the man is short. Kearney was better at being a racist than at being a labor leader. It was easier to make Chinese workers pay for the depression than to make Stanford pay. Stanford had Kearney arrested. Then Stanford did what the very rich have often done when a crowd learns the address. He left town. The family already had a farm south of the city, bought in 1876, about six hundred and fifty acres, a place called Mayfield Grange. The crowd had marched on a mansion. The owner was becoming a country gentleman. The farm would grow, and one day it would have a different name.

Scapegoating is not just meanness. It is a political technology. The anger in the sandlot was not imaginary. Wages were down. The railroad really was a monopoly. A man who hauled goods for a living had reason to hate a company that set the price of moving goods. Kearney took that anger and nailed it to a people who could not vote, who had built the railroad, and who were still in the company's workforce because the company wanted them there. In his 1862 inaugural, read from a text, he said Asia sent the dregs of her population, and he called the Chinese a degraded and distinct people. On the stump the line was shorter. The dregs of Asia. The inaugural is one occasion. The campaign is another. By the time the tracks were finished he wanted more of them, because they kept the wage down. In 1877 his neighbors wanted them gone, and he was happy to let the neighbors do the shouting.

The workers and the owner were not on the same side of the door. The crowd was outside the mansion. The men the crowd wanted fired were the railroad's labor, paid by the month, living in camps the mansion never had to see. A monopoly can survive a riot if the riot aims at its workforce instead of its rates. That is what the sandlot gave the Big Four. A villain who was not them.

The party Kearney fronted did not stay in the street. It got men elected. It helped write the streak of anti-Chinese clauses into the new state constitution of 1879. And it produced, in San Francisco, a mayor the railroad's favorite paper tried to kill.

The Chronicle.

The San Francisco Chronicle began in January of 1865 as a theatrical handbill, the Daily Dramatic Chronicle, started by two brothers, Charles and Michael Henry de Young. Charles was the editor who treated scandal as a product. By the late eighteen seventies the handbill had become a daily, and for a time the railroad had a hand on it. That grip did not make the paper gentle. It made the paper a faction.

In 1879 a Baptist minister named Isaac Kalloch ran for mayor as the Workingmen's candidate. The Chronicle went after him personally. Kalloch answered from the pulpit and, by the paper's own later account, said things about Charles de Young's mother that a man of that temper would not let stand. In August of 1879 Charles de Young shot Kalloch. The minister lived. The sympathy of the shooting helped elect him.

Learn the dates, because the city learned them. On the evening of April twenty-third, 1880, Kalloch's son, Milton Kalloch, walked into the Chronicle office and shot Charles de Young dead at his desk. The New York Times ran the wire the next day, plain as a bill of lading. Charles de Young killed. Mayor Kalloch's son. The Chronicle office. Half past seven.

The son was tried and acquitted. One juror's reported view was that he would have done the same. Michael de Young, the surviving brother, took the paper. He would run it for decades, build a fair, and leave his name on a museum. Charles left a statue and a warning.

A newspaper can be a public record. It can also be a political weapon owned by a side. De Young did not shoot Kalloch because of a disagreement about grain rates. He shot him because the paper and the candidate had become personal enemies, and in that San Francisco the personal was how politics was conducted. The railroad did not order the shot. It did not have to. It had spent years teaching the city that a paper was a piece of property, to be captured, starved of wire service, or aimed. Once that is normal, editors start carrying the same tools as the men they cover.

Put the spring of 1880 on one calendar. April twenty-third, a son kills an editor in a newspaper office on the bay. May eleventh, eighteen days later, seven men die in a wheat field, and an eighth is dying of a scalp wound he does not yet know will kill him. Same state. Same railroad sitting under both stories, not as the finger on every trigger, but as the condition both fights were fought inside. A city and a valley that had come to expect the conflict.

White's line about the press belongs here too. People were not shocked. The Chronicle's own history tells the shootings as office lore. A museum in the park carries the surviving brother's name. The abnormal, repeated, becomes a plaque.

Yuma.

Jump back two years. The night of March twenty-eighth, 1878. Some notices date the death the twenty-ninth. The scene, in Rayner's telling, is a private railroad car standing on a siding at Yuma, on the Colorado River, where California meets Arizona.

Mark Hopkins was sick. He had not wanted the trip. Huntington wanted eyes on the ground the Southern Pacific was reaching for, out past the river, and Hopkins knew that Huntington's schemes usually had something behind them. He got off his sickbed, into his own company's car, and went to look. He went to sleep in the car. He did not wake up.

He was the partner the others could stand. The bookkeeper. The man who had answered panic, in the building years, with as few words as possible. He had let a palace rise on Nob Hill that he would never occupy. He died in the one house he actually used, a car on a company track, at the edge of the map.

Huntington did not come west for the funeral. He was, Rayner notes, too busy. He added that there might be other men as good as Hopkins, but that he would not live long enough to form such a friendship again. Read that twice. The affection is real. So is the arithmetic. A partnership is a machine that expects a man to be replaced, and the man who ran the machine from New York was not going to miss a vote in order to stand by a grave.

This is succession, the unromantic kind. Four men had owned the Central Pacific in equal shares and let a construction company they also owned pay them for building it. By the spring of 1878 one of the four was a car on a siding. Within months Colton, the fifth man they had let partway in, was dead on Nob Hill in a story nobody could finish. The survivors inherited the dead men's leverage, and they used it on the widows. Colton's widow, Ellen, was walked through a story in which her husband had been an embezzler, and she was pressed to give the shares back. Grief was not a pause in the business. It was an opening in the cap table.

Hopkins's house on the hill outlived him as an art school, then as a fire, then as a hotel with his name in gold on the roof. The siding at Yuma did not get a plaque. The railroad kept the car.

Birth of the Octopus.

In 1882 California elected an antimonopoly governor, George Stoneman, a Democrat, and a legislature that looked, on paper, like the railroad's worst day. The new constitution already promised a commission and a harder hand on rates. Stoneman wanted taxes, including back taxes, and a bill to outlaw the special contracts by which the railroad charged one shipper a quiet price and another shipper a punishment. In 1884 he called a special session of the legislature to do railroad business and nothing else.

Count the votes he thought he had. A Democratic majority of sixty-one to nineteen in the assembly. Thirty-two to eight in the senate. The bills sailed through the assembly. In the senate, bill after bill ended in a tie, and the lieutenant governor, a Democrat, broke the tie against them. Nine of ten Democratic senators from San Francisco voted the railroad's way. They were the lambs of a man the city called the Blind Boss.

Christopher Buckley. White uses the nickname the city used, and he cites the historians who wrote Buckley's career up as the Blind Boss of San Francisco. Buckley had publicly supported the antimonopoly bills. Then his senators killed them. White's explanation is not that Buckley loved Stanford. It is that a fight between a railroad and a reform wave was an opportunity only a fool would waste. The railroad could taste defeat, or it could sweeten Buckley's pot. Nineteenth-century slang for the dollar was simoleons. Buckley liked them.

The Pacific Railway Commission, looking back, estimated that the Southern Pacific spent about seven hundred thousand dollars on politics that year. That year is 1884, the special session. In grocery money, at 1884 prices, it is roughly twenty-six million dollars now. It did not all go to one envelope. White describes a stew: railroad money, kickbacks, payments from anyone who needed a franchise or a contract, protection, the ordinary boodle of a city boss, mixed until a senator could not have told you which dollar had started in a freight tariff. That mixing is the method. Once the money is in the stew, every dollar looks like every other dollar.

White's sentence for what came out of the pot is the sentence this episode is named for.

The political marriage of Leland Stanford and Christopher Buckley gave birth to the Octopus.

The creature already had a body in the cartoons. In the Wasp, a drawing called The Curse of California put the railroad on the page as an octopus, tentacles on the farmers, the miners, the whole state. Ambrose Bierce was writing for the Wasp in these years. The public had the image before White had the marriage certificate. What White adds is a date and the hands on it. Not a metaphor floating free. A boss, a senator, a special session, and a pile of money large enough to turn a sure majority into a tie.

Regulatory capture, again, but now you can watch the gears. The voters built a commission and elected a hostile governor. The railroad did not repeal the commission. It bought the senate votes that kept the commission's teeth in a drawer. The law stayed on the books, where it could be pointed at. The practice stayed in Buckley's pocket, where it could be used. A government that looks like it is governing the railroad, and a railroad that is governing the government. That is the octopus. Not the animal. The arrangement.

It did not last in every market. Within a year other transcontinental lines were reaching the coast, and a second track is the one thing an octopus in a rate table cannot survive. The political animal lasted longer. Between the middle eighties and the early nineties, White writes, the Octopus was quite real. In 1885 Stanford, with Buckley's help, bought his way into the United States Senate. Senators were still chosen by state legislatures, not by the voters. A boss who owned a city's delegation owned a piece of the Senate.

Frank Norris published The Octopus in 1901. It is a novel, not a history. In it the railroad raises its tariff to whatever, in the novel's phrase, all the traffic would bear, and its men have a sentence ready.

The railroad could not be operated at a legitimate profit.

Legitimate does the moral work. A rate extracted from a farmer with no other road is redescribed as the minimum a decent company needs in order to exist. Norris's narrator sees tentacles of steel clutching the soil. Fiction, and late, and still the name the public kept. Charge to the point where the customer breaks, and call the breaking point a principle.

Exclusion.

The sandlot got its federal law. It took five years from Kearney's three-month threat, and it arrived wearing the language of a treaty.

In 1879 Congress passed a bill to cut Chinese immigration hard, down toward fifteen passengers on a ship. President Rutherford B. Hayes vetoed it on the first of March, 1879. His reason was a treaty. The United States had promised China, in the Burlingame Treaty, a freer movement of people. Hayes did not veto because California's workers had changed his heart. He vetoed because he did not want to tear up a diplomatic bargain in public. Then he sent a minister to renegotiate the bargain. The new treaty let the United States restrict Chinese immigration, so long as the restriction was reasonable. Reasonable is a lawyer's word. It means whatever the next Congress can pass and the next president will sign.

In 1882 Congress tried for a twenty-year ban on Chinese laborers. President Chester A. Arthur vetoed that one too, as more than the treaty allowed. Congress came back with ten years. On May sixth, 1882, Arthur signed it. The Chinese Exclusion Act. National Archives historians call it the first significant federal law restricting immigration, and the first time federal law barred an ethnic working group because Congress claimed that group endangered the good order of certain places.

Be precise, because the precision is the point. This was not the first unfriendly statute. The Page Act, in 1875, had already made it much harder for Chinese women to enter the country, under the stated target of prostitution and forced labor. The 1882 law was wider. It shut out Chinese laborers, skilled and unskilled, for ten years. It made Chinese people ineligible for citizenship. It required papers of the Chinese who were allowed to come and go, merchants, teachers, students, travelers. A workforce that had been recruited, by this same railroad, with handbills the railroad's own officers could not read, was now a people the federal government would not admit and would not naturalize.

The sequel is how you know a panic has become a policy. In 1892 the Geary Act extended the ban and ordered Chinese residents to carry a certificate of residence or face deportation. In 1902 Congress stretched it again, including to Hawaii and the Philippines. The exclusion laws stayed until 1943, when China was a wartime ally. Congress repealed them and left a quota of a hundred and five people a year. Sixty-one years. A man who had laid rail in the Sierra in 1867 was old, or buried, before the statute that answered him came off the books.

Put the census back beside the statute. Seventy-five thousand Chinese people in California. One in twelve of the state. The men who spoke for the state described an invasion. The count describes a minority, concentrated because the jobs were concentrated, on the railroad, in the fields, in the laundries and the factories of one coast. Exclusion did not break the octopus. It fed it a story. The monopoly remained. The people who had built it were barred at the door.

Stanford's own record is the cleanest exhibit. As governor he had called them a degraded people. As railroad president he had wanted them by the hundred thousand, to bring the wage down. As a senator, years after this law, he would sit in a body that had made the ban normal. The inconsistency is not a mystery and it is not a private paradox. It is a sequence. Use a people when you need their hands. Describe them as a danger when their hands make your neighbors angry. Sign nothing yourself if other men will sign it for you.

Bierce and Hearst.

Ambrose Bierce's collected verse names Stanford, and it names Huntington. The marriage of Stanford and Buckley is still ahead. So is the strangest consequence of Stanford's fight with his own partner.

George Hearst was a miner who had been lucky on a scale that looks like fiction. The Comstock, the Homestake, the Anaconda, claims that became the backbone of American metal. He wanted a Senate seat. In those years the California legislature still handed the seat over. Hearst was appointed to a short vacancy in March of 1886, and then chosen for a full term that began on March fourth, 1887. He died in office in 1891.

He owned a newspaper, the San Francisco Examiner. He had picked it up in 1880, a weak Democratic daily, third in the city. On the day his full Senate term began, March fourth, 1887, the masthead changed. W. R. Hearst, proprietor. William Randolph Hearst was in his middle twenties. He had looked at Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and decided the Examiner should shout. Bigger headlines. Fires, adultery, violent crime, and preferably all three in one story. He hired, at high salaries, a staff of famous writers. Bierce was the first of them.

Watch the sequence that the dates will support. George Hearst's son takes the Examiner. He hires Ambrose Bierce, whose verse already names Stanford and Huntington. The avenue, the mining building, the castle, the masthead. The street sign does not say who hired the writer.

The Caption.

Now jump forward. Past a death we have not reached yet. Past the founding of a university. To a law book, and a paragraph at the top of a case that is not the case.

California tried to tax the railroad's property without letting the company deduct its mortgages the way an ordinary landowner could. The Southern Pacific refused to pay, and turned the refusal into test cases that ran for years. Counties spent time and money they did not have. The judge who heard the cases on the federal circuit in California was Stephen Field, a justice of the United States Supreme Court, riding circuit, which justices still did. The legal scholar Adam Winkler puts the relationship in capital letters. Leland Stanford of the Southern Pacific had a close relationship with Justice Stephen Field.

When the railroad decided to bring the test cases, Field recommended a lawyer, a law professor named John Norton Pomeroy, who admired the way Field wrote opinions. Recommending a lawyer to a friend is not, by itself, a crime. Field then did the thing modern judicial ethics would forbid. He presided. He did not step aside. And during the litigation, Winkler writes, he shared with the railroad's lawyers confidential memoranda about the case written by other justices.

In the circuit opinions, Field said corporations were persons protected by the Fourteenth Amendment. The amendment had been ratified in 1868 for a different person: the formerly enslaved, and anyone a state might try to crush. Its promise was that no state shall deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. Field's move was to slide the railroad into the word person. Treating the company's property differently from a widow's property was, he wrote, the very essence of tyranny. He offered a comparison meant to shock. A tax law that changed its rules according to whether the owner was a white man or a black man would be an outrage. So, he said, was a tax law that changed its rules because the owner was a corporation.

The amendment was written because states were denying rights to black Americans. Field used that sentence to protect a railroad's balance sheet. The people it was written for would spend the next half century losing most of the cases they brought under it. The railroad was just getting started.

In January of 1886 the Southern Pacific's case, Santa Clara County versus Southern Pacific Railroad, reached the Supreme Court itself. The question on the table was Field's question. Does the Fourteenth Amendment cover corporations? Before the argument, Chief Justice Morrison Waite told the lawyers the Court did not want to hear about it. When the decision came down, the Court ruled for the railroad on the narrowest ground available. The county had included the value of the fences along the tracks in the tax bill. California law did not allow that. Because the case could be decided on that ground, the opinion said it was not necessary to consider any other questions, including the grave constitutional questions decided below.

Read that again, because the next man counted on you not to. The Supreme Court did not hold, in 1886, that corporations are persons under the Fourteenth Amendment. It said it did not need to reach the question. It decided a fence.

Then the reporter went to work. His name was J. C. Bancroft Davis. His job was to write the syllabus and the headnotes, the summary at the front of a published opinion, so a busy lawyer can see what was decided without reading the decision. Headnotes are not the law. They are a caption. Davis's caption said this.

The defendant Corporations are persons within the intent of the clause in section one of the Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States, which forbids a state to deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.

Above the opinion, he printed a remark he attributed to the Chief Justice, from before the argument.

The Court does not wish to hear argument on the question whether the provision in the Fourteenth Amendment which forbids a state to deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws applies to these corporations. We are all of the opinion that it does.

Davis checked with Waite before he published. Waite's reply survived.

Davis printed it anyway. He had once been president of a railroad himself, the Newburgh and New York Railway. Winkler does not think the caption was a sleepy mistake. A reader who stopped at the headnote would walk away believing the Court had held that corporations were people. The Court had not.

Three years later the caption became a citation. In 1889 a railroad, the Minneapolis and St. Louis, killed three hogs in Iowa and was ordered to pay twenty-four dollars in damages. Twenty-four dollars. In grocery money of 1889, roughly nine hundred dollars today. The company spent tens of thousands of dollars taking the case to the Supreme Court. The money was cheap if the prize was a stronger shield against every future fence law, livestock law, and tax. Field wrote the opinion. He upheld the Iowa statute. Along the way he wrote that corporations are persons under the Fourteenth Amendment, and then he added one sentence.

It was so held in Santa Clara County versus Southern Pacific Railroad.

It had not been so held. A caption had been promoted into a precedent. Field had written, three years earlier, an angry note that the Court had dodged the constitutional question. He had not forgotten. He cited the dodge as if it were a decision.

Three words. A holding is the rule a court must use to reach its result. Dicta are remarks a judge makes on the way by, which do not control the case. A headnote is a reporter's summary, not a vote. Santa Clara's holding was a fence. The corporate sentence was a headnote, plus a remark the Chief Justice left the reporter free to omit, plus Field later treating that headnote as if the Court had voted it. A strange idea becomes a normal one by repetition, in the book every lawyer keeps on the desk.

In 1912 a lawyer named Charles Wallace Collins, who had served as a librarian for Congress and for the Supreme Court, counted every Fourteenth Amendment case since 1868. Six hundred and four. Twenty-eight involved African Americans, and those plaintiffs usually lost. Three hundred and twelve, more than half, involved corporations, which used the same sentences against laws on wages, zoning, and child labor. Collins called the rebuilt amendment the Magna Charta of accumulated and organized capital.

If those cases were a year, the cases about black Americans would run out before the end of January. The corporate cases would last until November.

Mussel Slough made the papers for a week. The headnote is still in the bound volume. A later court can narrow it. It cannot pretend it was never printed. The railroad did not need to win every case. It needed the word person to be available the next time a state tried to tax a fence.

A Telegram from Florence.

Come back. The caption is two years in the future. The boy is in Italy now.

March, 1884. Leland Stanford is a railroad president and a former governor. The Senate seat is still a year away. The house on Nob Hill has a dome seventy feet high. He and his wife have one child. The boy was born on May fourteenth, 1868. He is fifteen. He is in Florence. David Starr Jordan will call the illness Roman fever. University histories call it typhoid.

On March thirteenth the fever kills him, two months short of his sixteenth birthday. His mother will keep his room as he left it. His father has spent twenty years turning a franchise into a government.

Grief does not launder a fortune. It can change what the fortune is for. The next hour is the farm.

In a hotel in Florence, a fifteen-year-old boy has a fever. The octopus has an heir. The heir will be made of stone, and scholarships, and a clause that says forever.
