Narrator (Kore): What a Mission Was.
Narrator (Kore): In 1774, Father Francisco Palóu put up a wooden cross near a big tree on San Francisquito Creek and marked it as a possible mission site.
Narrator (Kore): [HARD] August 5, 1775. Night. A small Spanish supply ship called the San Carlos, a packet built at the naval base of San Blas, feels her way through a strait that is, at its widest, only about three miles across, and often hidden in fog. Narrator (Kore): The San Carlos was the first ship that did. Her commander was Lieutenant Juan Manuel de Ayala. His pilot, José de Cañizares, took the launch and drew the first working chart of the bay. They anchored by an island Ayala named Isla de los Ángeles. The cove is Ayala Cove. The island is Angel Island. California Historical Landmark 236, at Aquatic Park, Beach and Larkin, marks the entrance. Without that chart, the presidio and the mission of 1776 would have been a guess.
Narrator (Kore): In March 1776, the commander Juan Bautista de Anza and Father Pedro Font rode out to inspect the cross. Font measured the tree with a surveying instrument: fifty varas high, about a hundred and thirty-seven feet. They looked at the creek, decided its flow was too unreliable to water mission fields through a dry summer, and moved on.
Narrator (Kore): The mission went about fifteen miles to the southeast. January 12, 1777. On the bank of the Guadalupe River, near an Ohlone village the Spanish wrote down as So-co-is-u-ka, laurel wood, Franciscan friars plant a cross and put up a shelter for worship. They name the place Misión Santa Clara de Thámien, after Saint Clare of Assisi and after the local people, the Thámien. It is the eighth mission in Alta California.
Narrator (Kore): Every person who came into a mission was written into a book. A baptismal book. A marriage book. A burial book. The line gave a Christian name, the parents, the village, a godparent, and the minister's signature. That is the shape of the form, not one person's entry. The books were kept with real care, which is why historians can count what happened, and why Ohlone families today, including the Muwekma Ohlone Tribe, can trace ancestors through the registers at San Francisco and Santa Clara.
Narrator (Kore): A California mission was a church, a farm, a fort's partner, and a labor system. Its stated purpose was conversion. A baptized Native person was called a neófito, a neophyte, a new believer. An unbaptized one was a gentil, a gentile. California was months by sea from Mexico, and supply ships were small, so the friars needed the neophytes to grow the wheat, tend the cattle, weave the cloth, tan the leather, and render the tallow. Every mission sat in the district of a presidio, a fort with a small garrison. The soldiers protected the friars, and they also went out to bring back people who left.
Narrator (Kore): Once baptized, a neophyte was expected to live at the mission, under the friars' rules, and could not simply walk away. People who left without permission were treated as runaways. Santa Clara University's Ohlone heritage project puts it plainly. Native people from dozens of communities were brought to live together at the mission, and,
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): laboring without pay, these Native Californians built the mission structures and provided the agricultural and industrial workforce to support Spain's colony in California.
Narrator (Kore): Neophytes were not bought and sold, and some came, at least at first, for food, trade goods, or safety. They could not leave freely, they were not paid, and they were physically punished.
Narrator (Kore): Junípero Serra was born Miguel José Serra on November 24, 1713, in Petra, on Mallorca. He held a chair of philosophy at the university in Palma, gave it up in 1749, and was president of the California missions from 1769 until his death on August 28, 1784, at Mission San Carlos Borromeo in Carmel, when only nine of the twenty-one missions existed. He is buried there. He walked that coast on a leg injured years earlier, on the road up from Veracruz.
Narrator (Kore): His successor, Fermín Francisco de Lasuén, founded nine more missions between 1786 and 1798, including La Purísima in 1787.

## 04. The Royal Road
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: borodin from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: horses.mp3, surf.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): The Royal Road.
Narrator (Kore): Letters moved north from Mission Santa Clara on a footpath that was an Ohlone trail long before the friars, along the edge of the hills, above the marshes, past the creek with the tall redwood, to Mission San Francisco de Asís. That mission was founded in 1776, about forty-five miles up the peninsula. From there, letters went on by ship, or south by horse to Monterey, the capital, and from Monterey down the coast to San Diego, and eventually to Mexico City.
Narrator (Kore): By the time all twenty-one missions existed, they stretched from San Diego, founded in 1769, to Sonoma, founded in 1823, about six hundred miles. Spanish California's long-distance freight came by sea, on supply ships from San Blas. The road was for people, animals, letters, and orders.
Narrator (Kore): There was no single El Camino Real in Spanish times. Camino Real means royal road. There were many caminos reales, and most of them followed older Native trade routes. The Autry Museum of the American West says it bluntly. El Camino Real, as a single road lined with missions, each a day's ride from the next, is an American construct of the twentieth century.
Narrator (Kore): Mission San Francisco de Asís is Saint Francis of Assisi, the founder of the Franciscan order. Today almost everyone calls it Mission Dolores, after a nearby creek and lagoon the Anza expedition named for Our Lady of Sorrows, Nuestra Señora de los Dolores. The town by the bay was not called San Francisco at first. It was Yerba Buena, good herb, for a wild mint that grew on the hills.
Narrator (Kore): Mexico City was months away. Whoever controlled the landings and the stamps held everyone else.

## 06. Secularization
[CUE: singing-bowl chapter chime] [MUSIC: mozart40_2 from 0s, ducked] [AMBIENCE: fs_42139.mp3]

Narrator (Kore): Secularization.
Narrator (Kore): 1821. Mexico wins its independence from Spain, and the far-off province of Alta California becomes Mexican.
Narrator (Kore): The missions were run by Spanish-born friars loyal to a church still tied to Spain, and they controlled the best land in California, along with most of its labor. Mexican liberals believed in separating church power from the state. Soldiers and settlers in California wanted that land. Some officials believed the neophytes ought to be free citizens.
Narrator (Kore): The word is secularization, from the Latin saeculum, the world, as opposed to the cloister. A secular priest serves a parish under a bishop. A regular priest, like a Franciscan, lives under a religious order. To secularize a mission meant, on paper, to turn it into an ordinary parish and to release its lands, herds, and people.
Narrator (Kore): It came in steps. On July 25, 1826, California's first Mexican-born governor, José María de Echeandía, issued a proclamation of emancipation that let qualified neophytes in the southern districts leave mission rule and become Mexican citizens.

Narrator (Kore): By December 31, 1832, at the peak of the system, the missions together held about one hundred fifty-one thousand head of cattle and about one hundred thirty-eight thousand sheep. Measles epidemics in 1806 and 1828 had already swept through the crowded compounds. The missions kept needing new converts because the people already there kept dying.
Narrator (Kore): Harris writes that disease, rather than extermination campaigns, killed about half of the roughly three hundred thousand Native people of Alta California under Spain and Mexico.
Narrator (Kore): On August 17, 1833, the Mexican Congress passed the decree for the secularization of the missions of California. On August 9, 1834, Governor José Figueroa issued the rules for how it would work. A vara is a Spanish yard, about thirty-three inches, so that plot came to somewhere between about seven and twenty-eight acres. Half the livestock, shared out. Narrator (Kore): 1839. In Monterey, the governor of Alta California is a Californio named Juan Bautista Alvarado, born in Monterey in 1809, so thirty that year, a man who had come to power in 1836 in a local revolt against a governor sent from Mexico City. Narrator (Kore): That piece of ground, today, is the northern part of the Stanford University campus.
Narrator (Kore): A grant worked like this. The proving of it comes before this hour ends. You petitioned the governor. If you could not write, a neighbor wrote it. You attached a diseño, the drawing, a map with no surveyor behind it: a creek bend, a big oak, a hill. The governor asked the local officials and the mission whether the land was free and whether you owned cattle. If they said yes, he issued a título, a title. Then a local official walked the bounds with you and put you in juridical possession. The oak was the corner. The neighbors were the survey.
Narrator (Kore): What is missing is a precise line. A grant might say two leagues, more or less. Under Mexican law a governor could grant as little as a third of an acre or as much as eleven square leagues, about fifty thousand acres. Two leagues was most common. In a country with plenty of land and few people, more or less was fine. Your neighbors knew where the oak was.
Narrator (Kore): Fourteen hundred seventy-one acres is almost six square kilometers, nearly three times the country of Monaco.
