# Cardinal — Cinematic Moments (by episode)

Quotes were checked against the EPUB text extracted on Oct 13, 2026. Page numbers are given only where the EPUB carries print page markers (White-J, Baker, Siddiqui); elsewhere the chapter is the locator. Flags mark contested or single-source items.

## Episode 2
- **Chang, Ghosts of Gold Mountain** (Ch 7 “The Strike”): “The men who were working at that hour walked out of the tunnels and away from the tracks. The ones who were sleeping slept on…”
  - *Why:* A silent walkout in the snow.
- **Rayner, The Associates** (Ch 2): “Death is carrying off the Americans most fearfully,” Huntington wrote to his brother. “There are eight to ten deaths a day.”
  - *Why:* Huntington stuck in the mud of Panama in 1849: our toll-man starts as a stranded traveler.
- **Rayner, The Associates** (Notes, ch 3): Judah, Sacramento Union, Nov 9, 1860: “I have struck a lucky streak.”
  - *Why:* The surveyor's optimism before he is pushed out.
- **Ambrose, Nothing Like It** (Ch 11 (1867)): “In late May, unbelievably and unacceptably, the Chinese went on strike. ‘This is the hardest blow we have had,’ E. B. Crocker reported.”
  - *Why:* Labor revolt at the summit, with the boss's own words. **[Ambrose's framing is dated; pair with Chang]**
- **Ambrose, Nothing Like It** (Ch 16): Crocker “offered a bet of $10,000 to Durant, saying that the CP would lay ten miles of track in one day.”
  - *Why:* A wager and a race against the clock.
- **Ambrose, Nothing Like It** (Ch 17 “Done”): “Stanford swung and missed, striking only the rail. It made no difference. The telegraph operator closed the circuit and the wire went out, ‘done!’”
  - *Why:* The perfect cold open: the hero misses and the nation cheers anyway.

## Episode 2/3

  - *Why:* Hidden books; the end-turn of Ep 2.

## Episode 3
- **Winkler, We the Corporations** (Ch 4): “Davis, it turns out, had once served as president of the Newburgh and New York Railway Company.”
  - *Why:* The court reporter who wrote the corporate-personhood headnote had run a railroad. **[DEEP CUT]**
- **Winkler, We the Corporations** (Chronology): 1886: Davis “includes an inaccurate headnote saying the Supreme Court held that corporations were persons.”
  - *Why:* A footnote that becomes law.
- **White, Railroaded** (Ch 7): Paraphrase: Denis Kearney, “a San Francisco drayman and petty entrepreneur,” turns anti-Chinese politics into an insurgency in the late 1870s.
  - *Why:* The sandlot rallies.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 1.2): During the 1877 crisis, workers “marched up to Leland Stanford’s mansion and threatened him.” Kearney: “I will give the Central Pacific just three months to discharge their Chinamen.”
  - *Why:* A mob at the Nob Hill door.
- **Rayner, The Associates** (Ch 12): Hopkins dies in his rail car at a siding in Yuma, Mar 28, 1878; Huntington: “My old friend Mr. Hopkins has gone over the river.”
  - *Why:* A tycoon dying alone in his private car.
- **Rayner, The Associates** (Ch 13): The railroad gets its patents to San Joaquin lands, then announces prices “ranging from $17 to $40 an acre.” This leads to Mussel Slough.
  - *Why:* A price notice that becomes a gunfight. **[Rayner + standard histories]**
- **Rayner, The Associates** (Ch 17): Quoting Norris’s The Octopus: “California likes to be fooled.”
  - *Why:* A line the narrator can hold in reserve.

## Episode 3→4
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 1.3): “On March 13, 1884, two months before his sixteenth birthday, the Stanfords’ only child died.”
  - *Why:* A fever room in Florence: the single death that creates the university.

## Episode 4
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 1.3): Stanford rehired Muybridge in 1876, “apparently not begrudging Helios the murder and child abandonment” in between.
  - *Why:* The man who invents cinema on the farm had just killed his wife's lover. **[DEEP CUT]**
- **Miller, Why Fish Don't Exist** (Ch 4): Paraphrase: in 1890 the Stanfords travel to Bloomington to recruit Jordan; Jane soon becomes “the only mark on David’s dream life.”
  - *Why:* The president and the widow at odds. **[Miller argues Jordan's involvement; contested]**
- **Miller, Why Fish Don't Exist** (Ch 9): Paraphrase: upon learning the death would be ruled a poisoning, Jordan “set sail for Hawaii.”
  - *Why:* The president races to control the story. **[contested]**
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 38, p. 217): “By the time David Starr Jordan and Timothy Hopkins reached Honolulu, it was apparent that all the suspects were lying.”
  - *Why:* A noir line.
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 43, p. 248): On Mar 15, the day Jordan left with the body, “Tay Wang” wrote to Jordan asking for a meeting. “It is the strangest piece of evidence…”
  - *Why:* A mystery letter at the dock.
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Notes ch 16): Ross's offending speech was reported as “Warning Against Coolie ‘Natives’ and Japanese” (Call, May 8, 1900).
  - *Why:* Jane fired a professor for attacking the Chinese labor her husband's railroad used. **[DEEP CUT]**
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Intro (text00007)): “The children of California shall be our children,” Leland Stanford told his wife, Jane, when they decided to build Palo Alto.
  - *Why:* A vow at the founding that the whole series pays off: the institution claims the future's kids.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 1.3 “Blood That Trots”): Paraphrase: Muybridge photographs Stanford's trotters at the Palo Alto farm to settle whether all four hooves leave the ground. It's the first motion picture.
  - *Why:* A frozen horse in mid-air: the farm literally invents cinema.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 2.1 “Local Ghosts”): The chapter subtitle reads “The Almost Certain Murder of [redacted-style asterisks]”.
  - *Why:* Harris's playful censoring is itself a scene beat for Jane's death. **[contested; one-sentence treatment]**
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 17, p. 101): On the night of Nov 14, Jordan visits historian George Howard and tells him the faculty should “keep still.”
  - *Why:* A night visit to silence a professor.
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 28, p. 167): “On February 15, 1905, roughly a month after the Poland Spring Water incident, Bertha Berner and a maid, May Hunt, accompanied Jane Stanford on the steamship Korea…”
  - *Why:* A second attempt follows the first; departure by ship.
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 29, p. 173): “Oh God, forgive me my sins… Is my soul prepared to meet my dear ones?… This a horrible death to die.”
  - *Why:* Last words. The series' strongest cold open.
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 36, p. 208): “…the combination of strychnine in both Mrs. Stanford’s body and the bicarbonate seemed conclusive. She had been poisoned.”
  - *Why:* The chemists' verdict. **[the murder is contested (Jordan argued heart failure); one sentence]**
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Notes ch 36, p. 339): Newspaper headline: “Mrs. Stanford Died from Natural Causes” (Examiner, Mar 14, 1905).
  - *Why:* The institution's counter-story in a headline. **[contested]**
- **White, Who Killed Jane Stanford?** (Ch 45, p. 266): The choir sings “Nearer, My God, to Thee,” and the casket goes into the steel chest in the mausoleum.
  - *Why:* Episode-ending image.

## Episode 5
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 2.3): When Hoover had typhoid as a junior, his professor Branner “even paid his favorite pupil’s medical bills.”
  - *Why:* The orphan is adopted by the institution.
- **Whyte, Hoover** (Ch 2, p. 43): “It was in Branner’s presence, in a geology lab, that Lou Henry first laid eyes on Herbert Hoover.”
  - *Why:* A love story in a lab.
- **Whyte, Hoover** (Ch 5, p. 100): A 1905 Royal Commission found the air at Hoover's Sons of Gwalia mine “notably bad.”
  - *Why:* The future humanitarian's mine. **[DEEP CUT]**
- **Whyte, Hoover** (Ch 6, p. 124): Hoover joined the Stanford board of trustees in 1912, “in circumstances as cloudy as the awarding of his geology degree.”
  - *Why:* The pioneer student becomes the owner's representative.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 2.3 “Hooverville”): Paraphrase: Hoover, born 1874, is in Stanford's pioneer class, then a mining engineer in Western Australia and China.
  - *Why:* An orphan boy turning into a global mining fixer.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 2.4): Paraphrase: Terman, the “genius son” of the IQ inventor; internment of the Santa Clara Japanese in the same chapter.
  - *Why:* The father ranks children; the son builds the industry. Intercut with families taken away.

## Episode 6
- **Levine, Surveillance Valley** (ARPA chapter (EPUB part0008)): SRI was “doing ARPA contract work on chemical warfare in Vietnam” while Lick funded Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center.
  - *Why:* The Mother of All Demos and napalm research in the same building. **[DEEP CUT]**
- **Fisher, Valley of Genius** (Preface): “Doug Engelbart’s 1968 demonstration… is known as the Mother of All Demos.”
  - *Why:* Origin myth.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 3.1 “Space Settlers”): “The first lease at the Stanford Industrial Park was granted to Varian’s R & D department in 1953—no surprise, because Terman was an investor and board…”
  - *Why:* The insider signature on the first lease.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 3.2 “The Solid State”): Shockley’s recruits, “a team Shockley later tarred as ‘the traitorous eight.’”
  - *Why:* A betrayal origin myth with a villain's own nickname.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Section III frontispiece): Poster: “Strike Against the Stanford Empire” (1970), April Third Movement archive.
  - *Why:* A ready-made title card.

## Episode 7
- **Wu, The Master Switch** (Ch 9): FM radio's nonprofit start was “a precursor of Stanford’s funding of the Google search engine in the 1990s.”
  - *Why:* An outsider sees the same pattern.
- **Chafkin, The Contrarian** (Ch 2, p. 24): Thiel “poured his grievances into the Stanford Review… that he founded that spring.”
  - *Why:* The grievance paper that seeds a political machine.
- **Soni, The Founders** (Photo insert): Musk's joy “when the X.com debit card successfully spit out money at an ATM close to the company’s Palo Alto office.”
  - *Why:* A small, human beat.
- **Vance, Elon Musk** (Zip2 chapter (unpaginated EPUB)): “As the story goes, Musk dropped out of Stanford after two days.”
  - *Why:* Stanford's most powerful non-alumnus. **[DEEP CUT (Vance says “as the story goes”)]**
- **McCartney, Friends in High Places** (Ch 1): Steve Bechtel's chauffeur drives him to “a secluded, 2,700-acre stand of redwoods… the Bohemian Grove.”
  - *Why:* Cold-open texture for Ep 7. **[second source with Denton, so the Grove is allowed as a detail]**
- **McCartney, Friends in High Places** (Ch 14): Shultz, “the outsider and former academic,” becomes president of Bechtel a year after joining.
  - *Why:* Stanford professor → Bechtel → Secretary of State → Hoover Institution. **[DEEP CUT]**
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Section IV frontispiece): Security fences built around HP’s Deer Creek lab after a 1976 bomb attack (Measure, Aug–Sept 1976).
  - *Why:* Suburban tech behind fences: tension in a sunny frame.
- **Harris, Palo Alto** (Ch 4.2 “War Capitalism”): Chapter topic: “The Office of Technology Licensing and the Privatization of the Cold War University.”
  - *Why:* The licensing office as the new ticket booth.
- **Berlin, Troublemakers** (Ch 15): Paraphrase: Kleiner & Perkins starts at 3000 Sand Hill Road; Bob Swanson, just fired from the firm, goes on to found Genentech.
  - *Why:* A fired kid at the gate becomes a biotech empire.
- **Berlin, Troublemakers** (Part 3 opener): Paraphrase: Niels Reimers's recombinant-DNA patent application is “criticized for its potential to undermine Stanford’s fundamental mission.”
  - *Why:* The university patents life itself.
- **Berlin, Troublemakers** (Ch 20): “‘We didn’t need a business plan for Pong or the VCS,’ Alcorn replied. He needed one now.”
  - *Why:* The moment a garage becomes a corporation.
- **Levy, In the Plex** (Ch 1): “That’s why Page called his system BackRub.”
  - *Why:* The unglamorous original name.
- **Levy, In the Plex** (Ch 1): Page's Excite plan: “Excite’s total outlay would be $1.6 million, including $300,000 to Stanford for the license.”
  - *Why:* The one that got away. Stanford's cut sits inside the pitch.
- **Levy, In the Plex** (Ch 1): The Menlo Park house owner charges Google “$1,700 a month to rent the garage and several rooms.”
  - *Why:* The garage myth with a rent receipt.
- **Levy, In the Plex** (Ch 2): VC meetings in “the glass-walled buildings in Menlo Park’s Sand Hill Road.”
  - *Why:* The gate again.
- **Denton, The Profiteers** (Ch 12): On the Watergate tapes, Nixon calls Bohemian Grove “the most faggy goddamned thing you could ever imagine.” The Bechtels court Shultz there.
  - *Why:* Bechtel bonds formed at the Grove. **[single-source, per the Grove rule; slur, so handle with care or paraphrase]**
- **Denton, The Profiteers** (Ch 35): Shultz at his 1982 nomination hearing: “Private interests have no business with nuclear weapons.”
  - *Why:* A line undercut by his employer's history. **[single-source framing]**

## Episode 8
- **Mallaby, The Power Law** (Ch 13, p. 334): “In 2008… Sequoia hired two investors from the Stanford endowment.”
  - *Why:* The endowment and VC become one circuit. **[DEEP CUT]**

  - *Why:* A Stanford dropout's fraud happened outside the gate.

  - *Why:* Naming a surveillance firm after a corrupting orb.
- **Baker, How to Rule the World** (Ch 4, p. 94): “I called Sam, the Daily editor. ‘Holy shit,’ he said. ‘We need to get going.’”
  - *Why:* A freshman reporter takes on the president.
- **Baker, How to Rule the World** (Ch 6, p. 120): The Rosewood bills itself as “the modern clubhouse for Silicon Valley… directly on Sand Hill Road… on land owned by Stanford.”
  - *Why:* The land lease still pays, 70 years on.
- **Siddiqui, Hubris Maximus** (Ch 6): “Musk had sat in the Oval Office opposite President Trump…”
  - *Why:* Founder and president, face to face.
- **Siddiqui, Hubris Maximus** (Ch 15): Musk's lawyer after the 420 verdict: “The jury got it right.”
  - *Why:* Untouchability as a pattern (a mirror of Ep 3's courts).
