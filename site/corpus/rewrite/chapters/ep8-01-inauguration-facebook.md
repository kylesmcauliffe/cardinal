# Episode 8: The Open Era

## The rotunda

**Washington, D.C. January 20, 2025.**

The cold was not the story. The seating was.

In the Capitol rotunda, Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Jeff Bezos, Sundar Pichai, and Tim Cook were placed in the first rows behind Donald Trump's family — ahead of much of the incoming cabinet in the photographs the wire services published that afternoon. The *Wall Street Journal* reported that the chief executives of the world's largest technology companies sat "directly behind Trump" while he took the oath, a prominence the paper contrasted with cabinet secretaries still finding their places. (Collins 2025; AP News 2025; see rewrite/notes/inauguration-2025-01-20.md)

Musk had spent more than a quarter of a billion dollars to help elect him. Zuckerberg and Bezos had ended years of public friction with Trump and appeared at St. John's Church that morning alongside Cook and Pichai. (Reuters 2025-01-20; BBC 2025-01-20)

Nobody in the rotunda needed a historian to explain what the seating meant. They needed only to look.

Then cut back twenty-one years.

## Palo Alto

**Palo Alto, California. 2004.**

A moderation guideline is a sentence written by an employee that determines what a hundred million people may say. The chapter opens there because it is the most accurate available description of what the company had become.

When does subliminal become explicit?

On 20 January 2025, at the presidential inauguration in the Capitol rotunda, the
founders and chief executives of several of the largest technology companies in
the world were seated in front of much of the incoming cabinet.

The chapter begins with the seating and largely declines to interpret it,
because the proximity is the evidence and it needs no assistance.

Everything load-bearing in this chapter comes from disclosure filings, which is
the only responsible way to write about living people in a book making this
argument.

Peter Thiel contributed roughly fifteen million dollars to J. D. Vance's 2022
Senate campaign in Ohio, principally through a supporting committee — one of the
largest sums ever directed by a single donor to a single Senate candidate. Thiel
had employed Vance at his investment firm, had mentored him, and had introduced
him to the network described in Act III chapter 13.

Vance won the seat in November 2022. He was elected vice president in November
2024.

That is the network from a single company's payroll in 2002 reaching the second
office of the United States within twenty-two years, and it can be documented
entirely from Federal Election Commission records. No claim about coordination
is required, and none is made.

The broader pattern is visible in the same filings.

Technology executives and investors contributed at scale to committees
supporting the successful campaign, and several who had previously been publicly
associated with the other party or with no party endorsed publicly for the first
time. Elon Musk's contributions through a supporting committee were among the
largest individual political expenditures on record, and he campaigned
personally.

The chapter cites amounts from filings and declines to characterise motives,
because the motives are not knowable and the filings are.

After the inauguration Musk was given a formal advisory position in the federal
government concerned with spending reduction and the reorganisation of federal
agencies, described in official announcements and reported extensively.

The chapter records the position, its stated remit, and its authority as
described in the establishing documents. Where the position's boundaries were
disputed — and they were, in litigation and in congressional correspondence — it
says so.

What is structurally novel is not that a wealthy person had influence over
government. That is Act I chapter 10, and Collis Huntington was more candid
about it. What is novel is a serving executive of firms holding major federal
contracts occupying a formal advisory role over the agencies that let them, with
the conflict identified and not resolved.

The argument of this chapter is that January 2025 changed the visibility rather
than the substance, and that this distinction is the whole point of the book.

The relationships were not new. They had been building for two decades in
disclosure filings that almost nobody read, in board seats, in a network that
funded itself, and in an institution that supplied the founders. Every mechanism
this book describes was operating in 2015 exactly as it was operating in 2025.

What ended was the discretion. An elite that once exercised influence through
donations, foundations, and quiet access began exercising it in front of
cameras, on a platform one of them owned, from a seat in the rotunda.

The book's thesis stopped requiring inference and started being televised. It
was equally true the morning before.

There is one further novelty. In 2022 one of the men in the rotunda had bought a
communications platform outright and reoriented its moderation and ranking
policies according to his own stated views. A previous generation of proprietors
bought newspapers, and the comparison is fair as far as it goes. It goes less
far than it looks, because a newspaper's readers knew they were reading a
proprietor's newspaper, and a ranking algorithm's users generally do not
experience the feed as an editorial product at all.

At an event at Capital One Arena on the day of the inauguration, Musk made a
gesture that was immediately and widely discussed, with interpretations
diverging sharply and along predictable lines.

This book does not adjudicate it.

The gesture occurred and was recorded, the dispute about what it meant was
extensive and remains unresolved, and intent is not knowable from a video. The
project's rule for contested material is to state what is documented, name the
disagreement, and build nothing on it, and that rule is applied here.

A seating chart is a fact. An advisory role established by
executive action is a fact. The gesture is a controversy, and controversies age
badly while filings do not.

The drafts of this project leaned on this episode heavily and also contained
several 2025 events narrated as fact that could not be verified at all. Those
have been removed, and the reasoning is recorded in the project's cut-material

The realignment that produced the rotunda seating deserves a section, because it
was not inevitable and happened quickly.

For most of the period covered by Act III, the corridor's politics were broadly
liberal on social questions and libertarian on economic ones, and its money went
predominantly to Democrats. The industry's self-image was progressive, and its
relationship with Democratic administrations was close enough that the traffic
in personnel between them was routine.

Several things changed that. Antitrust enforcement became bipartisan, and the
appointments made after 2021 were more hostile to the industry's structural
position than anything in the preceding forty years. Content moderation became a
partisan battlefield in which the platforms were attacked from both directions
and satisfied neither. Cryptocurrency regulation and the tax treatment of
unrealised gains became salient. And a generation of founders who had
experienced the internal politics of their own companies during the late 2010s
concluded that their workforces were an adversary.

The result was that a portion of the industry's most prominent figures moved,
publicly and rapidly, and the 2024 cycle was the first in which technology money
was a major factor on the Republican side.

It was the same set of positions — light regulation, favourable capital
taxation, resistance to antitrust, and freedom to operate — pursued through a
different party because the other one had stopped supplying them. That is what
an interest does, and describing it as an interest rather than as a conversion
is the analytically accurate move.

The chapter closes on a contrast and then stops.

A citizen's participation in an American election consists of a vote. In 2022 a
single investor directed fifteen million dollars to a single Senate campaign,
and the candidate became vice president.

Both facts are legal. Both are documented. They are not the same kind of
participation, and the difference is not a matter of opinion.

And the reader should notice what this chapter did not have to do. It made no
claim about a meeting, a plan, or an agreement. It cited filings, a seating
arrangement, and an executive action. The argument of this book does not require
anybody to have conspired, and at the point where the mechanism becomes fully
visible it turns out that nothing needed to be hidden in the first place.

Act I chapter 11 described a crowd on a San Francisco sandlot being directed
toward the Chinese while the railroad's owners watched from Nob Hill. The
mechanism has not changed. What has changed is that the men on the hill no
longer find it necessary to stay up there.

When a platform becomes infrastructure, who is responsible for what it does?

A moderation guideline is a sentence, written by an employee, that determines
what several hundred million people may say to each other.

It is revised continuously. It is published only in part. It is enforced at a
scale that requires automation, and the automation is imperfect in ways that
fall unevenly. And for most of the period this chapter covers, the company that
wrote it insisted that it was not making editorial decisions at all.

Facebook was founded in a Harvard dormitory in 2004 and moved to Palo Alto
within months, into the corridor described in the previous act. Its first
outside investment came from Peter Thiel, from the group in Act III chapter 13.

Its growth mechanics were straightforward and unusually effective. A real-name
policy made the social graph accurate, which made it far more valuable to
advertisers than the pseudonymous networks that preceded it. Expansion school by
school created density before breadth, so that the product was useful on arrival
rather than empty. And network effects did the rest: a social network is worth
what its membership is worth, which means a better competitor with fewer users
is not a better product.

Two acquisitions closed the position. Instagram in 2012, for about a billion
dollars, when it had a small team and no revenue. WhatsApp in 2014, for roughly
nineteen billion. Both prices were widely mocked and both were rational: each
target was the most plausible route by which a competitor could reach comparable
scale.

The advertising system extended Google's and differed from it in a way that
matters.

A search query is a stated intention, as Act III chapter 12 described. A social
feed has no stated intention; the user is not looking for anything. What the
platform sells instead is inferred disposition — an assessment, built from
behaviour, of what a person is like and what they might be susceptible to,
combined with the ability to reach precisely defined slices of the population.

That is a more invasive product than search advertising and a more powerful one
for certain purposes, because it can reach people who were not looking.

Throughout its expansion the company described itself as a technology company
and a neutral platform rather than a media company, and this was not merely
rhetoric. It was load-bearing.

Section 230, the 1996 provision quoted in the PayPal chapter, provides that a service is not treated as
the publisher of content supplied by its users. That protection is what made the
business possible at scale. But the same platform was continuously making
editorial decisions — ranking the feed, promoting some material and demoting
other material, and removing content under policies it wrote itself.

A system that decides what a billion people see when they open an
application is exercising editorial judgment, whatever the statute calls it. The
company's own engineers understood this; the ranking algorithm was the product.

The position collapsed in public over roughly two years.

The 2016 American election produced sustained scrutiny of political
misinformation and of foreign influence operations conducted through the
platform. In March 2018 reporting in the *Observer* and the *New York Times*
revealed that Cambridge Analytica had obtained data on tens of millions of
Facebook users, harvested through a personality quiz application that had also
collected information about users' friends under permissions the platform had
granted developers.

The company's founder testified before Congress. In July 2019 the Federal Trade
Commission announced a settlement including a five-billion-dollar penalty — at
the time by far the largest privacy-related penalty the agency had imposed —
along with structural requirements on privacy oversight.

The chapter uses these figures because they are documented in agency records,
and it notes that several widely repeated user and account numbers from this
period have been reported inconsistently and should be checked against filings
rather than against coverage.

The most serious material in this chapter is not about privacy.

In 2018 the United Nations Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on
Myanmar reported on the campaign of violence against the Rohingya and addressed
the role of social media, finding that Facebook had been a significant
instrument for the spread of hate speech in a country where, for many users, the
platform was effectively the internet. The mission's report was direct about the
company's slow and inadequate response.

Facebook commissioned an independent human rights impact assessment, published
the same year, which concluded that the platform had been used to incite offline
violence and that the company had not done enough to prevent it. The company
accepted the findings and described measures it was taking.

The structural facts are these. The platform had expanded into a country with
little local-language moderation capacity, in a context of documented ethnic
tension, and the ranking systems that maximised engagement did what such systems
do with inflammatory material. The company was warned by civil society
organisations inside Myanmar years before the crisis and did not act at
sufficient scale.

This is what it means for a platform to be infrastructure. A telephone company
is not responsible for what people say on the phone; it is also not amplifying
selected calls to millions of people because they generate engagement.

The people who enforce the rules are largely not employed by the company that
writes them.

Content moderation is performed substantially by contracting firms, in Manila,
in Nairobi, in Hyderabad, in Warsaw, and in Phoenix and Austin, by workers paid
a small fraction of engineering salaries to view the material that is removed.
The psychological consequences are documented in reporting, in litigation, and
in a 2020 settlement with American moderators over trauma-related claims.
Workers in Kenya have brought their own cases.

The line from Act I chapter 11 is direct. The system's stability depends on
people it does not employ, held at arm's length through a contracting
arrangement, doing the work that makes the product usable and appearing nowhere
in the description of it.

The internal evidence about how the ranking system behaved became public in
2021, and it is the most useful documentation available anywhere of how a
chokepoint on attention actually operates.

A former employee provided a large volume of internal research to regulators and
to the *Wall Street Journal*, and testified before Congress. The documents
included the company's own studies of the effects of its products, and the
finding that received most attention concerned Instagram's effects on teenage
girls' body image.

The more structurally important material concerned ranking. Internal research
examined how changes to the feed algorithm affected what was distributed, and
documented that content provoking strong reactions travelled further than
content that did not, with predictable consequences for the tone of political
discussion on the platform. Researchers proposed changes; some were adopted and
some were not, and the tradeoff against engagement was explicit in the
deliberations.

The company disputed the characterisation of the documents and argued that the
selections were unrepresentative, which is a fair objection to make about any
leaked archive and does not dispose of the contents.

What the episode establishes for this book's purposes is narrower than the
headlines and more damaging. The firm knew, in detail, from its own research,
how its ranking systems shaped what people saw and how they behaved, and it made
decisions about that with commercial considerations in the room. That is
editorial judgment by any ordinary meaning of the phrase, exercised at a scale
no publisher in history has approached, by a company whose legal position was
that it was not a publisher.

Not the law, and not the business model.

What changed after 2018 was believability. The company's account of itself — a
neutral technology platform connecting people, with no editorial role and no
responsibility for outcomes — stopped being accepted by regulators, by
journalists, and eventually by a substantial part of its own workforce.

That is the same failure Herbert Hoover suffered in 1932, described in Act II
chapter 8. The machinery was intact and the explanation had stopped working.

The company changed its name in 2021 and continued.

## A letter to a daughter

December 1, 2015. Days after their daughter was born, Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan published a letter to her, and near its end a pledge: they would give 99 percent of their Facebook shares, then worth about $45 billion, during their lives "to advance human potential and promote equality." The vehicle was the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, and it was not a foundation. It was a limited liability company that could fund nonprofits, make private investments and take part in policy debates. That same day the company told the Securities and Exchange Commission that Zuckerberg would control the voting and disposition of any shares the new entity held, and that he intended to keep his majority vote.
