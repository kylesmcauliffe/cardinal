What happens when the legitimacy engine loses credibility?

The most consequential piece of accountability journalism about Stanford in a
generation was written by undergraduates.

In November 2022 the *Stanford Daily* published an investigation into research
papers co-authored by the university's president, Marc Tessier-Lavigne, raising
questions about manipulated images in work from laboratories he had led. The
reporting was led by a freshman.

Eight months later he resigned.

Precision matters here more than anywhere else in this act, because this is
where the coverage was loosest and where a careless account would be both unfair
and analytically useless.

The university's board appointed a special committee, which retained outside
counsel and independent scientific advisers. Its report, released in July 2023,
found that multiple papers on which Tessier-Lavigne was a principal author
contained manipulated data or serious deficiencies, and that these had occurred
in his laboratories.

It also found that the manipulation had been done by others, and that the
evidence did not establish that he personally knew of or participated in it. Its
criticism of him was that he had failed to correct the scientific record with
sufficient decisiveness once problems became apparent, over a period of years.

He resigned as president on 19 July 2023, retained his faculty position, and
requested retractions of three papers and corrections to others.

The distinction between misconduct by a principal investigator and inadequate
correction of the record by one is real, and a book that collapses it forfeits
the right to complain about anyone else's precision.

The scandal is not the point. Universities have scandals.

The point is what the credential rests on. This book has argued since Act II
chapter 6 that a credential is an instrument for lowering the cost of trusting a
stranger, and that its value derives entirely from the issuer's capacity to
verify. Stanford's product is verification: this person has been assessed, this
work has been checked, this finding can be relied upon.

A failure of verification at the very top of the institution is a failure of the
product. It is not a moral embarrassment; it is a defect in the thing being
sold.

The Hoover Institution's position on campus became a sustained argument in the
same period, and it is the tension Act II chapter 5 planted.

A university claims institutional disinterest — it does not have positions, its
scholars do. The Hoover Institution has a stated mission with substantive
political content and has supplied personnel to Republican administrations for
half a century. Whether an institution of that kind belongs inside a university
claiming neutrality is a genuine question, and both positions have serious
advocates. Faculty petitions and public arguments over particular fellows' roles
recurred through this period.

Admissions came under simultaneous pressure from two directions. The 2019
admissions bribery prosecutions revealed a market in fraudulent athletic
recruitment and test administration at elite institutions including Stanford, in
which wealthy families purchased places through a consultant. And in June 2023
the Supreme Court held in the Students for Fair Admissions cases that race-
conscious admissions programmes at Harvard and the University of North Carolina
were unlawful, ending an arrangement that had operated for decades.

Between them these episodes put the same question in two forms: what is the
credential actually screening for, and does anyone still believe the answer?

One pressure on the institution is older than any of these and is the same
pressure Act I described.

Stanford remains among the largest landowners on the San Francisco Peninsula, on
the acreage the Founding Grant forbade it to sell. Its expansion is governed by
agreements with Santa Clara County, and the negotiations over those agreements
have for years turned on housing: how much the university must build, for whom,
and at what price, in a region where the cost of housing has become the dominant
political fact.

The university's own workforce cannot generally afford to live near it. Neither
can the teachers, nurses, and municipal employees of the surrounding towns. East
Palo Alto, the community across the freeway described in Act III chapter 1, has
been substantially reshaped by displacement pressure from the same market.

The endowment that made the institution permanent is a land portfolio whose
appreciation is inseparable from a housing crisis, and the institution is a
principal beneficiary of that appreciation and a participant in the local
politics that determines whether more housing gets built.

This is the least glamorous of the pressures on Stanford and probably the most
consequential locally. A university endowed with railroad land in 1885 is, a
hundred and forty years later, a large landholder in a region where land is the
binding constraint on ordinary life.

There is a structural account of the research problems that is more damning than
the personal one, and the chapter should give it.

A principal investigator at a major research university runs a laboratory that
may have dozens of people in it, funded by grants that must be continuously
renewed, in a system where publication in a small number of high-prestige
journals determines careers. The investigator is an administrator and a
fundraiser as much as a scientist, and cannot personally verify most of the work
published under their name. The junior researchers doing the work are on
temporary contracts, dependent on the investigator for their futures, and
rewarded for positive results.

Those incentives reliably produce a certain rate of image manipulation,
selective reporting, and unreplicable findings, and the replication crisis
across several fields over the last fifteen years is the aggregate evidence.

That is a defect in the design of the research system rather than in the
character of individuals, and the individuals who bear the cost are
overwhelmingly the junior researchers, whose careers depend on outcomes they do
not control and who are the ones most likely to be blamed when something is
found.

Applications continued to rise. The endowment continued to grow. Licensing
revenue continued. Graduates continued to be placed, and the corridor described
in Act III continued to draw its founders from the same source.

One number makes the point. Applications to selective American universities rose
through precisely the period in which public confidence in higher education, as
measured by the standard survey series, fell sharply. Both things are true at
once, and they are not in tension. People can believe an institution is self-
serving and still want the credential it issues, because the credential's value
does not depend on the institution being admired. It depends on employers
continuing to read it.

The machine is intact. What has eroded is the belief that its outputs can be
taken on faith, and this book has now shown that erosion three times: the
Reformation in Act I chapter 7, the Depression in Act II chapter 8, and here.

In each case the mechanism survived and the story did not, and in each case the
institution responded by restructuring rather than reforming.

The consequence, which the next chapter is about, is that an elite which no
longer enjoys automatic deference stops bothering to be subtle. Legitimacy that
must be argued for is expensive. Power exercised openly is cheaper, and by 2025
it was being exercised openly.

## Rosewood

**Menlo Park, California.**

The Rosewood Sand Hill bills itself as "the modern clubhouse for Silicon Valley… directly on Sand Hill Road… on land owned by Stanford." (Baker 2026, ch. 6, p. 120) <!-- bib: baker-how-to-rule -->

Seventy years after Kleiner and Perkins signed the first lease, the university was still collecting rent on the view. Deal makers ate lunch where venture partners had learned to say no. The land bank outlasted every company on the cap table.

The freshman reporter and the hotel on the hill were the same story told at different scales: who verifies, and who owns the ground under the verification.
