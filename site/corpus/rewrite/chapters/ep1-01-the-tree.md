# Episode 1: The Ledger

## The Tree

November 1769. The Santa Clara Valley, Alta California.

The fog had burned off by midday, and the plain ran empty to the hills. Gaspar de Portolá’s soldiers made camp on the bank of a creek whose name they had not yet fixed in writing. Above them stood a redwood unlike any tree the diarists had seen on the march north from San Diego: one trunk split into two, rising like a tall stick against the sky. Someone in the party would call it *el palo alto*—the high tree—and the name would outlive the empire that sent them here.

Father Juan Crespí, a Franciscan priest traveling with the expedition, kept the kind of journal Madrid expected from a reconnaissance: distances, water, fuel, the disposition of the land for settlement or defense. His entries for the November halt describe the double redwood and the camp on the creek in the expedition's usual administrative Spanish; later editors and local historians tie that halt to the tree that still stands beside San Francisquito Creek, on ground that will later be a university town named for the stick itself (Harris 2023, intro).

Portolá had not come to plant a colony. Spain had no surplus population to send. José de Gálvez, inspector general of New Spain, had ordered the push north after expelling the Jesuits in 1767—a Bourbon administrator’s solution to a strategic problem: Russians moving down the Pacific coast, British ships in the north Pacific, and a Spanish claim on paper that had never been occupied in force (Harris 2023, intro; repo ch. 2-01).

The expedition was the first human instrument of that policy on the ground. Portolá commanded the soldiers. Junípero Serra, already aging, would found missions behind the military line. Crespí wrote the country into existence for readers who would never see it.

What they saw was useful. The valley floor offered grass and water. The creek gave a line on a map. The double redwood gave a landmark visible from a distance—a natural surveyor’s mark before anyone in California owned a surveyor’s chain. In a territory where possession would always be argued with reference to trees, bends in water, and hand-drawn sketches, a tree like this was already a kind of deed.

They did not stay. The expedition moved on toward Monterey and the Bay, searching for the harbor Spain’s charts promised. They would stumble onto San Francisco Bay from the land side and fail, at first, to recognize what they had found. But they had paused long enough for ink to fix this place in the record, and the record is the subject of this episode.

The redwood still stands, fenced and braced, on the edge of a campus where the street signs carry the names of men who ordered missions built and grants signed. Twelve feet up and lit at night, the signs are easy not to read. The tree is harder to ignore. It was never a secret. It was only subliminal.

Portolá’s men broke camp and marched away. The Ohlone people whose villages lay in the valley did not appear in Crespí’s pages as negotiators or proprietors. They appear, when they appear at all, as part of the landscape being assessed—potential neophytes for a system not yet built here, labor for missions still on paper. That omission is also a record. Whoever keeps the journal decides who counts as a person with a name worth preserving.

Within eight years Franciscans would found Mission Santa Clara de Asís on this plain, close enough that the tree would mark the edge of a world remade by registers and rations (timeline; repo ch. 2-01). The creek would become a boundary in grants and diseños. The tall stick would become a postal address, then a railroad stop, then a university seal.

But in November 1769 none of that exists. There is only fog lifting, soldiers cooking, a priest writing, and a tree that is already doing work no one has yet paid for—holding a line on the ground that strangers will still be fighting over two centuries later.

The expedition left the valley. The diary remained.

## A system, not a settlement

Spain came north from Baja California in 1769 and did not send settlers, because
it did not have any to send. What it sent instead was a system with three parts.

The presidio supplied force: a garrison, usually small, frequently unpaid, and
chronically short of everything. The pueblo supplied provisions, in theory,
though the civilian towns established at San José and Los Angeles remained tiny
for decades. And the mission supplied labour and record, which made it the
component that actually did the work.

Twenty-one missions were eventually founded along the coast, linked by the road
that came to be called El Camino Real. Each was an agricultural enterprise —
grain, cattle, hides, tallow, wine — run by two or three Franciscan priests
using Indigenous labour, and each maintained registers of baptisms, marriages,
and burials that in many cases survive.

The strategic purpose was never conversion alone. Spain was in California
because Russian fur traders were moving down the Pacific coast and British ships
were in the north Pacific, and the empire had a claim it had not occupied.
Occupation required people, people required an economy, and the fastest
available route to both was to convert the existing population into a resident
agricultural workforce. The theological project and the imperial one arrived on
the same ships and were not, at the level of policy, easily separable.

## 320 Dolores Street

Mission San Francisco de Asís was founded in June 1776, a few days before a
document was signed on the other coast asserting that governments derive their
just powers from the consent of the governed.

The building is still there, at 320 Dolores Street. It is the oldest intact
structure in San Francisco. It survived the earthquake of 1906 when almost
nothing around it did, and it is worth walking into, because the scale is
instructive: it is small, thick-walled, and plain, and it looks exactly like
what it was, which is an administrative outpost with a chapel in it.

The Presidio was established a few weeks later at the tip of the peninsula,
guarding the entrance to the bay. Force and record, sited within a few miles of
each other, and between them the whole apparatus of Spanish California.

What Spain was actually claiming was the harbour. The Golden Gate is a narrow
entrance to one of the largest protected anchorages in the world, and the empire
that held it would control access to everything behind it. The mission and the
presidio are instrumentation around a passage, and the passage is the
reason either of them exists.

## Legibility as power

Consider what the register does that no other instrument in eighteenth-century
California could do.

It creates a population. Before the register there are people living in a
territory in kinship groups the Spanish did not understand and did not try to;
after it, there is a numbered, named, located, and countable body of persons
with recorded obligations. The register makes the population an object that can
be administered by someone who has never met any of them.

This is the same operation the Roman census performed and the same operation the
Linear B tablets performed at Pylos, and it will be the same operation performed
by a railroad's freight schedules, a university's admissions files, and a
platform's user database. Whoever holds the register decides who exists in the
official sense, and the official sense is the only one that generates
consequences.

The Franciscans were not doing this cynically. They believed the enterprise was
salvific and many of them worked themselves into the ground on it. That is
precisely why the chapter belongs where it does: the method does not require
bad faith, and it operates identically whether the people running it are venal
or sincere.

## What the labour actually was

The mission economy ran on Indigenous labour that was not free to leave.

Once baptised, a neophyte was considered subject to mission discipline.
Departure without permission was treated as flight, and soldiers were sent to
retrieve those who left. Work was assigned, rations were distributed, and
punishment for infractions was corporal and administered by the priests. Housing
was communal and, for unmarried women and girls, locked at night.

Contemporary observers noticed. Visitors from other European powers, including
French and Russian expeditions with no particular sympathy for Indigenous
Californians, recorded the arrangement in terms ranging from approving to
appalled. The Franciscans defended it in their own correspondence, which means
the terms of the debate are preserved and the arrangement is not in dispute.

The demographic outcome is the central fact of the period. Scholarly estimates
of the Indigenous population of California before contact vary substantially —
the most cited reconstructions put it somewhere in the range of three hundred
thousand, with a wide margin — and the population within the mission districts
fell catastrophically over the six decades of the mission system, principally
through introduced disease compounded by concentrated living conditions,
malnutrition, and disrupted subsistence. The figures differ by source and by
method, and the disagreement is worth naming rather than resolving with a single
round number.

Death rates inside the missions exceeded birth rates for most of the period,
which meant the system required continuous recruitment simply to maintain its
workforce. That is not an incidental cruelty. It is a structural property of the
arrangement, and it is visible in the missions' own registers, because the
missions counted burials too.

## The archive against the archivist

Which produces the strangest feature of this chapter's evidence.

Almost everything known in detail about Indigenous experience in the mission
period comes from records kept by the institution responsible. The registers,
the annual reports, the correspondence with superiors, the responses to official
questionnaires: all of it produced by Franciscans, for Franciscan and royal
purposes.

And it documents the resistance anyway. It records flight, and the expeditions
sent to recover the people who fled. It records revolts — at San Diego in 1775,
at Santa Barbara and elsewhere in 1824 — with names, causes, and outcomes. It
records the burials. An institution that counts everything ends up counting the
evidence against itself, which is a recurring piece of good fortune for the
historian and a recurring problem for the institution.

The registers are still consulted. Descendant communities, including the Muwekma
Ohlone, use them to establish lineage, which means a set of documents created to
convert people into an administrable population is now used by their descendants
to demonstrate that they were there. The instrument has outlived its purpose and
been turned around.

## Who the claim was against

## The rivals who never came

It is worth asking whether the strategy was necessary, because the threat Spain
was defending against never fully materialised and the defence nonetheless
determined the shape of California.

The Russian-American Company established Fort Ross on the coast north of the Bay
in 1812, roughly sixty miles from the Presidio, and farmed and hunted there for
three decades. It was a commercial outpost rather than an invasion, and it was
sold in 1841 to John Sutter — the same Sutter on whose land the gold would be
found seven years later, which is the kind of connection this book will keep
encountering and should not overstate.

The British were a more serious prospect. The Hudson's Bay Company operated in
the Pacific Northwest and maintained an agent in San Francisco from the early
1840s, and British naval interest in California was real enough that American
officials worried about it continuously in the years before 1846.

Neither took the territory. But the anticipation of them is why the missions
were founded, why the Presidio was garrisoned, and why Spain held a harbour it
never developed. The chapter's point is that the claim was the asset, and a
claim is maintained against possibilities rather than against events. Fifty
years of occupation, twenty-one missions, and a demographic catastrophe were the
price of foreclosing something that did not happen.

When the United States finally took California it did so from Mexico, and what
it acquired was the Spanish claim with two changes of ownership recorded on it.

## Who this cost

The peoples of the Bay shoreline had their territory designated a strategic
asset of the Spanish crown in documents they never saw, in a language they did
not read, on the basis of a legal theory — that European sovereigns could
allocate non-Christian lands among themselves — that had been developed several
centuries earlier and several thousand miles away.

And the garrison was itself expendable. Soldiers at the Presidio were unpaid for
long periods, supplied irregularly, and unable to leave. They were the
instrument of the empire in California, not beneficiaries of it, and their
complaints survive in the same correspondence that documents everything else.

By 1821 Spain has held the best harbour on the Pacific coast for forty-five
years using a garrison, a chain of missions, and a filing system, and has
developed essentially none of it. That was not a failure of the strategy. It was
the strategy, and the claim it established is what Mexico inherits, and what the
United States eventually takes.
