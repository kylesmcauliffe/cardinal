# Off the Land

<!-- The note was folded in from legacy chapter 4-08 on Oct 31, 2026. -->

This book follows one piece of land. The other industry California built in the twentieth century grew at the far end of the state and never touched the Farm, but it was organized the same way, and it is worth a page.

In December 1908 the leading American film companies, Thomas Edison's among them, pooled sixteen motion picture patents in the Motion Picture Patents Company, and Eastman Kodak agreed to sell raw film only to its members. Independent producers who would not take a license went west, many of them to Los Angeles. It was far from the trust's New Jersey base, the federal appeals court that covered California had been reluctant to enforce patent claims, and the weather allowed filming all year. On October 1, 1915, a federal court in Philadelphia held that the trust was an illegal restraint of trade, and after its appeal was dismissed the trust was formally ended in 1918.

Within a generation the business the independents built had its own concentration. By 1945 the five largest studios had interests in 3,137 of the country's 18,076 theaters, and those theaters paid 45 percent of the domestic film rental collected by the eight companies in the case. In the 92 cities with more than 100,000 people, at least 70 percent of the first-run houses were tied to one of the five. On May 3, 1948, in United States v. Paramount Pictures, the Supreme Court upheld findings of price-fixing and block booking and sent the question of the theaters back to the trial court. In the decrees that followed, the studios with theater chains had to sell them to new, independent companies. The decrees stood until August 7, 2020.

The studios also held people. Olivia de Havilland's contract with Warner Bros. began on May 5, 1936. When she refused roles, the studio suspended her without pay and added the suspensions to the end of her term, about twenty-five weeks in all. She sued in August 1943, and on December 8, 1944, the California Court of Appeal held that the state's seven-year limit on personal service contracts meant seven calendar years. Her contract was over.
<!-- Sources: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2d/67/225.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Law -->

The trust had owned patents and the studios had owned theaters, and in both cases a court could name the thing that was owned.
