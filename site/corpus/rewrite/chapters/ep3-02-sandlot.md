# Episode 3: The Octopus

## Sandlot

July 1877. San Francisco. The fog held until noon, and then the sun came through the unfinished masonry of the new city hall like a taunt.

Men gathered on a vacant lot beside the building, in what the newspapers called sandlot meetings because the ground was still raw and the city was still being invented. They were unemployed, or underemployed, or employed at wages that did not meet the rent. The transcontinental line had released thousands of construction workers into a labor market at the same moment it exposed California manufacturers to eastern competition. The Comstock silver boom had collapsed. Drought had killed cattle in the interior. The depression that began on the eastern seaboard in 1873 had arrived on the Pacific with a delay that did not soften it.

Denis Kearney stood on a box or a barrel and spoke. He was a drayman and petty entrepreneur, in Richard White's phrase, who had turned anti-Chinese politics into an insurgency by the late 1870s (White 2011, ch. 7). He ended his speeches with a line the crowd could carry home: the Chinese must go.

<!-- kyle: ch/2-11 --> Nob Hill was visible from where he was standing. The mansions of Stanford, Crocker, Hopkins, and Huntington were up there, built with the proceeds described in the previous episode, and Crocker had constructed a forty-foot fence around a neighbor's house on three sides after the man refused to sell — the Spite Fence, which became a standing local grievance and a perfect symbol available to anyone who wanted one.

Nob Hill rose above them. The mansions of Stanford, Crocker, Hopkins, and Huntington caught the afternoon light. Crocker's Spite Fence was visible from parts of the city, a forty-foot wall around a neighbor who would not sell. The railroad's owners had built with the proceeds described in the previous episode. They were not hidden. They were elevated.

### Take the grievance seriously

<!-- kyle: ch/2-11 --> This chapter fails if the men on the sandlots are caricatured, so it starts with what was actually happening to them.

This chapter fails if the men on the sandlots are caricatured.

The 1870s were bad in California. <!-- kyle: ch/2-11 --> The completion of the transcontinental line had released thousands of construction workers into the labor market at the same moment it exposed California manufacturers to eastern competition for the first time. The Comstock silver bonanza collapsed. Drought killed the cattle. The national depression that began in 1873 arrived, and unemployment in San Francisco was severe and sustained.

The Southern Pacific was, on the merits, an excellent target. Its rates were extractive. Its land holdings were enormous. Its political operation was notorious. It had paid the men who built its line a differential it never made up. A movement organized against the railroad in the 1870s would have had ample material and a genuine constituency.

### What was built instead

Anti-Chinese violence was extensive and specific. <!-- kyle: ch/2-11 --> There had been a massacre in Los Angeles in 1871 in which a mob killed some seventeen or eighteen Chinese residents, one of the largest mass lynchings in American history. In San Francisco in July 1877 crowds attacked Chinese laundries and businesses over several nights. Chinese communities were driven out of towns across California and the wider West over the following decade, in Eureka, in Tacoma, in Seattle, and at Rock Springs in Wyoming in 1885, where at least twenty-eight Chinese miners were killed. These were not disturbances. They were expulsions, and they were frequently organised by people who announced them in advance.

In July 1877 the city did not sleep easy. Crowds moved through streets where laundry steam rose from basement doors. Shopkeepers who could not bar their shutters fast enough lost windows, then stock, then the week's receipts. The police answered where they could. The papers counted damage and arrests and moved on. Chinatown, centered on Dupont Street and Portsmouth Square, held its own institutions against the pressure: associations, temples, newspapers, attorneys on retainer. The violence was aimed at people who could not vote and could not naturalize. It was also aimed at a distraction from the hill.

### At Stanford's door

During the crisis, workers marched up to Leland Stanford's mansion and threatened him (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2). Kearney said, "I will give the Central Pacific just three months to discharge their Chinamen" (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2).

The mob was at the door of a man who would soon campaign for the United States Senate as a friend of labor and a defender of California's workingmen.

Stanford had been governor. He had been one of the merchants who understood that the money was in the passage, not the gold. His fortune was tied to a company that had been the largest employer of Chinese labor in the state. The contradiction did not resolve itself on the steps of Nob Hill. It moved into slogans, ballots, and federal law.

Up the hill, the Spite Fence still walled a neighbor in. Down below, men who had laid track in the snow listened to a drayman tell them whom to blame. The railroad's directors were Kearney's actual subject. They went unmolested that summer in any way that touched rates or land titles.

### What the party won

Most of these were struck down in federal court, which is itself part of the story.

The Workingmen's Party won substantial representation at California's 1879 constitutional convention and shaped the resulting document. Provisions targeted Chinese residents directly: prohibitions on employment by corporations and public bodies, authorizations for municipalities to remove Chinese residents from city limits. Most of these were struck down in federal court, which is itself part of the story. The convention also created a railroad commission empowered to set rates. Within a few years that commission would function as an instrument of the company it was created to regulate.

The party burned out within a few years. The energy it generated did not disappear. It narrowed.

Kearney himself was arrested more than once for incitement. He served time. He left the country briefly. He returned to a city that had already written exclusion into its constitution and was waiting on Washington to make it federal. The sandlots emptied. The slogan did not.

### Summer nights

July 1877 did not confine anger to speeches.

Crowds moved when the fog burned off. They broke glass where Chinese families kept shops. They chased men who could not appeal to a jury that would believe them against a white accuser. The police answered in patches. The newspapers counted damage and moved on to the next edition. Dupont Street held because concentration was also defense: associations that could post bail, attorneys who could file habeas corpus, elders who knew which doors to bar.

Kearney's rhetoric gave the violence a sentence men could repeat. It did not invent the violence. Los Angeles in 1871 had already shown what a mob could do when it decided a neighborhood was disposable. The sandlots added unemployment and a visible hill.

Stanford's mansion sat in the row with Crocker's fence. Workers marched to that door and threatened the man inside (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2). They did not march with equal force on Huntington's Washington office or on the Sacramento bureau that drafted bills. The geography of grievance stopped where the cable car stopped climbing.

### Eight hours and convict labor

The Workingmen's program was not only exclusion. They wanted an eight-hour day. They wanted an end to competition from convict labor. They wanted the railroad and the banks regulated. Those demands required confronting entities with attorneys on retainer and commissioners already captured.

Exclusion required confronting people who could not vote. The party chose the easier fight. Historians who caricature the sandlots miss the real unemployment. Historians who romanticize the sandlots miss the expulsions. Both were true at once.

### Constitutional summer

The 1879 convention met in Sacramento while memory of the July riots was still fresh. Delegates who owed their seats to Workingmen energy wrote Chinese exclusion into the document wherever they could. They also wrote a railroad commission into being, answering a decade of rate grievance with a state agency.

The commission would fail its founders on the merits. The exclusion language would fail in federal court. The combination is the decade in miniature: one kind of control resisted in court, another kind absorbed into it.

### The ledger, early

Exclusion was not yet federal in 1877. It was already visible on the sandlots.

Railroad rates: unchanged by the march on Stanford's mansion. Land concentration: unchanged. The men on the hill looked down on a city learning to chant a sentence that pointed away from them.

In the desert, one of the four Associates was dying in a private car on a siding. In the valley, patents and price notices were moving toward a wheat field. The next death among the partners would not be in San Francisco. It would be in Yuma, with a telegram to a brother in New York.

The toll had not yet become federal law. It was already becoming habit.

### Crocker's symbol

The Spite Fence was not a metaphor. It was lumber and height.

Crocker had wanted a lot. The owner refused. Crocker built forty feet of wall on three sides and left the man looking at boards where a view had been. San Francisco gossiped. Cartoons drew it. Kearney could point uphill without naming a partner. The fence said what rates said in wood: the railroad's men could wait you out.

Stanford's mansion did not need a fence. His power sat in stock, in senators, in the largest Chinese payroll in the state. When workers threatened him in 1877, they threatened the visible politician, not the invisible rate clerk.

### White's drayman

Richard White calls Kearney a San Francisco drayman and petty entrepreneur who turned anti-Chinese politics into an insurgency in the late 1870s (White 2011, ch. 7). The phrase matters because it denies genius. Kearney was not a theorist. He was a man with a wagon and a microphone made of box and barrel who read the crowd's appetite and fed it.

Insurgency does not require a manifesto. It requires a sentence repeated until newspapers quote it without attribution.

### Comstock and drought

The 1870s squeezed California from more than one direction.

The Comstock silver bonanza had collapsed, taking jobs and swagger with it. Drought had killed cattle in the interior, breaking ranch payrolls that had fed towns along the line. Eastern competition arrived on the same rails that had promised prosperity when the spike was driven. Men who had swung hammers in the mountains stood on raw lots beside half-built city hall and listened to a man tell them the Chinese must go while Nob Hill glittered in afternoon sun.

Take the grievance seriously. Then name what the grievance did not touch.

### Toward 1879

The sandlots were not a permanent institution. They were a pressure valve.

When delegates met to rewrite the state constitution, many carried sandlot credentials. They wrote railroad regulation into the document because monopoly was real. They wrote Chinese exclusion into the document because monopoly was hard to hit. Federal courts would strike much of the exclusion language. The railroad commission would cozy up to the company it was meant to restrain. The party would burn out. The sentence would remain.

Kearney's three-month ultimatum to the Central Pacific passed without discharge of Chinese crews on the terms he had named (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2). The railroad kept its labor force and its differential. The mob had threatened Stanford. It had not rewritten the wage ledger.

Episode 3's next death among the famous would be quiet: a bookkeeper in a private car. The loudest deaths were still ahead in a wheat field and in a Congress that would close a border.

### Torchlight and city hall

The vacant lot beside the half-built city hall was not a ballroom. It was dirt, scrap lumber, and men who had nowhere else to be at the hour the meeting was called. Torches came out when speeches ran late. The unfinished masonry framed the crowd like a ruin that was also a promise: San Francisco still building itself while men inside it went hungry.

Kearney's gift was to make the hill visible in every sentence even when the sentence named someone else. The Chinese must go. The Central Pacific must discharge. The banks must answer. Only one of those targets could not answer with a senator.

When workers marched on Stanford's mansion, they climbed toward cable-car country (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2). They did not stay for tea. They threatened a man whose company had employed the workers they now demanded discharged within three months (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2). The ultimatum expired. The payroll did not empty on Kearney's schedule.

That failure mattered. It showed where sandlot power stopped and corporate power continued. The next episodes in this book will show the same stop line in court reporters' notes and in Kentucky incorporation filings. In July 1877 it was visible as a door on Nob Hill that closed without changing a rate.
