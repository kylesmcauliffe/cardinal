# Episode 5: The Engineer's Century

## Earthquake

**Stanford campus. 18 April 1906. A little after five in the morning.**

The ground heaved once and kept moving. Bricks came down in the Memorial Church before anyone could get outside. The new library and gymnasium, nearly finished, collapsed into rubble. The memorial arch at the entrance to the quad fell. Two people were killed on campus. Dust hung in the oaks.

Thirty miles north, San Francisco was burning. The fire would run for three days along streets the railroad had made rich.

On the farm the university had been built on, the spire that marked Jane Stanford's memorial lay in pieces on the grass.

## What broke

At a little after five in the morning on 18 April 1906, the San Andreas Fault ruptured a few miles from the campus. Stanford was badly damaged. The new library and gymnasium, nearly finished, were destroyed. The memorial arch came down. The church's spire collapsed and its façade was wrecked. Two people were killed on campus. The physical loss was estimated in the millions of dollars at a time when that was a very large sum, and much of it was uninsured (Harris 2023, ch. 2.1).

Students and faculty who had been asleep in Encina Hall and the scattered wooden cottages stumbled into the quad in nightclothes. The water mains were broken. Telegraph lines were down. For hours the only information came from people on horseback and the smoke column visible to the north.

The university closed for the remainder of the academic year and reopened in the autumn. It rebuilt, though some structures were never replaced and the church was not fully restored for years. It did not seek to relocate, did not reduce its programme permanently, and did not, so far as the record shows, seriously consider whether it should continue.

## What did not break

The reason it could absorb this is the reason it had absorbed the previous shocks. Its assets were land and an endowment, not buildings; its governance was a board that did not require anyone's permission to act; and its income did not depend on any particular structure standing up. An institution built to be indifferent to the death of its founders turned out to be substantially indifferent to the destruction of its campus.

Both founders were now dead. Leland Stanford had died in a financial panic with the federal government suing his estate. Jane Stanford had died in Honolulu in circumstances the coroner's jury named as poisoning and the university's president named as heart failure; the chemists had found strychnine in her body and in the bicarbonate she had taken (White 2022, 208). The mausoleum stood on the hill above the wrecked church.

The university did not close. Governance did not change. The self-perpetuating board carried on, filling its own vacancies as the Founding Grant provided. The endowment, released from probate seven years earlier, continued to appreciate. David Starr Jordan remained president until 1913. Enrolment grew.

## Jordan in the rubble

Jordan was fifty years old in 1906. He had spent seventeen years building a coeducational western university into a national presence, and he had done it while publishing on fish taxonomy, peace, conservation, and heredity with the same pen.

In the days after the earthquake he was everywhere on campus — assigning labour crews, negotiating with insurers, writing donors, reassuring parents. He was good at institutions in crisis. He had silenced faculty during the Ross affair. He would sail for Hawaii when Jane Stanford's death threatened to become a scandal the university could not control (Miller 2018; White 2022).

The spire in the grass was a photograph the country would see in newspapers. The institution that owned the photograph was already arranging who would interpret what it meant.

## The city and the farm

San Francisco's fire destroyed the commercial centre of the West Coast and displaced a quarter of a million people. Tent camps appeared in Golden Gate Park. Refugees moved down the Peninsula on the same railroad right-of-way Leland Stanford had helped build.

Stanford's land holdings — thousands of acres under the Founding Grant — did not burn. They appreciated as the region rebuilt. The clause that forbade selling the land, written by a grieving father to protect a memorial, meant the university sat on an asset that could not be liquidated and therefore could not be spent down in a panic. It could only be held, leased, or developed.

In 1906 nobody on campus was drafting industrial park leases. The idea that the rubble would become the world's most valuable research real estate was not yet thinkable. What was thinkable was reopening in the autumn, which is what happened.

## Hoover elsewhere

Herbert Hoover was not on the farm that morning. He was in London, managing mines and partnerships, building the reputation that would put him in charge of Belgian relief eight years later.

He had left Stanford in 1895 with a geology degree and no money. The earthquake did not touch his balance sheets. It touched the institution that had credentialed him and that would later name a tower after him and house his archives.

When he returned to California as a public man, the campus he remembered would be a mix of old sandstone and new construction, the church without its spire, the quad full of students who had no memory of the founders alive.

## Lewis Terman and the test

In 1906 Lewis Terman was still teaching high school in San Bernardino County, not yet on the Stanford faculty. The Stanford-Binet revision was a few years away. The army would test more than a million men with group intelligence instruments during the Great War, and Terman would help build them.

The earthquake did not slow that project. If anything, the war and the mobilisation that followed made sorting seem urgent — who could be trained quickly, who should be officer material, which immigrants resembled which scores.

The church spire fell in April. The test sheets would ship to training camps by the tens of thousands before the decade was out.

## Reopening

Classes resumed in the autumn of 1906 in whatever rooms could be made habitable. Jordan presided over commencement in a campus that still showed scars. Donors gave for reconstruction. Faculty recruited as before.

The Memorial Church would be rebuilt without fully restoring the spire for years. The image of the fallen tower became part of the university's story about resilience — a private institution that endured what destroyed its neighbour city because it was built on land and paper, not on wooden blocks south of Market Street.

The students walking to geology labs in 1907 included Lou Henry's cohort and the men who would manage extraction economies around the world. The students walking to psychology offices a decade later would be measured on Terman's scales.

The ground had shaken. The board still met. The land still could not be sold.

That prohibition, treated for decades as a nuisance, would outlast every building destroyed in April 1906. It is still in the grant. The rubble was temporary. The clause was not.

## San Francisco burns

San Francisco's fire destroyed the commercial centre of the West Coast and displaced a quarter of a million people. Tent camps appeared in Golden Gate Park. Refugees moved down the Peninsula on the same railroad right-of-way Leland Stanford had helped build.

The university's response was not to ask whether the project remained viable. It was to rebuild and reopen. Jordan wrote donors, reassured parents, and kept enrolment growing. The institution behaved as if the founders' deaths and the city's ruin were weather events — terrible, expensive, and not decisive.

## The church

The Memorial Church spire collapsed into the quad. The façade was wrecked. Two people died on campus. The image traveled in newspapers as a symbol of shock. The board treated it as a repair estimate.

The church would be rebuilt without fully restoring the spire for years. Jane Stanford's memorial survived in damaged form, as the institution survived. Indifference to founders' deaths had been practice since 1884. Indifference to fallen stone was easier.

## Tests and rubble

In 1906 Lewis Terman still taught high school outside the main campus orbit. The Stanford-Binet revision was a decade away. When it came, it would sort the generation that rebuilt the campus and fought the Great War.

The earthquake did not slow that project. If anything, war and mobilisation made sorting seem urgent. The spire fell in April. The test sheets would ship to training camps by the tens of thousands before the decade was out.

## Hoover's distance

Hoover felt the earthquake as news from home, not as rubble under his boots. He was building the career that would make him indispensable to London and Washington. The farm that had admitted him conditionally would still be there when he returned as trustee and ex-president, its land clause intact beneath whatever architecture replaced the fallen arch.

## Forward without naming it

The land clause in the Founding Grant forbade sale. In 1906 that clause looked like a grieving father's dead hand. Sixty years later it would produce long leases on Page Mill Road. The earthquake cracked stone. The clause did not.

Jordan remained president until 1913. Lewis Terman would publish the Stanford-Binet revision in 1916. Frederick Terman was a boy on campus when the spire fell. The sorting and the leasing belonged to the same institution that reopened in the autumn as if the ground had merely shifted beneath it.

## What the photograph showed

Newspapers carried the image of the fallen spire beside accounts of San Francisco's fire. The university's survival read as resilience. The board's minutes read as insurance claims and donor letters. The Founding Grant's land clause did not appear in the captions. It would matter more than the spire.

## Enrolment after

Enrolment grew after the earthquake. The programme did not permanently shrink. The self-perpetuating board filled its own vacancies. The endowment appreciated. The institution behaved as if founders' deaths and fallen stone were weather — terrible, expensive, not decisive.

Hoover in London read the news and kept mining. Terman's father had not yet published the test. Jordan had not yet sailed for Honolulu on Jane Stanford's death. 1906 is a local disaster and a proof of institutional form: land and paper outlast brick.
