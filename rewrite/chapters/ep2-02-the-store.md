# Episode 2: The Toll

## The Store

1852–1856. Sacramento.

The scale of the shock

San Francisco had somewhere under a thousand residents at the beginning of 1848.
Within two years it had more than twenty-five thousand, and California's non-
Indigenous population went from a few thousand to well over two hundred thousand
within four years. Ships arriving in the bay were abandoned by their crews, who
left for the diggings; at one point several hundred vessels sat derelict in the
harbour. Some were broken up for timber. Some were run aground deliberately,
filled in around, and built on — there are ships under the financial district,
found periodically during excavation.

Prices behaved accordingly. Eggs, flour, lumber, and labour reached levels that
correspondents wrote home about in disbelief. A city was being built by people
who had no intention of staying, using materials that had to come around Cape
Horn.

## Who captured the surplus

The arithmetic of mining is the part the mythology omits.

Placer gold — the loose material in streambeds that a man with a pan could
recover — was accessible for a few years and then substantially exhausted.
During that window the average return was, by most reconstructions, roughly
comparable to wages available elsewhere, with enormous variance: a small number
of men did extremely well, most made an ordinary living for hard work in bad
conditions, and many lost money on the cost of getting there. After the placers
played out, mining required capital — hydraulic operations, stamp mills, and
eventually deep shafts — and became an industry employing wage labour rather
than an opportunity for individuals.

The reliable money was in supply. Merchants selling equipment and provisions.
Shippers controlling the passage from the eastern United States. Landlords in
San Francisco, where a lot on the waterfront appreciated faster than any claim
in the Sierra. Bankers, who held gold dust, issued notes against it, and
financed the merchants. Levi Strauss arrived in 1853 and sold dry goods; the
trousers came later and the business was a wholesale supply house long before it
was a brand.

Every one of these positions is a passage. The harbour was the only realistic
way into northern California, so everything and everybody passed through San
Francisco, and whoever owned the ground they passed over collected.

The extractive story — the miner, the pan, the strike — obscures the
infrastructural reality, and the obscuring is part of the method. A myth of
luck explains the winners as fortunate rather than as positioned, which is a
considerably more comfortable account for the winners.

## The state joins in

The Foreign Miners' Tax of 1850 imposed a monthly licence fee on miners who were
not United States citizens. It was aimed principally at Mexican, Chilean, and
Peruvian miners, many of whom had arrived early and knew more about placer
mining than the Americans did. It was widely enforced by threat, produced
substantial revenue for the state, and was repealed after the foreign miners
largely left, which had been the object.

A second version in 1852 was aimed at the Chinese and was more durable,
eventually supplying a significant share of state revenue. It is the first
appearance in California of a manoeuvre that this act will describe again in
chapter 11 and the story will describe again in the opening episodesV: extract from a group
defined as outsiders, in a way that a majority of voters will support, while the
actual concentration of wealth continues undisturbed.

## The catastrophe

The Gold Rush was, for the Indigenous population of California, the most
destructive event in its history, and this is not a matter of interpretation.

Miners moved into the foothills and the interior valleys in enormous numbers,
displacing communities, destroying fisheries with hydraulic sediment, and taking
land. The violence was organised. State and local authorities paid bounties and
funded militia expeditions against Indigenous communities, and the California
legislature appropriated money for the purpose; the federal government later
reimbursed a substantial portion of it. An 1850 state statute, titled an act for
the government and protection of Indians, permitted the arrest of Indians deemed
to be loitering and their assignment to labour, and permitted the indenture of
Indigenous children — a system of forced labour operating in a free state.

The Indigenous population of California declined by a very large proportion
during the 1850s and 1860s. Scholarly estimates of the pre-contact population
and of the decline vary considerably, and the range should be stated rather than
collapsed, but no serious account puts the collapse at less than catastrophic,
and California's first governor said publicly in 1851 that a war of
extermination would continue until the Indian race became extinct.

The eighteen treaties negotiated with California tribes in 1851 and 1852, which
would have reserved substantial land, were never ratified by the Senate. The
tribes had already ceded their claims.

## What California took away

California took away something in these years that it has never unlearned, and
Brannan is its clearest exponent.

The money is not in the commodity. The money is in the position between the
commodity and the person who wants it — the harbour, the store, the bank, the
only road in. Positions of that kind do not require luck, do not require skill
at the underlying activity, and do not exhaust when the placers do.

Twelve years later, four Sacramento merchants who had made ordinary money
selling supplies to miners applied the same insight to a railroad, and the
federal government paid for it.

## Four merchants

Leland Stanford sold groceries. Collis Huntington and Mark Hopkins ran a
hardware business together. Charles Crocker sold dry goods. All four were in
Sacramento, all four had made ordinary merchant money supplying the mining
economy, and none of them was an engineer.

They were, in other words, the shovel-sellers of chapter 6 operating at a
different scale, and they understood the same thing Brannan had understood: the
money is in the position, not the commodity.

Theodore Judah brought them the project, incorporated the Central Pacific with
them in 1861, secured the legislation described in the previous chapter, and
then fell out with them over how the company would be financed and controlled.
He was bought out and travelled east to raise money to buy them out in turn. He
crossed Panama, contracted yellow fever, and died in New York in November 1863
at thirty-seven.

The four divided the work by temperament. Huntington handled Washington and the
purchasing. Crocker ran construction. Hopkins kept the books. Stanford handled
California politics, which he did by becoming the governor.

## Both chairs

Stanford was elected Governor of California in 1861 and served from January 1862
to December 1863 — precisely the period in which the Pacific Railway Act passed,
the Central Pacific was organised, and California committed public support to
the enterprise.

He was simultaneously president of the Central Pacific Railroad.

As governor he advocated the railroad as a public necessity, which it arguably
was. He signed legislation providing state and county assistance to it. He
presided over the groundbreaking in Sacramento in January 1863 in both
capacities, and nobody at the ceremony appears to have regarded the arrangement
as remarkable.

There was no conflict-of-interest framework to violate. The roles were simply
held at the same time by the same man, and the disclosure was total: everyone
knew. This is worth dwelling on, because it is the cleanest illustration in the
book of a point the Voice Guide insists on. Nothing here is concealed. The
method does not depend on secrecy. It depends on a structural arrangement
being visible and unremarkable.

## The subsidy, and where it went

The public contribution was enormous. Land grants of alternate sections along
the right of way, doubled in 1864 to twenty sections per mile — which is to say
twenty square miles of land for every mile of track. Government bonds at six per
cent, issued per mile, graduated by terrain: sixteen thousand dollars a mile
across the flat, thirty-two thousand across the high plains, and forty-eight
thousand a mile in the mountains.

That last figure produced an immediate and characteristic manoeuvre. Where the
mountains began was a question of geology, and geology was a matter of expert
opinion, and the Central Pacific obtained an opinion placing the base of the
Sierra Nevada considerably further west than any geologist unconnected to the
company would have put it. Lincoln accepted it. The company collected the
mountain rate across some twenty-odd miles of what was, by any ordinary
description, valley floor.

The larger method was the construction company. The Central Pacific
contracted the building of its line to the Contract and Finance Company, which
was owned by the same four men. The railroad — a corporation with public
obligations, public credit, and eventually public shareholders — paid the
construction firm, which was private and theirs, at prices they set on both
sides of the transaction.

The profit was captured at the point of transfer. The railroad could show modest
returns while the men controlling it became extraordinarily rich, and the
accounts of the Contract and Finance Company were destroyed before a
congressional investigation could examine them.

Keep this manoeuvre in mind. Later, a university under pressure over its
military research will divest the research institute to itself, and the
structure of the transaction will be recognisable.

## The workforce

Crocker's construction superintendent, James Strobridge, opposed hiring Chinese
workers. Crocker overruled him — the labour supply was inadequate, white workers
left for the mines constantly, and a trial group performed well. From 1865 the
company recruited heavily, first in California and then directly from Guangdong.

They were paid less than white workers doing the same work, and unlike white
workers they were not provided with board, which they organised themselves. The
work was lethal. The Summit Tunnel was driven through granite by hand and with
black powder and nitroglycerine, at a rate measured in inches a day, through two
of the worst winters on record. Men were killed by explosions, rockfalls,
avalanches, and cold. The company did not systematically record their deaths,
which is why every figure offered is an estimate and why the estimates vary so
widely.

In June 1867 several thousand Chinese workers in the Sierra struck. Their
demands were an increase in pay to match white workers, a reduction in the
working day in the tunnels, and an end to the practice of whipping and of
restraining workers from leaving. It was, by any measure, one of the largest
labour actions in the United States to that date.

Crocker broke it in about a week by cutting off the food supply.

They went back to work. Two years later they finished the line, and on 28 April
1869 a Central Pacific crew — Chinese, with a handful of Irish rail handlers —
laid ten miles and fifty-six feet of track in a single day to win a bet with the
Union Pacific. It has never been beaten.

Thirteen years later the United States passed a law barring Chinese labourers
from entering the country. The next chapters describe how.

## What the monopoly then did

With the line complete, the Central Pacific and its successor the Southern
Pacific held the only rail connection between California and the east, and
proceeded to price it.

Rates were set not by cost but by what a shipper could bear — a practice the
company defended openly as charging what the traffic will bear. A grower in the
Central Valley had one way to reach a market. A town that cooperated got a
station; a town that did not got a line routed around it and ceased to exist.
The company acquired control of the shipping and the ferries as well, so that
alternatives to its rails led to its docks.

Whoever owns the rails sets the prices. The earlier chapters established the pattern and this
chapter supplies the American address.

Four men now held a fortune built on federal land, federal credit, and a
workforce paid a differential that was never made up. One of them was about to
convert his share of it into something that could not be regulated, taxed, or
broken up.
