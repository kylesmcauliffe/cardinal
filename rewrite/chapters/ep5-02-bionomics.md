# Episode 5: The Engineer's Century

## Bionomics

**Stanford. Autumn 1906.**

The test sheet lay on the desk in neat rows: vocabulary, analogies, arithmetic, a column for the examiner to enter a number at the end. Lewis Terman had been revising Alfred Binet's French instrument for American schools, and what he wanted from it was not what Binet had wanted. Binet had designed his scale to find children who needed help. Terman's Stanford revision, published in 1916, would produce a single score intended to represent a stable, largely inherited general capacity — something you could administer to a roomful of recruits and sort.

In 1906 the revision was still in progress. Terman was thirty years old, a psychologist on a campus whose first president had spent twenty years arguing that nations rose and fell on heredity. The sheet on the desk was an interface waiting for a nation that was about to ask for one.

## Jordan whole

David Starr Jordan was one of the most distinguished American scientists of his generation. He was an ichthyologist of the first rank who described a very large number of fish species and whose taxonomic work is still cited. He was president of Indiana University at thirty-four and Stanford's first president at thirty-nine. He was a serious conservationist with connections to the Sierra Club. He was, notably and at some cost to himself, a committed pacifist who campaigned against American entry into the First World War.

He was also the man who named the campus. Alvarado Row, and a long list of Spanish and Mexican officials still on the signs, were Jordan's choices: a taxonomist arranging the dead the way he arranged fish, so that students would walk through a classification of California power. The building that carried his own name was renamed in 2020. The streets he assigned were not.

He was also chairman of the eugenics committee of the American Breeders Association, an early and influential advocate of sterilisation legislation, and the author of works arguing that the quality of a nation is a matter of heredity and that social policy should be organised accordingly.

All of this is the same man, at the same time, and the record is more damning for refusing to separate the parts. Eugenics in the first decades of the twentieth century was not a fringe enthusiasm held by cranks. It was mainstream progressive science, held by university presidents, public health officials, conservationists, and social reformers, and it was attractive precisely to people who believed that expertise should govern.

## What the argument claimed

The structure of the eugenic argument matters more than its scientific content, which was wrong.

It held that socially significant human characteristics — intelligence, criminality, pauperism, alcoholism, what was then called feeblemindedness — were substantially hereditary. From that premise several conclusions followed. Social problems were biological rather than economic. Charity that allowed the unfit to reproduce made things worse. And the state had a legitimate interest in who reproduced.

Consider what that proposition does for an institution whose endowment came from a railroad monopoly.

It explains the distribution of wealth as a distribution of quality. If ability is hereditary and success tracks ability, then a class that has accumulated enormous advantages has done so because it is better, and its children will inherit both the money and the merit that justifies it. The argument converts a structural outcome into a natural one.

The university did not merely educate the people who would run California. It supplied the scientific account of why they should. That is made on the basis of the participants' own published work.

## The statute

The California statute of 1909 is written in the flat administrative prose of a public health measure. It authorised the medical superintendents of state institutions to perform an operation on inmates when, in their judgment, it would improve the person's physical, mental, or moral condition. It required no consent from the patient. It required no court order.

Over the following decades California sterilised more people under this authority than any other state in the union — by most reconstructions, something in the region of a third of the national total. Figures vary between institutional records and later studies, and the variation is itself part of the record: the people this was done to were not, in general, considered worth counting carefully.

The 1909 law was amended and broadened in 1913 and again in 1917. The operations were performed at state hospitals including Sonoma and Patton. The people subjected to them were overwhelmingly poor, disproportionately Mexican and Black, institutionalised and unable to consent.

Jordan did not sign every order. He supplied the vocabulary in which the orders made sense.

## Conservation and vigour

The link between conservation, national fitness, and racial anxiety is real, and it is frequently overstated, so the record states it precisely.

A strand of early twentieth-century American conservation was explicitly concerned with the physical and racial quality of the population. Wilderness was valued partly as a corrective to the supposed degeneration produced by urban industrial life, and several prominent conservationists were also prominent eugenicists. Madison Grant, a founder of major conservation organisations, wrote one of the most influential racist tracts of the period.

This does not mean conservation was a eugenic project. It means the two movements drew on an overlapping constituency and an overlapping vocabulary about national vigour, and that some of the same people were in both.

## Terman's instrument

Lewis Terman, professor of psychology at Stanford, published the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale in 1916 — an American revision of Binet's French test. Terman's version did something different with it. It produced a single number intended to represent a stable, largely inherited general capacity, and it was designed for use at scale.

It was used at scale almost immediately. During the First World War the army tested well over a million recruits using group instruments developed by Terman and his colleagues, and the results were published and widely discussed. The conclusions drawn from them about the relative capacities of national and racial groups were used in the immigration debates of the early 1920s, and the tests' cultural loading was obvious to critics at the time and was largely disregarded.

Terman was also an active eugenicist, which places him alongside Jordan in the same institutional line, and he began in 1921 the longitudinal study of gifted children that still bears his name and still runs.

A standardised, cheap, apparently objective measure of human capacity is precisely what a credentialling system needs in order to scale beyond the point where individual judgment is possible. The Scholastic Aptitude Test, first administered in 1926, descends directly from this work.

The instrument that would sort American students for the next century was developed by a professor of psychology at Stanford, and its author believed it was measuring something hereditary.

## The campus as screen

Stanford in the 1920s operated an explicit cap on the enrolment of women, fixed at five hundred by a decision of Jane Stanford's in 1899 and maintained in the form of a ratio for decades afterwards. A university that had been coeducational from its first day had, within a decade, limited the proportion of women it would admit.

Black students were present in very small numbers and were excluded from most campus housing and social organisations. Jewish students at elite American universities in this period were subject to quotas that were sometimes explicit and frequently effective without being written down.

And the university sat inside the residential geography of the Peninsula. Palo Alto and the surrounding towns operated restrictive covenants; whoever could not buy a house within reach could not easily take a job or a place within reach either.

The interface screened. That is what an interface does. The screening is the function rather than a defect in it.

## Two Termans

Lewis Terman ranked children from a laboratory. His son Frederick, born in 1900, would grow up on a campus where ranking was presented as science and where the state's most aggressive sterilisation law bore the imprint of the president's public arguments.

Frederick Terman studied electrical engineering at Stanford, taught there, and left for the Massachusetts Institute of Technology for graduate work. He came back to Palo Alto in the 1930s as the Depression deepened. The father had built the test. The son would build the industry that hired the people the test helped sort.

In 1906 the earthquake had knocked the Memorial Church spire into the quad and cracked the façade. The university rebuilt. Jordan remained president until 1913. Enrolment grew. The sorting science and the sorting campus grew with it.

The boy who would run Harvard's radar laboratory in the next war was eleven years old that autumn, watching his father normalize the idea that some people were fit to reproduce and others were not. The sheet on the desk was waiting for the army.

## What was done, and to whom

Individual cases survive in institutional files — names reduced to case numbers, diagnoses written in the language of the era, signatures of superintendents who believed they were performing public health. It authorized knives.

## Berkeley's parallel

Berkeley's parallel track in urban planning and restrictive covenants applied the science of sorting to a map. The university on the farm and the university across the Bay were both producing expertise that explained who should live where and who should not reproduce. The street names Jordan chose on campus — Alvarado, Lasuen, the Spanish officials — were a taxonomist's walk through conquest. Students still passed under them in 1906 when the spire fell.

## Terman in 1916

When the Stanford-Binet revision appeared in 1916, the United States was about to test more than a million soldiers. The scores would be debated in Congress during the immigration fights of the 1920s. Lewis Terman believed he was measuring heredity. The army believed it was selecting officers. Congress believed it was selecting citizens. The university supplied the number that made all three conversations seem scientific.

Frederick Terman was sixteen when the revision published. He would build microwave steeples and industrial parks. His father built the test that sorted the children who would attend them.

## The screen tightens

By the 1920s the campus cap on women, the housing exclusions, and the testing apparatus formed a single interface. You could be measured, admitted in limited numbers, credentialed, and still unable to live within reach of the jobs the credential unlocked. The earthquake cracked stone. The interface held.

## Graduate School of Business

The Graduate School of Business opened in 1925 among the earliest on the west coast. An undergraduate degree certifies general capability. A graduate business degree certifies fitness for management for people already in the working world — a second gate that re-sorts people who passed the first.

## Jordan until 1913

Jordan remained president until 1913 while sterilisation operations accumulated in state hospitals and Terman's tests moved toward the army. The institution educated the people who would run California and supplied the scientific account of why they should. The boy on campus in 1906 would run radar countermeasures in Cambridge and return to lease land his father's colleagues had sorted by score and statute.
