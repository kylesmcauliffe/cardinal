# Episode 5: The Engineer's Century

## Radio Lab

**Palo Alto. 1939.**

The garage behind the house at 367 Addison Avenue was small enough that two men could not pass each other without turning sideways. William Hewlett and David Packard had about five hundred dollars and a resistance-capacitance audio oscillator based on Hewlett's graduate work. Their professor had told them not to go east.

Frederick Terman stood in the driveway that year with the same argument he had been making for a decade: the American electronics industry lived on the Atlantic seaboard — RCA, General Electric, Bell Laboratories — and California kept exporting the people who could have built something here. Stay. Build here. He provided encouragement, a small loan, and access to university facilities.

The garage is now a California state landmark with a plaque calling it the birthplace of Silicon Valley. It is worth visiting for the deflation. Two engineers in a shed are a common occurrence and they usually produce nothing. What made this one consequential is everything that was already around it, and the first item on the list is that their professor had told them to stay.

## Terman's view

William Hewlett and David Packard were Stanford engineering graduates. In 1939 a young engineer with ambition went east, as almost all of Terman's best students had.

Terman told them not to. He had a view, which he had been developing for some years, that California could sustain a technical industry of its own, and that the reason it had not was that the region kept exporting its graduates. He provided encouragement, a small loan, and access to the university's facilities.

The point is not the loan. It is that a professor was functioning as an allocator of talent, deliberately, against the prevailing gradient, and that he understood this as a strategy rather than as mentorship.

Hewlett-Packard's first product was a resistance-capacitance audio oscillator, based on Hewlett's graduate work, and its first significant customer was Walt Disney, which bought eight of them for the sound system used with *Fantasia*. Within two years the customer base was substantially military, and that is the transition this chapter is about.

## The selection

Rearmament money began moving in the United States from around 1938, and it did not spread evenly. It went where three things already sat close together: engineering schools capable of producing and holding technical staff, laboratories with equipment and a research culture, and industrial capacity able to manufacture at scale.

Those criteria were applied by identifiable people. The National Defense Research Committee, established in 1940 under Vannevar Bush, and the Office of Scientific Research and Development that succeeded it in 1941, made decisions about which institutions received contracts. Bush was an MIT man. The largest wartime research contracts went to MIT, to Harvard, to Caltech, and to Bell Laboratories.

Stanford did not get them. In 1940 Stanford was a good regional university with a strong engineering department and no particular claim on the national scientific establishment. California was a large agricultural state with an aircraft industry in the south and a naval presence around the Bay. The proposition that the San Francisco Peninsula would become the centre of the world's technology industry would have struck an informed observer in 1940 as eccentric.

The outcome was not fated. It was selected, later, by people applying criteria, and it could have been selected otherwise. Boston very nearly kept it, and for a period after the war Route 128 was ahead.

## What California did have

The Pacific war made the west coast the theatre's logistical base. Shipyards at Richmond, Sausalito, and Vallejo. Aircraft manufacturing in southern California at a scale that reorganised the region's economy. Naval installations around the Bay, and Moffett Field in Sunnyvale, which had been built for airships in the 1930s and became an aeronautical research centre.

It also had a growing technical education base at Stanford, Berkeley, and Caltech, and a population that was about to increase very rapidly.

Readiness, not destiny. The distinction is the chapter's whole argument.

## Berkeley, and the neighbour

One institution did receive an enormous wartime commission, and it was not Stanford.

Ernest Lawrence's Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley had built cyclotrons through the 1930s on foundation money, and when the Manhattan Project needed to separate uranium isotopes at scale, Lawrence's electromagnetic method became one of the principal approaches. The laboratory expanded enormously. Los Alamos was directed by Berkeley's J. Robert Oppenheimer.

Berkeley demonstrates that California could host science at the highest level and that federal money would go there when the case was strong enough. It also demonstrates the model Terman would later copy: a laboratory whose scale depended on federal contracts, attached to a university, with a director who functioned as an entrepreneur.

Stanford watched this happen to its neighbour, and its dean of engineering spent the war somewhere else thinking about it.

## The father and the son

Lewis Terman had published the Stanford-Binet scale in 1916 and tested more than a million soldiers in the Great War. Frederick Terman built microwave laboratories and advised students in a rented garage. The father ranked children; the son ranked companies and careers.

In 1939 the son was still on campus, not yet running eight hundred people in Cambridge. The war would send him east. The students who stayed would build instruments the Navy would buy.

## The garage and what followed

The plaque on Addison Avenue tells a founding myth. The payroll records tell a procurement story. HP's early customers included Disney and then the federal government. Defence and aerospace purchasing would dominate the region's electronics firms for twenty-five years before the consumer market became visible.

Terman's advice in 1939 was the seed. The industrial park lease in 1951 would be the trellis. The war laboratory at Harvard would be the education that tied them together.

Two men in a garage. A professor who refused to let them leave. A federal selection process that had not yet chosen Palo Alto. The oscillator on the bench was already humming toward a military specification.

The chapter ends where the next one begins: not in Palo Alto, but on the East Coast, with Terman managing radar countermeasures and with families on the Peninsula being loaded onto trains.

## Richmond and the second migration

The Second World War produced the largest internal migration in American history. Something in the region of a million and a half people moved to California during the war years to work in shipyards, aircraft plants, and the supporting economy, and among them were several hundred thousand Black Americans leaving the South in the second Great Migration. Richmond's population multiplied several times over in four years around the Kaiser shipyards.

The industries that recruited them did so under federal pressure. Executive Order 8802, in 1941, prohibited discrimination in defence industries and established the Fair Employment Practice Committee, after A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington. It was the first federal action of its kind. It did not end housing segregation on the Peninsula, but it changed who could earn wages within driving distance of Stanford.

## Moffett Field

Moffett Field in Sunnyvale, built for airships in the 1930s, became an aeronautical research centre as the Pacific war made the Bay a logistical hub. Terman could see from campus that federal installations were reshaping the county even while research contracts went elsewhere.

## HP's turn toward the Pentagon

Disney's eight oscillators for *Fantasia* were a charming origin story. The payroll that followed was defense work — instruments sold into laboratories and contractors whose names would fill the industrial park Terman would lease. Hewlett and Packard stayed because their professor told them to. They stayed long enough to become the park's anchor tenant in 1956.

## February 1942

Executive Order 9066 arrived in the same season Terman's laboratory at Harvard scaled toward eight hundred staff. The juxtaposition is not metaphor. It is a calendar. Jamming experiments in Cambridge. Trains at Tanforan. The county's land market and labour market both changed in the year the war made Stanford's dean into a federal manager.

The garage on Addison Avenue still stood behind the house. The plaque was decades away. The federal selection process had not yet named Palo Alto the winner. Terman was learning in Massachusetts what he would spend twenty years selling in California: follow the money, concentrate, become indispensable, lease what you cannot sell.

## The oscillator and Fantasia

Hewlett-Packard's first product was a resistance-capacitance audio oscillator, based on Hewlett's graduate work. Walt Disney bought eight units for the sound system used with *Fantasia* — a civilian customer that makes a good story. Within two years the customer base was substantially military. That transition is the chapter's hinge: the garage is real, and so is the procurement office.

## Readiness, not destiny

Geography mattered once the Pacific war made the west coast the theatre's logistical base. Shipyards, aircraft plants, naval installations, Moffett Field — readiness, not destiny. Terman's advice to Hewlett and Packard in 1939 was a bet on that readiness before the bet paid off.

## The other filter

Federal money was not the only thing selecting for California. People were arriving, and the arrival was itself a selection. Among the migrants were Black workers leaving the South for Kaiser yards in Richmond. Executive Order 8802 prohibited discrimination in defence industries after Randolph threatened a march on Washington. Housing on the Peninsula remained segregated. The jobs were new. The map was old.

## Handoff

Two men in a garage. A professor who told them to stay. A war that would send him east. Families on the same calendar losing farms under Executive Order 9066. The chapter ends where the next begins: Cambridge, eight hundred staff, radar countermeasures, and a grievance that would become an industrial park.

## Crisis selects ecosystems

Rearmament from 1938 did not spread evenly. It went where engineering schools, laboratories, and manufacturing capacity sat close together — criteria applied by Vannevar Bush's National Defense Research Committee and the Office of Scientific Research and Development. Bush was an MIT man. Stanford watched contracts go east while shipyards boomed on the Bay without the university capturing the research share.

## Property and 1942

Japanese American incarceration belongs in this act because it happened here and was an asset transfer. Property sold at distress prices or lost during absence. Beneficiaries not required to account. The same story as mission registers and relief ledgers, running at speed under Executive Order 9066 while Terman prepared to run eight hundred staff in Cambridge.

## Handoff to Harvard

The Second World War selected California's logistics base and missed Stanford's research contracts until Terman learned in Massachusetts how to become indispensable. Hewlett and Packard stayed in Palo Alto with an oscillator. Disney bought eight. The Pentagon bought the rest. The next chapter is jamming German radar and trains leaving the county without farms.
