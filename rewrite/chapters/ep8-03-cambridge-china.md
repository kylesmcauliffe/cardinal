# Episode 8: The Open Era

## Cambridge Analytica

**London / Menlo Park. 2014–2018.**

In March 2018, reporting in the *Observer* and the *New York Times* described how a personality-quiz app had harvested Facebook data and passed it to the political consultancy Cambridge Analytica. On April 4, 2018, Facebook's chief technology officer, Mike Schroepfer, wrote that the company believed the information of up to **87 million** people — mostly in the United States — may have been improperly shared with Cambridge Analytica, a higher figure than the fifty-million estimate in earlier press accounts.

The Federal Trade Commission's July 2019 administrative complaint against Cambridge Analytica alleged that the firm's app harvested profile data from roughly **250,000–270,000** users who installed it and from an additional **50–65 million** "friends" in those users' networks, including at least thirty million identifiable U.S. consumers.

Workers in Kenya have brought their own cases.

The internal evidence about how the ranking system behaved became public in
2021, and it is the most useful documentation available anywhere of how a
chokepoint on attention actually operates.

A former employee provided a large volume of internal research to regulators and
to the *Wall Street Journal*, and testified before Congress. The documents
included the company's own studies of the effects of its products, and the
finding that received most attention concerned Instagram's effects on teenage
girls' body image.

The more structurally important material concerned ranking. Internal research
examined how changes to the feed algorithm affected what was distributed, and
documented that content provoking strong reactions travelled further than
content that did not, with predictable consequences for the tone of political
discussion on the platform. Researchers proposed changes; some were adopted and
some were not, and the tradeoff against engagement was explicit in the
deliberations.

What the episode establishes for this book's purposes is narrower than the

how its ranking systems shaped what people saw and how they behaved, and it made
decisions about that with commercial considerations in the room. That is
editorial judgment by any ordinary meaning of the phrase, exercised at a scale
no publisher in history has approached, by a company whose legal position was
that it was not a publisher.

What happens when the world's two largest economies share a supply chain and distrust each other?

A phone is designed in California, its processor fabricated in Taiwan, its
components sourced across a dozen countries, its final assembly done in China,
and it is sold everywhere as an American technology product.

Every part of that arrangement was chosen deliberately, for margin, by the firms
that now describe it as a strategic vulnerability.

This book has already documented the offshore turn twice, in two different
industries, in the same years.

Fairchild opened semiconductor assembly in Hong Kong in 1963, because packaging
and testing were labour-intensive and Hong Kong wages were a fraction of
Californian ones. Phil Knight was arranging contract manufacture of shoes in
Japan in the same period, and the production moved to Korea, then Taiwan, then
Indonesia, then China as each became more expensive.

Apple's manufacturing arrangements are the mature form of a decision made sixty
years earlier. The logic never changed: keep the parts that cannot be copied —
design, brand, the operating system, the store — and contract out the parts that
can.

The consequence, compounded over decades, is that the physical capacity to make
things migrated to Asia while the profits stayed in California, and that the
firms holding the profits no longer possess the capacity.

Contract manufacturing at that scale is not a passive activity. It builds
supplier networks, tooling expertise, logistics, and engineering capability, and
after enough iterations the contractor can design as well as build.

China's technology sector by the 2010s was not an assembly economy. It had
platform companies of global scale, a domestic payments and commerce
infrastructure more advanced than the American equivalent in several respects,
and research output in artificial intelligence that by most bibliometric
measures had become one of the two largest in the world.

A substantial number of the people who built it were trained in American
universities, many of them in this corridor. Stanford, Berkeley, and their peers
educated a generation of Chinese engineers and researchers, some of whom stayed
and some of whom returned. That is the elite reproduction mechanism of this book
operating internationally, and it operated in both directions.

There is one bottleneck in the modern technology economy that the Peninsula does
not control, and it is the most important one.

Advanced semiconductor fabrication — the manufacture of the most sophisticated
logic chips — is concentrated in a very small number of facilities,
overwhelmingly in Taiwan and secondarily in South Korea. The equipment required
to make them, particularly extreme ultraviolet lithography systems, is produced
by a single company in the Netherlands.

This is the first time in this book that California is on the wrong end of a
chokepoint, and the reaction to that discovery is the substance of the chapter's
second half.

The concentration happened for the reasons the fabless model made inevitable.
Moore's law required exponentially rising capital investment in fabrication, the
number of firms that could afford a leading-edge plant fell steadily, and
American firms increasingly chose to design rather than manufacture. The fabless
model that made Nike's margins and then the chip designers' margins produced, at
the end, a dependency on a small island that a rival power claims.

The response was industrial policy, and it is the earlier triangle reassembled
under a new name.

Export controls restricted the sale of advanced chips and chipmaking equipment
to China, tightened repeatedly from 2022. The CHIPS and Science Act, signed in
August 2022, committed tens of billions of dollars in federal money to
semiconductor manufacturing and research in the United States.

Public money, allocated by the state, to private firms, to build capacity deemed
strategically necessary, with universities attached for the research component.
That is the Pacific Railway Act's structure with silicon instead of track, and
the argument for it is the same argument: the private market will not build this
on the timescale the country requires.

The clearest diagnostic of how the position is understood came when somebody
else occupied it.

TikTok, owned by a Chinese company, became one of the most used applications in
the United States, particularly among younger users. The response was sustained
political pressure culminating in federal legislation requiring divestiture, on
national security grounds relating to data access and the possibility of content
manipulation.

The concerns are not frivolous. What the episode demonstrates is what the
American political system believes about platform power when the platform
belongs to someone else: that a foreign-controlled algorithm shaping what a
hundred million citizens see is a matter of national security requiring
emergency legislation.

Applied consistently, that belief has implications for domestically owned
platforms shaping what citizens see in dozens of other countries.

There is a second flow the chapter should record, because it complicates the
rivalry framing usefully.

Californian technology companies spent two decades trying to enter the Chinese
market, and the terms on offer were the mirror image of the arrangement they
enjoyed elsewhere.

Google operated a censored Chinese search service from 2006 and withdrew in 2010
after a series of intrusions and a decision it framed publicly as a matter of
principle. Facebook was blocked and courted the market for years without
success. Apple stayed, and the price of staying was compliance: hosting Chinese
users' iCloud data in China under local partnership, removing applications from
the Chinese store at official request, and manufacturing at a scale that made it
deeply exposed to the same jurisdiction.

The pattern reveals what platform power actually rests on. In most of the world
these firms set terms because no state was willing or able to impose its own. In
China a state was both, and the firms either left or complied. Nothing about the
technology changed; the difference was entirely a matter of who held sovereign
authority and was prepared to use it.

That is worth holding onto, because it is the clearest available evidence
against the proposition that platform dominance is a force of nature. It is a
relationship with public authority, and where public authority asserted itself,
the relationship changed.
