# Episode 1: The Ledger

## Nine Days

January–February 1848. Coloma and Guadalupe Hidalgo.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed on 2 February 1848, transferring California to the United States for fifteen million dollars.

Gold had been found at Sutter's Mill nine days earlier.

Nobody in Mexico City knew. Nobody in Washington knew. Word reached the eastern United States months later and was not widely believed until December, when the president confirmed it in a message to Congress and the rush began in earnest. For those nine days, and for a good while afterwards, the most valuable territory on the continent changed hands at a price set in complete ignorance of what it contained.

The coincidence is the best-known fact in California history and it is usually told as irony. It is more useful as an argument about what the treaty actually accomplished, because the gold was in the ground either way. What changed on 2 February was not the value of California. It was the legal system that would determine who owned it.

## The war, briefly

The fighting in California was small and confused, and this chapter treats it as
such.

American settlers around Sonoma declared a republic in June 1846 and raised a
flag with a bear on it, an episode that lasted under a month and was overtaken
by the arrival of United States naval forces. There was resistance in the south,
notably at San Pasqual, where Californio lancers handled American dragoons
roughly. The territory was secured by January 1847 under a capitulation
negotiated at Cahuenga.

The war was decided in Mexico, not in California. What California contributed
was the reason for it: the harbour at San Francisco had been an explicit
American objective for years, and the administration in Washington had attempted
to buy it before deciding to take it.

## What the treaty did

The instrument that mattered ran to a few thousand words and did three things.

It transferred sovereignty over an enormous territory — California, Nevada,
Utah, and parts of several other future states — in exchange for fifteen million
dollars and the assumption of certain claims. It established the boundary. And
it addressed the property and citizenship of the Mexican nationals who lived
there.

That third element is the one this chapter turns on, because it is where a
military occupation becomes a system of ownership. Conquest by itself produces
control. It does not produce title, and without title there is nothing to buy,
sell, mortgage, or develop. Capital cannot operate in a territory where
ownership is a matter of who is standing on the land.

The fifteen million dollars purchased a legal framework. That is the actual
acquisition, and it is the reason California became investable in 1848 rather
than merely occupied.

## Article X

The treaty as negotiated contained an article protecting Mexican land grants in
the ceded territories. The United States Senate struck it before ratification.

What replaced it was a diplomatic statement of assurance — the Protocol of
Querétaro — indicating that grant-holders retained their rights and could assert
them in American courts. The distinction between a treaty article and an
assurance about access to litigation looks technical. It was the whole game.

Because in 1851 Congress passed the California Land Act, which established a
commission before which every holder of a Spanish or Mexican grant had to appear
and prove title. The burden of proof was on the claimant. The proceedings were
conducted in English under American rules of evidence. Decisions could be
appealed to the federal district court and then to the Supreme Court, and they
routinely were, by whichever party lost.

Consider what a Californio family had to produce. A grant document from a
government that no longer existed. A *diseño* sketching boundaries by reference
to an oak tree and a creek. Evidence of continuous occupation. Witnesses. And a
lawyer, for a process that took, on average, many years — in a number of cases
more than a decade.

## The method

The outcome was a transfer of land accomplished procedurally.

Most claims were eventually confirmed. This is important and is regularly left
out: the commission was not a rubber stamp for dispossession, and the majority
of the acreage claimed was upheld. But confirmation took years, and years of
litigation had to be paid for, and the way a cattle-rich, cash-poor rancho
family paid for it was by selling or mortgaging the land at whatever price was
available during the period of uncertainty. Attorneys were frequently paid in
acreage. Squatters occupied the disputed land while the case ran, and by the
time title was confirmed there were people living on it who had improvements, a
claim in equity, and votes in a state legislature increasingly hostile to large
grants.

Families that won were ruined by winning. That is a more durable method of
transfer than confiscation and a much harder one to protest, because at every
stage it is a legal process with an appeal available.

The land ended up with American purchasers, lawyers, banks, and squatters.
Within a generation, Californio landholding in the north had largely collapsed.

## What fifteen million dollars bought

The sum deserves a moment, because it is the price at which the largest transfer
of territory in North American history was settled.

Fifteen million dollars, plus the assumption of some three and a quarter million
in claims by American citizens against Mexico, for a cession of roughly half a
million square miles. It was not a trivial amount of money in 1848 — it was
comparable to the Louisiana Purchase four decades earlier, and rather more than
the federal government's ordinary annual revenue at the time.

It was also, within eighteen months, less than the value of the gold coming out
of a single year's production in the territory it had purchased.

What the
United States bought was not a quantity of gold it did not know about. It was
jurisdiction: the authority to determine, through its own courts and its own
statutes, who owned what. Everything extracted from California over the
following century — the gold, the land, the timber, the water rights, the
railroad grants — was allocated under a legal system that the treaty installed.

Jurisdiction is the most valuable thing a state can acquire, and it is almost
never priced correctly, because its value depends entirely on what is done with
it afterwards.

## Citizenship

The treaty also addressed the people. Mexican nationals in the ceded territory
could retain Mexican citizenship by declaring so within a year, or become United
States citizens by default. Most became Americans, and Californios participated
in the constitutional convention the following year and in the early state
legislature; several were substantial political figures.

Within a generation that participation had largely ended, and the method was
the one described above rather than any formal exclusion. A landholding class
whose land is consumed by litigation stops being a political class. The
Californio delegates who helped write California's first constitution watched
their families' holdings pass to the lawyers who had represented them.

The treaty promised protection of property and delivered access to courts. In a
contest between a written guarantee and a procedure, the procedure wins.

## Indigenous title

Neither sovereign recognised it, before or after.

Mexican law had extended citizenship in principle without recognising Indigenous
land title in practice. American law did not extend either. Eighteen treaties
negotiated with California tribes in 1851 and 1852, which would have reserved
substantial territory, were sent to the Senate and never ratified; they were
placed under an injunction of secrecy for half a century. The tribes had ceded
their claims in the belief that reservations would follow. They did not.

There is no procedural complexity to describe here and no litigation to trace.
The claims were simply not recognised.

The fighting in California lasted seven months and changed very little. The paperwork lasted forty years and changed everything: who owned the coast, who owned the interior, who could borrow against it, and who was left holding a sketch map and a lawyer's bill.

The legal framework is now in place. The gold has been in the river for two weeks. And the largest voluntary migration in the history of the hemisphere is about to arrive in a territory where the land is already owned, the title is already contested, and the courts are three thousand miles away.

## After the treaty

Sam Brannan bought the shovels first.

Brannan was a Mormon elder, newspaper publisher, and storekeeper who had a
general store at Sutter's Fort and another in San Francisco. When word reached
him in the spring of 1848 that gold had been found on the American River, he
went and looked, confirmed it, and then went quietly around the region buying
every pick, pan, shovel, and length of canvas he could find.

Then he walked through the streets of San Francisco holding up a bottle of gold
dust and shouting.

The order of operations is the pattern in one anecdote. Brannan did not find
gold, did not mine gold, and by most accounts never seriously tried. He
identified the bottleneck between the deposit and the people who wanted it,
bought the bottleneck, and then created the demand. He is said to have become
California's first millionaire, and he did it selling implements at prices that
would have been indefensible in any market where a competitor could have arrived
in time.

## What happened to the men at the centre of the story

James Marshall found the gold and got nothing. He tried mining, tried lecturing,
tried a vineyard, and died poor in 1885 in a cabin in Kelsey. The State of
California voted him a small pension for a few years and then discontinued it.

John Sutter, on whose land the discovery was made and whose private establishment at New Helvetia had been the valley's only real enterprise, was destroyed by it within three
years. His workforce left for the diggings. Squatters occupied his land, killed
his cattle, and could not be removed, because the courts were overwhelmed, the
title was Mexican-era and contested, and the squatters had votes. His claim was
ultimately rejected by the Supreme Court in 1858. He spent the rest of his life
petitioning legislatures for compensation and died in a Washington hotel still
petitioning.

The discoverer and the landowner both ended as claimants. The man who bought the
shovels ended as a millionaire. That is not a moral about luck. It is a
statement about position.
