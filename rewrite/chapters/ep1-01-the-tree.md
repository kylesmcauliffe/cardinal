# Episode 1: The Ledger

## The Tree

November 1769. The Santa Clara Valley, Alta California.

The fog had burned off by midday, and the plain ran empty to the hills. Gaspar de Portolá’s soldiers made camp on the bank of a creek whose name they had not yet fixed in writing. Above them stood a redwood unlike any tree the diarists had seen on the march north from San Diego: one trunk split into two, rising like a tall stick against the sky. Someone in the party would call it *el palo alto*—the high tree—and the name would outlive the empire that sent them here.

Father Juan Crespí, a Franciscan priest traveling with the expedition, kept the kind of journal Madrid expected from a reconnaissance: distances, water, fuel, the disposition of the land for settlement or defense. His entries for the November halt describe a level plain wooded with oak and live oak and a good stream running through it, land he called "so very good and mellow that it can not be excelled." The tall redwood the soldiers named *palo alto* comes down through the expedition's tradition more than through his page; later editors and local historians tie that halt to the tree that still stands beside San Francisquito Creek, on ground that will later be a university town named for the stick itself.

Portolá had not come to plant a colony. Spain had no surplus population to send. José de Gálvez, inspector general of New Spain, had ordered the push north after expelling the Jesuits in 1767—a Bourbon administrator’s solution to a strategic problem: Russians moving down the Pacific coast, British ships in the north Pacific, and a Spanish claim on paper that had never been occupied in force.

The expedition was the first human instrument of that policy on the ground. Portolá commanded the soldiers. Junípero Serra, already aging, would found missions behind the military line. Crespí wrote the country into existence for readers who would never see it.

What they saw was useful. The valley floor offered grass and water. The creek gave a line on a map. The tall redwood gave a landmark visible from a distance—a natural surveyor’s mark before anyone in California owned a surveyor’s chain. In a territory where possession would always be argued with reference to trees, bends in water, and hand-drawn sketches, a tree like this was already a kind of deed.

They did not stay. The expedition moved on toward Monterey and the Bay, searching for the harbor Spain’s charts promised. They would stumble onto San Francisco Bay from the land side and fail, at first, to recognize what they had found. But they had paused long enough for ink to fix this place in the record, and the record is the subject of this episode.

The redwood still stands, fenced and braced, on the edge of a campus where the street signs carry the names of men who ordered missions built and grants signed. Twelve feet up and lit at night, the signs are easy not to read. The tree is harder to ignore. It was never a secret. It was only subliminal.

Portolá’s men broke camp and marched away. The Ohlone people whose villages lay in the valley did not appear in Crespí’s pages as negotiators or proprietors. They appear, when they appear at all, as part of the landscape being assessed—potential neophytes for a system not yet built here, labor for missions still on paper. That omission is also a record. Whoever keeps the journal decides who counts as a person with a name worth preserving.

Within eight years Franciscans would found Mission Santa Clara de Asís on this plain, close enough that the tree would mark the edge of a world remade by registers and rations. The creek would become a boundary in grants and diseños. The tall stick would become a postal address, then a railroad stop, then a university seal.

But in November 1769 none of that exists. There is only fog lifting, soldiers cooking, a priest writing, and a tree that is already doing work no one has yet paid for—holding a line on the ground that strangers will still be fighting over two centuries later.

The expedition left the valley. The diary remained.

## The rivals who never came

The Russian-American Company established Fort Ross on the coast north of the Bay
in 1812, roughly sixty miles from the Presidio, and farmed and hunted there for
three decades. It was a commercial outpost rather than an invasion, and it was
sold in 1841 to John Sutter — the same Sutter on whose land the gold would be
found seven years later, which is the kind of connection this book will keep
encountering and should not overstate.
