# Episode 5: The Engineer's Century

## Harvard Radio Research Lab / Internment

**Cambridge, Massachusetts. 1942.**

Frederick Terman had eight hundred people and a budget larger than Stanford's entire pre-war engineering programme. The Radio Research Laboratory at Harvard worked on radar countermeasures — jamming, deception, and the detection of enemy radar. Allied bombing depended on aluminium chaff dropped to blind German sets. The work was classified, urgent, and paid for by the federal government without apology.

Three thousand miles west, on the same spring day, families in Santa Clara County were packing what they could carry.

## The laboratory

The Radio Research Laboratory, established at Harvard in 1942, worked on radar countermeasures. It was a serious operation with a serious product; Allied bombing operations depended on its output.

Terman learned how federal research money actually moved: who decided, on what timetable, against what criteria, and what a proposal had to look like. He learned procurement and classification, and how to run an organisation in which most of the staff could not discuss most of the work. He learned how to recruit and manage several hundred technical people at speed, which almost nobody in American academic life had ever done.

And he learned the thing that made the rest of it worth knowing: that the funding would not stop. Total war had established that scientific capability was a strategic asset, and no government that had learned that lesson was going to unlearn it.

Vannevar Bush made the intellectual case in *Science, the Endless Frontier*, delivered to the president in July 1945. Bush argued that basic research was the source of practical advance, that it would be underfunded by private industry because its returns were unappropriable, and that the federal government should therefore fund it permanently — through universities, with the scientists themselves controlling the allocation.

Terman read it as a business plan.

## The grievance

Stanford had been a bystander to the largest scientific mobilisation in history. The contracts had gone to MIT, to Harvard, to Caltech, and to Bell Laboratories. Berkeley, thirty miles from Palo Alto, had built cyclotrons and staffed the Manhattan Project. Stanford had contributed individuals — Terman among them — to other people's laboratories.

Terman's diagnosis was not that Stanford had been unlucky. It was that Stanford had been the wrong shape. Federal science money went to institutions that were already visibly excellent in the fields the government needed, and it went to people the men making the decisions already knew. Stanford was adequate across many fields and pre-eminent in none, which meant that when a decision-maker in Washington needed a laboratory, Stanford was on nobody's list.

He wrote it down and he said it repeatedly for the next twenty years.

## Steeples

His strategy had a name, and it was the opposite of what most universities were attempting.

The conventional postwar ambition was breadth: build up every department, become comprehensive, cover the field. Terman argued for concentration. Identify a small number of areas where federal money was going and where Stanford could plausibly become pre-eminent, and put everything into those. He called them steeples of excellence — narrow, tall, and visible from a distance.

He chose electronics and microwave physics, which is where the defence money was going, and he recruited accordingly: senior people poached from other institutions with the promise of resources, and money to build around them.

The method followed the funding rather than the discipline, which meant the university's research agenda was substantially set by federal priorities. And it was ruthless about what it declined to support, which is why it succeeded where more equitable strategies did not.

## The land

There is one more piece, and Terman appears not to have recognised its full value immediately.

The university owned eight thousand acres of the San Francisco Peninsula, and under the Founding Grant of 1885 it could not sell any of it. For sixty years this had been treated as a constraint. The land was leased for grazing, used for agriculture, and otherwise sat there, an enormous illiquid asset generating almost nothing.

An asset that cannot be sold can be leased. And a university that leases land to companies whose staff it trains, whose research it partners on, and whose legitimacy it underwrites is not a landlord in any ordinary sense.

## Internment

Executive Order 9066, signed in February 1942, led to the forced removal and incarceration of some hundred and twenty thousand people of Japanese ancestry from the west coast, roughly two-thirds of them United States citizens. They were given days or weeks to dispose of homes, farms, and businesses.

The economic consequence was a transfer. Property was sold at distress prices, abandoned, or lost for non-payment during the years of absence, and it was acquired by people who remained. Japanese-American farmers had, despite the Alien Land Law of 1913, built substantial holdings in California agriculture, and much of it did not come back.

This is the same story the act has been telling at speed and under wartime authority: a population with no political standing supplies something valuable, and its removal has beneficiaries who are not required to account for the gain.

Santa Clara County was not an exception. Farms and businesses in the valley were liquidated or left. The same year Terman ran jamming experiments in Cambridge, the trains left Tanforan and other assembly points.

The father who had ranked children by test scores did not speak for the families on those trains. The son who would build the industrial park would return to a campus where the labour pool and the land market had both been altered by federal order.

## The classified fraction

A substantial and growing fraction of the research Terman would build at Stanford was classified. The public funded it, the university performed it, and the public could not examine the results. Terman regarded this as unproblematic and most of his contemporaries agreed. It was wartime practice continued into a cold war that appeared to be permanent.

It became a problem in 1969, when a few hundred students walked into a building and shut it down. The target they chose was the laboratory Terman built.

## 1945

Terman came back to Palo Alto in 1946 to a department of a few dozen people, carrying a management playbook and a grievance.

The war was over. The funding channel Bush described was opening. The land could not be sold. The county's Japanese-American community could not simply return to what had been taken.

A designer, a plan, a permanent funding channel, and an enormous piece of unsellable land. Everything the next episode needs is now in place.

Terman stepped off the train with the numbers from Harvard in his head and the acreage of the Founding Grant under his feet. The episode ends there: 1945, a plan, and land that could only be leased.

## Recruiting like an acquisition

Terman recruited the way a company acquires a business. He identified individuals whose presence would attract federal funding and other researchers, and he offered them resources rather than salary — laboratory space, equipment budgets, graduate students, and the freedom to build a group. He then used their arrival to recruit the next tier, on the argument that the field was moving to Palo Alto.

The clearest single instance waiting in 1945 was the Varian brothers, Russell and Sigurd, who with William Hansen had developed the klystron tube at Stanford before the war under an arrangement that gave the university a share of the royalties. The klystron mattered enormously to radar and later to microwave communications and to accelerators. Varian Associates would be founded in 1948, and in 1951 it would become the first tenant of the industrial park.

That sequence is the whole model in one company: university research, a licensing arrangement that pays the university, a spin-out firm founded by the researchers, and a lease on university land.

## Consulting and companies

Terman also encouraged faculty to consult for industry and to found companies, at a time when most universities regarded both as distractions or conflicts. He regarded them as the point. A professor with a company brings in contracts, places graduates, and generates the reputation that attracts the next recruit.

## The problem waiting in 1969

Terman regarded classified research as unproblematic and most of his contemporaries agreed. It became a problem when students walked into the Applied Electronics Laboratory and published what they found. The laboratory Terman built was the target they chose.

The episode's end turn is 1945: Terman comes home with a plan, and the land cannot be sold. The next episode opens when Varian signs the lease and the operating system switches on.

## Aluminium chaff

Allied bombing depended on aluminium chaff dropped to blind German radar — output of the Radio Research Laboratory Terman ran. The laboratory was a serious operation with a serious product. Running it taught him budgets, secrecy, procurement, and staffing at speed.

## Where Stanford stood

MIT and Harvard captured wartime contracts. Caltech and Bell Laboratories did the same. Berkeley staffed the Manhattan Project. Stanford contributed individuals to other people's laboratories. Terman's diagnosis: wrong shape — adequate everywhere, pre-eminent nowhere — meant exclusion from Washington lists.

## Incarceration, spelled out

Executive Order 9066 led to forced removal and incarceration of roughly one hundred and twenty thousand people of Japanese ancestry from the west coast, about two-thirds of them United States citizens. ## 1946

Terman came back to a department of a few dozen people. He carried a management playbook and a grievance. He had eight thousand acres that could not be sold. He had a son of the man who ranked children by test scores. He had a county reshaped by war, migration, and removal.

1945 ends the episode: a plan, unsellable land, and a funding channel that would not close. The park is next.

## The train back

Picture Terman on a train west in 1945 with a playbook written in procurement codes and a grievance written in contract comparisons. MIT captured the war. Harvard captured the war. Caltech captured the war. Berkeley captured the bomb. Stanford captured individuals.

He will spend twenty years making sure the next war's money cannot bypass Palo Alto. He will lease what he cannot sell. He will put Varian on the board. He will tell Hewlett and Packard to stay. He will build steeples narrow and tall in microwaves because that is where the checks will be signed.

Below the train window, farms that Japanese American families lost in 1942 are someone else's farms now. The county that will host the industrial park is already sorted by removal and covenant. The son of the man who ranked children will rank companies. The humanitarian graduate who fed Belgium will fund an archive that shapes who can argue about what happened.

Episode 5 ends in 1945. Episode 6 opens when Varian signs the lease and the operating system switches on.
