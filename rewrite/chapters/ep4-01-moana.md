# Episode 4: The Boy and the Farm

## Moana

February 28, 1905. Honolulu. The trade wind came off the reef and through the palms along Kalākaua Avenue, and inside the Moana Hotel a seventy-six-year-old woman was dying in convulsions.

The episode opens here and will return here before it ends. The reader is allowed to know that much at the edge of the scene. Inside the room, only the cramps and the prayers are real.

Bertha Berner would later testify to what she heard. Physicians would write reports. Newspapers on two coasts would publish conflicting headlines. For the length of a convulsion, there is only a body on a hotel bed and a voice asking God for forgiveness.

Jane Lathrop Stanford had left San Francisco six weeks earlier on the steamship *Korea*, accompanied by her secretary Bertha Berner and a maid, May Hunt. She had been shaken by an incident in January, when a bottle of Poland Spring water on Nob Hill tasted bitter and laboratory analysis found strychnine. She had vomited in time. She had told those close to her that someone meant to kill her. She had not stayed to find out who. She had gone to the islands to rest, to walk in the sun, and to put an ocean between herself and the household where poison had once been poured into her drinking water (White 2022, ch. 28, 167).

On the night of February 28 she took bicarbonate of soda before bed, as she often did for indigestion. Within minutes she was seized with cramps. She cried out that she had been poisoned again. Physicians were sent for. They watched her body arch and stiffen in the way strychnine produces. She spoke to God and to the dead.

"Oh God, forgive me my sins… Is my soul prepared to meet my dear ones?… This a horrible death to die" (White 2022, 173).

She was seventy-six. She had seen the Exclusion Act pass. She had watched workers march on Nob Hill. She had signed checks when probate forbade endowment spending. She was the co-founder of a university that had opened fourteen years earlier on eight thousand acres of a Palo Alto stock farm. She had kept that university alive through a national depression, through six years when her husband's estate was frozen in probate, through a federal lawsuit for fifteen million dollars, and through the firing of a professor whose speeches had embarrassed the memory of the railroad. She had sold jewels to pay for a library. She had paid faculty salaries out of allowances the court permitted her as a widow. She had travelled to Washington to argue that the government's claim belonged to the corporation, not to the man. She had won.

None of that stopped the convulsions on Waikiki.

The attending physicians would conclude that strychnine had killed her. A coroner's inquest in Honolulu would agree. Analysis would find poison in the bicarbonate bottle on her night table. The university's president would later sail across the same ocean with a different story.

That story belongs to the end of this episode. What belongs here is the room at the Moana, the palms outside, and a woman who had spent two decades turning grief into brick, faculty, and a self-governing board that answered to no electorate.

Twenty-one years earlier, the grief had not yet been named in a charter.

### March 1884

March 1884. Florence, Italy. The Hotel Bristol faced the Arno, and upstairs in a fever room the Stanfords' only child was dying of typhoid two months before his sixteenth birthday.

Leland Stanford Jr. had been travelling in Europe with his parents as they often did. He collected antiquities with the seriousness of a boy who intended to have a museum. His father had been a grocer in Sacramento, a governor, a senator, and one of four men who had built the Central Pacific Railroad across the Sierra and out to the salt flats of Utah. His mother had managed households on Nob Hill and on the farm south of San Francisco Bay, where trotting horses ran on a private track and a photographer had once frozen a horse in mid-air for science and for pride.

The disease moved faster than the doctors' assurances. Typhoid did not negotiate with fortune.

He died on March 13, 1884 (Harris 2023, ch. 1.3). He was fifteen. He would not inherit the railroad, the mansions, or the lawsuits that followed monopoly.

The telegram that crossed the Atlantic in the days afterward did not carry only a boy's name. It carried the end of a direct heir to one of the largest fortunes on the Pacific coast, and the beginning of a question no grief could answer simply: what does a man with no child do with a fortune everyone hates?

California in the 1880s still answered to the Southern Pacific. Settlers had died in wheat fields over the price of railroad land. Workers had marched up to Leland Stanford's mansion and threatened him during the crisis of 1877 (Harris 2023, ch. 1.2). Congress had passed the Chinese Exclusion Act with Stanford's support in the Senate race that followed. The octopus, as the novelist Frank Norris would later call it, had tentacles in courts, in legislatures, and in the deeds that said who could farm which acre of the San Joaquin.

A railroad fortune was not a quiet inheritance. It was a target.

Leland Stanford had left the Senate. He still sat on boards. He still owned stock. The mansions on Nob Hill remained visible symbols of everything sandlot orators hated. When his son died, the fortune did not shrink. The political exposure did not shrink either. A man with no heir and every enemy had to decide quickly what permanence looked like.

Jane had buried parents and siblings. She had moved from Albany, New York, to Wisconsin to California with a husband whose ambitions kept outrunning his origins. She knew how to manage staff, budgets, and social obligation. She did not yet know she would manage a university through a depression while the United States sued her for railroad bonds.

### The farm before the campus

South of the city, on the peninsula, Leland Stanford had bought land for a stock farm. He bred and raced trotting horses there with the intensity of a man who could afford to settle any argument with money. In the 1870s he wanted to know whether a trotting horse ever lifted all four hooves off the ground at once. The human eye could not decide. The bet required proof.

He hired Eadweard Muybridge, a photographer who had already scandalized San Francisco by shooting his wife's lover dead and who had been acquitted on grounds of justifiable homicide. Stanford rehired him in 1876, "apparently not begrudging Helios the murder and child abandonment" in between (Harris 2023, ch. 1.3). Muybridge rigged a line of cameras along the track, triggered in sequence by the horse itself. In 1878 the sequential photographs showed what the eye could not hold: the horse does leave the ground.

Motion pictures would come from many hands in the decades that followed. The standard claim that this episode alone invented cinema overstates the case. What it demonstrated was simpler and more durable. A private fortune of sufficient size could commission the answer to a question nobody else had the resources to ask, and the resulting images carried the patron's name. Stanford paid. Muybridge worked. They quarreled over credit and publication. The farm kept running.

That farm would become a campus. The fortune that paid for the cameras would be entombed in a different kind of instrument, one that could not be picketed at a depot door.

Visitors to Palo Alto in the 1870s saw fences, barns, and horses worth more than many families' yearly wages. Visitors after 1891 would see the same acreage reframed as education. The land could not be sold under the grant. It could only change use. That clause would matter when silicon replaced hay.

Muybridge's 1878 plates still circulated in lectures and in Stanford's pride. They proved patronage could buy time sliced thin enough to settle a bet. The university would later slice time in physics labs and film studies departments without remembering the horse.

### What the widow would do

Jane Stanford survived her son by twenty-one years. She did not survive him by retreating into mourning on Nob Hill alone, though she grieved and though spiritualism would later draw her toward mediums and séances when fear returned.

In the months after Florence she and her husband faced a choice that would outlast every spike driven at Promontory. They could leave the money to cousins. They could fund charities. They could build monuments. They had already been trustees of institutions. They knew what endowments could do.

The vow Leland was supposed to have spoken after the collapse in Italy would be recorded in the language of adoption: not a child of their blood, but a state full of children who would never know the boy's name unless they read it on a gate. That vow would be executed in a legal document in November 1885 and opened to students in October 1891.

Between Florence and the Founding Grant, twenty months passed. Between the grant and opening day, six years. Between opening day and the night at the Moana, fourteen years of payrolls, panics, professors, and poison.

The woman dying in Honolulu in 1905 had lived all of those intervals. She had seen her husband die in his sleep during the Panic of 1893. She had watched the federal government sue his estate for railroad debts while four hundred students still expected classes. She had removed an economist who criticized coolie labor from a lectern built with coolie labor's unpaid differential inside the endowment. She had tasted strychnine in January and booked passage on the *Korea* in February.

The trade wind moved the palms. Inside the Moana she asked whether her soul was prepared.

The physicians would write their verdict. The president would write another. The choir would sing at the mausoleum. The board would appoint its own successors, as the grant allowed, and the campus on the farm would keep teaching.

Twenty-one years before that night, the only child was still breathing in Florence. The fortune had not yet been converted. The boy was still collecting antiquities, and his mother was still a mother in a fever room, not yet the executive who would keep a university alive when the government came for fifteen million dollars.

### The hotel on the beach

The Moana had opened in 1901, a wood-frame palace for tourists who could afford passage from the mainland. Jane Stanford could afford it. She had chosen Waikiki because distance from San Francisco felt like safety after January. Bertha Berner kept her schedule. May Hunt managed the trunks. Physicians on call were a form of insurance the widow could purchase.

Honolulu in 1905 was American territory, not yet a state. Sugar planters and naval officers shaped local politics. A wealthy white woman dying in convulsions would draw attention whether she wanted it or not. The first physicians on the scene would see the classic signs of strychnine: rigidity, arching, terror in the patient's eyes. They would not yet know that David Starr Jordan would cross the Pacific to offer a second opinion the university could live with.

Inside the room, Jane spoke to the God she had prayed to in séances and in daylight. She asked whether she would meet her son. She asked forgiveness. The words were not a confession of guilt. They were the prayer of a woman who believed poison was murder and who had spent twenty years trying to buy permanence for a name.

The university on the farm did not pause for her convulsions. Classes would meet. Trustees would meet. Jordan would remain president until the board decided otherwise. The Founding Grant had never required a living founder—only a board that replaced itself.

### Twenty-one years

The cut back is not metaphor. From February 1905 to March 1884 is twenty-one years and eleven days. In that span the Stanfords buried a child, signed a charter, opened a campus, survived panic and lawsuit, fired a professor, and tasted poison twice.

Episode 3 ended on a telegram from Florence saying the heir was dead. Episode 4 opens on a widow saying the death is horrible. The documentary form allows that flash-forward because the body on the hotel bed is the episode's destination as well as its departure lounge.

Readers who met Leland Stanford at Promontory, who watched Mussel Slough, who heard the Exclusion Act signed, now meet the institution's co-founder at the moment it can no longer protect her. The university she kept alive will survive her. That survival is the through-line of every chapter that follows this cold open.

Grief turned monopoly into a campus. Probate tested the campus. Ross tested its speech. Poison tested its narration. The cold open compresses those tests into one dying woman's voice. Between those wires lies the story of how grief became an institution that could survive its makers—and narrate their deaths.

The trade wind moved the palms again. Somewhere on the peninsula, red tile still glowed in afternoon light. A pioneer student from Oregon was mining ore far from California, rich enough that the university would one day want his name on a tower. None of that was visible from the Moana's windows.

Twenty-one years earlier, the only child was still breathing in Florence. The fortune had not yet been converted. The boy was still collecting antiquities, and his mother was still a mother in a fever room, not yet the executive who would keep a university alive when the government came for fifteen million dollars.

Leland Stanford had swung at the last spike in 1869 and missed. The telegraph had sent done anyway. His wife died in 1905 with physicians watching poison work, and a president would later tell the public her heart had failed. The nation had learned to accept the railroad's story once. The university would ask California to accept another.

The farm south of the city still bore the boy's name on the gate. The widow on the bed bore the boy's memory in prayer. The institution would bear both when the choir sang and the steel chest closed.

Bertha Berner would survive her employer and write memoirs historians still cite. May Hunt would be questioned because servants with access to bottles are always questioned. The Moana's manager would remember a wealthy guest who came for rest and found agony.

None of them could stop what was already in motion: a university that did not need Jane's pulse to keep enrolling students, and a president who would soon book passage west. The episode would return to this room before it ended. The reader already knows the ending. The body in the bed does not.

The next chapter takes the grief seriously. It does not explain it away. It reads the vow and signs the grant.
