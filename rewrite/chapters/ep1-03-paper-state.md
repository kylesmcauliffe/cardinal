# Episode 1: The Ledger

## Paper State

1790s–1821. Monterey, the Presidio, correspondence to Mexico City.

The supply ship came when it came.

For most of the Spanish period the Presidio of San Francisco was garrisoned by a
few dozen soldiers, frequently in arrears of pay, wearing uniforms that had worn
out, and dependent for European goods on vessels that arrived at intervals
measured in seasons rather than weeks. The empire that claimed the finest
harbour on the Pacific coast was in practice unable to keep its garrison in
boots.

This is a chapter about how that arrangement nonetheless worked, and for forty-
five years it did.

## Governing by correspondence

The binding constraint on Spanish California was the speed of a letter.

Madrid to Mexico City was months. Mexico City to Monterey was months more, over
land or by a sea route that fought contrary winds most of the way up the coast.
A question referred to higher authority and answered would consume the better
part of a year, by which time the situation prompting it had resolved itself one
way or another.

The practical consequence is that authority devolved to the point of contact.
Decisions in Alta California were made in Alta California — by the governor, by
the presidio commanders, by the mission presidents — and reported afterwards.
Instructions from the centre arrived as general policy rather than as specific
direction, and the famous formula of Spanish colonial administration, *obedezco
pero no cumplo* — I obey but I do not comply — describes a functioning
arrangement rather than mere insubordination. An official acknowledged the
crown's authority while explaining that local conditions made the particular
order impracticable, and by the time the exchange concluded, years had passed.

An empire governed this way is held together by documents, because documents are
the only thing that travels. Maps, manifests, reports, and registers were not
descriptions of Spanish authority in California. They were the substance of it.

## Charting as possession

The charts deserve particular attention, because their audience was not local.

Spanish expeditions surveyed the coast, named its features, recorded soundings
and anchorages, and filed the results. None of this improved life for anyone in
California. All of it was directed at other European powers, for whom a charted
and named coastline with a documented history of occupation constituted a claim
that a rival would have to contest formally rather than simply ignore.

This is why the enterprise looks so disproportionate from inside. Spain invested
in mapping, in mission foundations, and in a garrison at a harbour it did
nothing to develop, because the asset being protected was not the settlement. It
was the claim, and the claim had value in a diplomatic system where such things
were adjudicated on paper.

Sovereignty preceded settlement, and it preceded any economy that could have
justified it.

## The passage nobody built

The Golden Gate is roughly a mile and a half wide. Behind it lies a bay system
covering several hundred square miles, fed by the rivers draining the Central
Valley, with anchorage sufficient for any fleet then afloat and shelter from the
Pacific in every weather.

A passage of this kind is unusual in this book because nobody constructed it.
The Central Pacific had to lay track; Standard Oil had to build refineries; a
platform has to be coded. The Bay was simply there, and its value was obvious to
anyone who sailed past it — which is why the Spanish, who had sailed past it for
two centuries without finding it, moved to occupy it within seven years of the
Portolá expedition stumbling onto it overland in 1769.

What the Bay controls is access to the interior of northern California, and in
due course it will control the export of everything the interior produces:
hides, then gold, then wheat, then fruit, then the output of a technology
industry forty miles down the peninsula. Every subsequent chapter in this act
happens within a day's ride of it.

## The road

The other piece of durable infrastructure from this period is El Camino Real,
which was not a road in any engineered sense but a route: the track connecting
the missions, presidios, and pueblos in a chain up the coast.

It matters because it fixed the geography. The missions were sited roughly a
day's travel apart, on good water and workable land, and those sites became the
towns. San Diego, San Juan Capistrano, San Gabriel, Santa Barbara, San Luis
Obispo, Monterey, Santa Clara, San José, San Francisco: the settlement pattern
of coastal California was determined by a Franciscan logistics decision in the
1770s, and it has not changed since.

The route itself is still the Peninsula's main street. Drive south from San
Francisco today and El Camino Real runs through Millbrae, Burlingame, San Mateo,
Redwood City, Menlo Park, Palo Alto, Mountain View, and Sunnyvale — which is to
say it runs directly through the towns that later episodes is about, past the Stanford
campus, within sight of the buildings where the semiconductor industry was
founded.

A Spanish supply road became the main street of Silicon Valley, and it kept the
name.

## The other paper state

There is one more institution operating here, and it is the one that will produce the next transfer: the land grant.

Spanish authorities issued very few private grants in Alta California — a few
dozen over the whole period — because the missions held most of the usable land
in trust, at least in theory, for the Indigenous communities attached to them.
The grants that were made went to soldiers and officials, and they were made on
the same basis as everything else in this chapter: a document describing
boundaries by reference to landmarks, filed with an authority that had no
capacity to survey or enforce.

The instrument was in place before the land was available. When secularisation
makes the mission holdings available in the 1830s, there is already a method
for distributing them and already a class positioned to receive them.

## The rivals who never came

It is worth asking whether the strategy was necessary, because the threat Spain
was defending against never fully materialised and the defence nonetheless
determined the shape of California.

The Russian-American Company established Fort Ross on the coast north of the Bay
in 1812, roughly sixty miles from the Presidio, and farmed and hunted there for
three decades. It was a commercial outpost rather than an invasion, and it was
sold in 1841 to John Sutter — the same Sutter on whose land the gold would be
found seven years later, which is the kind of connection the story will keep
encountering and should not overstate.

The British were a more serious prospect. The Hudson's Bay Company operated in
the Pacific Northwest and maintained an agent in San Francisco from the early
1840s, and British naval interest in California was real enough that American
officials worried about it continuously in the years before 1846.

Neither took the territory. But the anticipation of them is why the missions
were founded, why the Presidio was garrisoned, and why Spain held a harbour it
never developed. Fifty
years of occupation, twenty-one missions, and a demographic catastrophe were the
price of foreclosing something that did not happen.

When the United States finally took California it did so from Mexico, and what
it acquired was the Spanish claim with two changes of ownership recorded on it.

## Who this cost

The peoples of the Bay shoreline had their territory designated a strategic
asset of the Spanish crown in documents they never saw, in a language they did
not read, on the basis of a legal theory — that European sovereigns could
allocate non-Christian lands among themselves — that had been developed several
centuries earlier and several thousand miles away.

And the garrison was itself expendable. Soldiers at the Presidio were unpaid for
long periods, supplied irregularly, and unable to leave. They were the
instrument of the empire in California, not beneficiaries of it, and their
complaints survive in the same correspondence that documents everything else.

By 1821 Spain has held the best harbour on the Pacific coast for forty-five
years using a garrison, a chain of missions, and a filing system, and has
developed essentially none of it. That was not a failure of the strategy. It was
the strategy, and the claim it established is what Mexico inherits, and what the
United States eventually takes.

1830s. Thin sovereignty and grants.

A Mexican-era California land grant describes its boundaries by reference to a
creek, a stand of oaks, a hill, and the corner of somebody else's property. The
accompanying map — the *diseño* — is frequently a freehand sketch. It carries
the signature of a governor whose own authority is contested, in a territory his
government cannot reach.

That document is worth several hundred thousand dollars in gold within thirty
years, and the courts of another country will spend decades deciding what it
means. Property as an assertion nobody is in a position to verify is the
condition of California in the 1820s, and it is the founding condition of every
fortune in this book.

## Inheriting a territory without the means to hold it

Mexico became independent in 1821 and acquired Alta California along with
everything else, which meant it acquired the same distances that had defeated
Spanish administration with substantially fewer resources to apply to them.

The new republic was fighting for its existence through most of the following
three decades: coups, constitutional crises, foreign interventions, the loss of
Texas, and finally the war with the United States. Alta California was the
furthest, poorest, and least populated of its territories, and it received the
attention that ranking implies. Governors were appointed in Mexico City and
frequently resisted on arrival. On several occasions Californios expelled them.
In 1836 a local revolt produced a governor who governed with local consent and
minimal reference to the capital.

The formal picture is a territory of the Mexican republic. The practical picture
is a few thousand non-Indigenous people, some hundreds of miles apart, governing
themselves through family alliance and local force, with a capital at Monterey
whose writ extended about as far as its ability to send someone.

## What replaced administration

Land grants did. Where Spain had issued a few dozen over five decades, Mexican
governors issued hundreds, and the practice accelerated through the 1830s and
1840s.

A grant was not primarily a real-estate transaction. It was an instrument of
governance in a place with no other instruments. Granting land to a man created
an interest — he now had something to defend, and defending it meant maintaining
order in his district, keeping a body of retainers, and cooperating with the
governor who had made the grant and might make another. In the absence of a
salaried bureaucracy or a standing army, this was the available method of
producing loyal local authority, and it is roughly what medieval European
monarchs had done for the same reason, as an earlier chapter described.

The men who received these grants became the Californio elite: a small number of
families holding very large properties, intermarried, running cattle for the
hide and tallow trade, and exercising the practical functions of government in
their districts. Fifty thousand acres was not an unusual holding. Some were much
larger.

This is the frontier oligarchy, and the phrase should be understood as a
description rather than an accusation. Nothing else was on offer. A government
that cannot enforce will be replaced by whoever can, and the people who can are
the people with land, cattle, and armed retainers.
