# Episode 8: The Open Era

## The rotunda

**Washington, D.C. January 20, 2025.**

The cold was not the story. The seating was.

In the Capitol rotunda, Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Jeff Bezos, Sundar Pichai, and Tim Cook were placed in the first rows behind Donald Trump's family — ahead of much of the incoming cabinet in the photographs the wire services published that afternoon. The *Wall Street Journal* reported that the chief executives of the world's largest technology companies sat "directly behind Trump" while he took the oath, a prominence the paper contrasted with cabinet secretaries still finding their places.

Musk had spent more than a quarter of a billion dollars to help elect him. Zuckerberg and Bezos had ended years of public friction with Trump and appeared at St. John's Church that morning alongside Cook and Pichai.

Nobody in the rotunda needed a historian to explain what the seating meant. They needed only to look.

Then cut back twenty-one years.

## Palo Alto

**Palo Alto, California. 2004.**

When does subliminal become explicit?

On 20 January 2025, at the presidential inauguration in the Capitol rotunda, the
founders and chief executives of several of the largest technology companies in
the world were seated in front of much of the incoming cabinet.

Everything load-bearing in this chapter comes from disclosure filings, which is
the only responsible way to write about living people in a book making this
argument.

Peter Thiel contributed roughly fifteen million dollars to J. D. Vance's 2022
Senate campaign in Ohio, principally through a supporting committee — one of the
largest sums ever directed by a single donor to a single Senate candidate. Thiel
had employed Vance at his investment firm, had mentored him, and had introduced
him to the network.

Vance won the seat in November 2022. He was elected vice president in November
2024.

That is the network from a single company's payroll in 2002 reaching the second
office of the United States within twenty-two years, and it can be documented
entirely from Federal Election Commission records. No claim about coordination
is required, and none is made.

The broader pattern is visible in the same filings.

Technology executives and investors contributed at scale to committees
supporting the successful campaign, and several who had previously been publicly
associated with the other party or with no party endorsed publicly for the first
time. Elon Musk's contributions through a supporting committee were among the
largest individual political expenditures on record, and he campaigned
personally.

After the inauguration Musk was given a formal advisory position in the federal
government concerned with spending reduction and the reorganisation of federal
agencies, described in official announcements and reported extensively.

What is structurally novel is not that a wealthy person had influence over
government. Collis Huntington had done that in the 1870s, and was more candid about it. What is novel is a serving executive of firms holding major federal
contracts occupying a formal advisory role over the agencies that let them, with
the conflict identified and not resolved.

The argument of this chapter is that January 2025 changed the visibility rather
than the substance, and that this distinction is the whole point of the book.

The relationships were not new. They had been building for two decades in
disclosure filings that almost nobody read, in board seats, in a network that
funded itself, and in an institution that supplied the founders. Every mechanism
this book describes was operating in 2015 exactly as it was operating in 2025.

What ended was the discretion. An elite that once exercised influence through
donations, foundations, and quiet access began exercising it in front of
cameras, on a platform one of them owned, from a seat in the rotunda.

The book's thesis stopped requiring inference and started being televised. It
was equally true the morning before.

There is one further novelty. In 2022 one of the men in the rotunda had bought a
communications platform outright and reoriented its moderation and ranking
policies according to his own stated views. A previous generation of proprietors
bought newspapers, and the comparison is fair as far as it goes. It goes less
far than it looks, because a newspaper's readers knew they were reading a
proprietor's newspaper, and a ranking algorithm's users generally do not
experience the feed as an editorial product at all.

At an event at Capital One Arena on the day of the inauguration, Musk made a
gesture that was immediately and widely discussed, with interpretations
diverging sharply and along predictable lines.

This book does not adjudicate it.

The realignment that produced the rotunda seating deserves a section, because it
was not inevitable and happened quickly.

For most of the period since the 1970s, the corridor's politics were broadly
liberal on social questions and libertarian on economic ones, and its money went
predominantly to Democrats. The industry's self-image was progressive, and its
relationship with Democratic administrations was close enough that the traffic
in personnel between them was routine.

Several things changed that. Antitrust enforcement became bipartisan, and the
appointments made after 2021 were more hostile to the industry's structural
position than anything in the preceding forty years. Content moderation became a
partisan battlefield in which the platforms were attacked from both directions
and satisfied neither. Cryptocurrency regulation and the tax treatment of
unrealised gains became salient. And a generation of founders who had
experienced the internal politics of their own companies during the late 2010s
concluded that their workforces were an adversary.

The result was that a portion of the industry's most prominent figures moved,
publicly and rapidly, and the 2024 cycle was the first in which technology money
was a major factor on the Republican side.

It was the same set of positions — light regulation, favourable capital
taxation, resistance to antitrust, and freedom to operate — pursued through a
different party because the other one had stopped supplying them. That is what
an interest does, and describing it as an interest rather than as a conversion
is the analytically accurate move.

A citizen's participation in an American election consists of a vote. In 2022 a
single investor directed fifteen million dollars to a single Senate campaign,
and the candidate became vice president.

Both facts are legal. Both are documented. They are not the same kind of
participation, and the difference is not a matter of opinion.

And the reader should notice what this chapter did not have to do. It made no
claim about a meeting, a plan, or an agreement. It cited filings, a seating
arrangement, and an executive action. The argument of this book does not require
anybody to have conspired, and at the point where the mechanism becomes fully
visible it turns out that nothing needed to be hidden in the first place.

In 1877 there had been a crowd on a San Francisco sandlot being directed
toward the Chinese while the railroad's owners watched from Nob Hill. The
mechanism has not changed. What has changed is that the men on the hill no
longer find it necessary to stay up there.

Facebook grew from a dormitory to global communications infrastructure between
2004 and 2012 through network effects, a real-name policy that made the graph
unusually valuable, and the acquisitions of Instagram and WhatsApp, which
neutralised the two credible challengers. Its advertising model extended
Google's: not stated intent, as in a search query, but inferred disposition
assembled from behaviour.

It is revised continuously. It is published only in part. It is enforced at a
scale that requires automation, and the automation is imperfect in ways that
fall unevenly. And for most of the period this chapter covers, the company that
wrote it insisted that it was not making editorial decisions at all.

Facebook was founded in a Harvard dormitory in 2004 and moved to Palo Alto
within months, into the corridor described in the previous act. Its first
outside investment came from Peter Thiel, from the group.

Its growth mechanics were straightforward and unusually effective. A real-name
policy made the social graph accurate, which made it far more valuable to
advertisers than the pseudonymous networks that preceded it. Expansion school by
school created density before breadth, so that the product was useful on arrival
rather than empty. And network effects did the rest: a social network is worth
what its membership is worth, which means a better competitor with fewer users
is not a better product.

Two acquisitions closed the position. Instagram in 2012, for about a billion
dollars, when it had a small team and no revenue. WhatsApp in 2014, for roughly
nineteen billion. Both prices were widely mocked and both were rational: each
target was the most plausible route by which a competitor could reach comparable
scale.

A search query is a stated intention. A social
feed has no stated intention; the user is not looking for anything. What the
platform sells instead is inferred disposition — an assessment, built from
behaviour, of what a person is like and what they might be susceptible to,
combined with the ability to reach precisely defined slices of the population.

That is a more invasive product than search advertising and a more powerful one
for certain purposes, because it can reach people who were not looking.

Throughout its expansion the company described itself as a technology company
and a neutral platform rather than a media company, and this was not merely
rhetoric. It was load-bearing.

Facebook commissioned an independent human rights impact assessment, published
the same year, which concluded that the platform had been used to incite offline
violence and that the company had not done enough to prevent it. The company
accepted the findings and described measures it was taking.

This is what it means for a platform to be infrastructure. A telephone company
is not responsible for what people say on the phone; it is also not amplifying
selected calls to millions of people because they generate engagement.

The line from the Chinese railroad crews of 1867 is direct. The system's stability depends on
people it does not employ, held at arm's length through a contracting
arrangement, doing the work that makes the product usable and appearing nowhere
in the description of it.
