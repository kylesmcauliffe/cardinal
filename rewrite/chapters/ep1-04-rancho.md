# Episode 1: The Ledger

## Rancho

1834–1839. Secularisation and Rancho San Francisquito.

[TK: Soto family diseño primary source, Bancroft Library]

The decrees said the land would go to the Indians.

Mexican secularisation legislation of the early 1830s provided that the mission
estates would be broken up and that a portion — in some formulations half —
would be distributed to the neophyte communities that had worked them, with the
remainder passing to the state. The Franciscans would be reduced to parish
clergy. The mission holdings, which comprised most of the productive coastal
land in California, would be returned to the people who had been compelled to
develop them.

Almost none of it happened. Within a decade the mission lands were in private
hands, the neophyte communities held essentially nothing, and the labour force
was working the same ground for new owners.

Putting the promise beside the outcome is the whole chapter, and the gap between
them establishes a pattern the story will document for the next two centuries:
inclusion announced, exclusion delivered, with a paper trail showing that
somebody meant it at the time.

The last private transaction in that sequence is still on the campus map. Peter
Coutts Road, Frenchman's Road, French Lake Drive, and Ayrshire Farm Lane record
Jean-Baptiste Paulin Caperon — "Peter Coutts," a French banker and self-described
socialist who assembled the Matadero Rancho stock farm in 1875 and sold it to
Leland Stanford in 1881 for $140,000. The ranchos became one estate. The estate
became a university that was forbidden to sell the land. The street names are
the receipt.

## Why it was done

The motives were real and the chapter gives them fairly.

The missions were an anomaly in a liberal republic: a clerical corporation
holding vast estates and controlling a subject labour force, exempt from
ordinary civil authority, loyal to a religious order many of whose members had
opposed independence and remained Spanish nationals. Mexican liberals objected
to this on principle and had been objecting for a decade.

There was also a fiscal argument and a settlement argument. The territory
generated almost no revenue. Land in private hands would be taxed, developed,
and defended by owners with an interest in defending it, and settlement was the
only real answer to the strategic problem of a coastline that Russia, Britain,
and increasingly the United States were eyeing.

None of this is pretextual. The mission system was indefensible on the terms the
Mexican republic professed, and dismantling it was a coherent policy.

## What actually happened

Administration was placed in the hands of commissioners appointed by the
governor, who were to inventory the estates, distribute the allotments, and
manage the remainder.

The commissioners were, very often, the same men who stood to receive the land.
The neophytes, who were not literate in Spanish, had no experience of individual
title, no capital, no legal representation, and no way to pay the taxes and
debts attached to the allotments they received. Those who did receive land
generally lost it quickly — sold under pressure, seized for debt, or simply
occupied by a neighbour with more retainers.

Mission livestock was slaughtered in enormous numbers for immediate hide and
tallow sales during the transition. Vineyards and orchards went untended.
Buildings were stripped. The physical capital that six decades of coerced labour
had accumulated was substantially destroyed within a few years, which suited
nobody's stated intentions and served the interests of whoever could convert an
asset to cash fastest.

And the land moved. The pace of private grants accelerated sharply through the
late 1830s and 1840s, and the recipients were the Californio families of the
previous chapter and the naturalised foreign merchants who had married into
them. By 1846 several hundred ranchos held most of the coastal land in
California.

## The rancho economy

What emerged was an economy of extraordinary simplicity. Cattle grazed on
enormous unfenced properties. Once a year they were slaughtered for hides and
tallow, which were traded to visiting ships for manufactured goods. There was
little agriculture beyond subsistence, almost no manufacturing, and no currency
worth the name.

It was also, for the families who held the land, a comfortable and in some
respects admirable society, and the chapter should not caricature it. Californio
ranch culture was hospitable, skilled, and genuinely attached to the place.

It ran on Indigenous labour. Former neophytes and other Indigenous Californians
worked the ranchos as vaqueros, field hands, and domestic servants, under
arrangements that ranged from waged employment to debt peonage to something
closely resembling the mission system with a private owner in place of a priest.
Mexican-era vagrancy provisions permitted the arrest of unemployed Indians and
their assignment to labour, which is a method the American state of
California will adopt and extend after 1850.

The legal fiction had changed. The arrangement had not.

## Assets before markets

Here is the structural claim the chapter exists to make, and it inverts the
sequence most people assume.

The standard story of concentration runs: a market develops, participants
compete, some win, and wealth concentrates as a result. Concentration is the
outcome of market activity.

California ran it backwards. Its land was concentrated into a few hundred
holdings before it had a commercial economy at all — before the Gold Rush,
before statehood, before there was a currency, a bank, a court that could
enforce a contract, or a market in anything but hides. Monopoly was not produced
by California's capitalism. It preceded it, and California's capitalism was
built on top of it.

This matters for everything that follows. When the Americans arrive in 1846 and
the gold is found in 1848 and half the world turns up looking for it, they are
arriving in a place where the land is already spoken for. The conflicts of the
1850s — squatters against grant-holders, the land commission, the litigation
that consumed the Californio fortunes — are conflicts over a distribution that
was settled in the 1830s by a small number of men, several of whom were
administering the process and receiving its proceeds.

## What the ranchos became

It is worth following the properties forward, because the boundaries drawn in
the 1830s are still visible on a map of California today.

Rancho boundaries became city boundaries. The grants were surveyed, contested,
confirmed, subdivided, and sold, but the original lines frequently survived the
process as property lines, and property lines become streets, and streets become
municipal limits. A great many towns in coastal California occupy the footprint
of a single Mexican-era grant and are named after it — the Ranchos San Antonio,
Las Pulgas, San Francisquito, and dozens of others persist in the names of
neighbourhoods, roads, parks, and shopping centres.

One of them matters more than the rest for this book. The land that became the
Stanford campus, and the towns of Palo Alto and Menlo Park around it, was
assembled in the 1870s from parcels that had been part of Mexican-era grants on
the San Francisco Peninsula, including Rancho San Francisquito. The eight
thousand acres a railroad baron will give to a university in 1885 were laid out
as cattle country by a Mexican governor's signature fifty years earlier.

This is not mysticism about land. It is an observation about how property
persists. A boundary, once recorded, tends to survive every subsequent change of
sovereignty, currency, language, and land use, because each new owner acquires
the previous owner's description and it is cheaper to keep it than to redraw it.
The mission road became the Peninsula's main street; the rancho lines became the
Peninsula's parcels; and the parcels became a research park.

## The second dispossession

The people the decrees named received nothing.

That sentence should be allowed to stand without elaboration, because the
documents are explicit. The legislation specified allotments for the neophytes.
Officials in Mexico City intended it. The provisions existed, were published,
and were not carried out, and the men who did not carry them out ended up with
the land.

It is the second dispossession of the same population within seventy years:
first of their territory by the mission system, then of the restitution the
dismantling of that system had promised. And it will be followed by a third,
after 1848, under a new flag and a different legal theory, which the next
chapters describe.

## Sutter

One arrival deserves his own section, because he built the closest thing Mexican
California had to a private state and because the gold will be found on his
property.

John Sutter was a Swiss émigré who reached California in 1839 having left debts
behind him in several countries. He obtained Mexican citizenship, and with it a
grant of some fifty thousand acres at the confluence of the Sacramento and
American rivers — well inland, away from the coastal ranchos, in territory the
Mexican government did not control in any sense.

What he built there he called New Helvetia. It had an adobe fort with walls and
cannon, workshops, a tannery, a distillery, agriculture, and a workforce drawn
from local Indigenous communities under arrangements that contemporaries
described in terms ranging from paternalist employment to something much closer
to bondage. He issued his own scrip. He maintained an armed force. He conducted
what amounted to a foreign policy, corresponding with Mexican officials,
American officials, and the Russians at Fort Ross, from whom he bought the
assets of the abandoned colony in 1841.

Sutter's Fort was the destination for American overland migrants coming down
from the Sierra, and by the mid-1840s that traffic was substantial and growing.
He sheltered them, employed them, and outfitted them, which made him
simultaneously a Mexican official and the principal facilitator of the American
settlement that would end Mexican California.

The instructive thing about Sutter is not his ambition but what his position
reveals. A single foreigner with a land grant, a fort, and a private workforce
was one of the significant powers in a Mexican territory, and the government
that had granted him the land had no means of restraining him. That is what thin
sovereignty means in practice.

He will be ruined within three years of the gold discovery, overrun by squatters
on land he could not defend and could not get the courts to protect. The man who
built the largest private establishment in Mexican California ended it as a
claimant, petitioning legislatures for relief.

## Who the arrangement failed

For Indigenous Californians, independence altered the paperwork and very little
else.

Mexican law after 1821 extended citizenship in principle, and the mission system
was formally understood as a temporary arrangement that would end with the
neophytes receiving the land. In practice the missions continued to operate as
labour systems for another decade, and when they were dismantled the labour
force was transferred rather than freed, as the next chapter describes. Outside
the mission districts, Indigenous communities that had maintained autonomy came
under increasing pressure from expanding ranchos.

The legal category changed. The obligation to work somebody else's land did not.

## What is now in place

By 1834 California has a landholding class with very large properties, local
power, and titles issued by a distant government that can neither survey them
nor revoke them. It has a resident foreign merchant class with capital and
connections. It has an Indigenous population still supplying most of the labour
and holding none of the title. And it has an administrative apparatus so thin
that the principal instrument of government is the gift of land.

That is a stacked deck waiting for a dealer, and the largest transfer of
property in California's history is about to run straight through it.

## The merchants

The other significant development of the period is the arrival of foreigners,
and they arrived as businessmen rather than as conquerors.

The hide and tallow trade drew New England and British ships to the California
coast from the 1820s. Cattle hides — "California banknotes," in the traders'
phrase — were the territory's only substantial export, and the trade required
agents resident on shore to accumulate cargo between voyages. Those agents
stayed. They learned Spanish, converted to Catholicism, became Mexican citizens,
married into Californio families, and received land grants of their own.

Richard Henry Dana, who sailed the coast as a common sailor in 1834 and
published his account six years later, described the arrangement with the
specificity of someone who had loaded the cargo himself. His book was widely
read in the United States, which meant that by the 1840s Americans had a
reasonably accurate picture of California as a place with an excellent harbour,
negligible defences, and a government unable to control it.

The merchants are not villains in this chapter, and several of them were loyal
Mexican citizens who behaved decently in 1846. But they constituted, by the
mid-1840s, a resident foreign commercial class with local knowledge, local
marriages, local property, and connections to a rapidly expanding country three
thousand miles away that had already annexed Texas.
