What happens when surveillance software sits inside government capacity?

Palantir was founded in Palo Alto in 2003 by Peter Thiel, from the group, with Alex Karp, Joe Lonsdale, Stephen Cohen, and Nathan Gettings.

Its founding argument deserves to be stated at full strength, because a chapter
that assumes bad faith would be both unfair and analytically weaker.

The premise was that the post-2001 security environment presented governments
with two bad options. They could refrain from analysing data and accept the
resulting failures, or they could conduct mass surveillance and accept the
resulting damage to civil liberties. Palantir's proposition was a third path:
better analysis of data already lawfully held, with fine-grained access controls
and audit logs, so that investigators could find what they needed without
trawling everything and without the trawling being invisible.

That is a serious argument. Karp has made a version of it publicly for two
decades, and the company's software genuinely does include access control and
auditing features of the kind described.

Stripped of the vocabulary, the product integrates data across systems that were
never designed to speak to each other, and makes the result searchable and
navigable by an analyst.

A government agency typically holds its information in dozens of incompatible
databases, built at different times by different contractors for different
purposes. The practical consequence is that a question spanning more than one of
them cannot be asked. Palantir's business is making the question askable.

Described that way, the lineage in this book is obvious. A mission register in
1776 made a population countable. A Roman census made a province taxable. The
Hoover collection made a period of European history citable. Each converted a
disorganised reality into something an institution could act on, and in each
case the institution's capacity was the capacity to see.

The customer list is a matter of public record through federal contract
databases, and it is intelligence agencies, defence departments, and law
enforcement, alongside a commercial business that the company built later and
has emphasised increasingly.

The most contested contracts are with United States Immigration and Customs
Enforcement, which has used Palantir systems for case management and
investigative work. Documents obtained through freedom of information litigation
and reported over several years established the scope of that work, including
its use in operations against undocumented people and their families.

The company's response has been consistent: it distinguishes between divisions
of the agency, argues that its systems are used for criminal investigations
including human trafficking, and maintains that decisions about enforcement
policy belong to elected governments rather than to vendors. Employees inside
the company have objected internally and some have resigned, and the company has
declined to withdraw.

Here is the structural claim, and it is why this chapter is in this book rather
than in a survey of defence contractors.

When a state's perception of a problem is mediated by a vendor's model, the
vendor is not selling a tool. It is supplying a faculty. What the agency can
see, which patterns it recognises as significant, and which questions occur to
its analysts are shaped by the system through which it looks.

That is a governmental function, and it is being performed under a commercial
contract by an entity that is not subject to freedom of information law, not
directly accountable to any legislature, and answerable to shareholders.

Terman's university-contractor-state triangle had rested on the idea that federal money routed through universities because universities supplied
legitimacy along with talent. This is the same triangle with the legitimacy leg
removed and nothing pretending otherwise. Palantir never claimed to be a neutral
platform. It said from the beginning what it was for.

In that respect it is the most honest company in this book, and the chapter
should say so.

The company remained private for seventeen years, which is unusual, and listed
directly in September 2020 rather than through a conventional offering.

Its revenue figures are in its filings and should be cited from them, by fiscal
year, with the government and commercial segments separated.
What is durable and worth stating is the shape of the business: a long period of
losses funded by private capital while building relationships with agencies
whose procurement cycles are measured in years, followed by profitability at
scale once those relationships matured. That is a model only available to a
company with patient capital and a network that supplies it, which is what the PayPal network supplied.

The obvious question is why a government with the resources of the United States
buys this capability rather than building it, and the answer is the same one Terman's generation gave about universities.

Federal agencies procure software through processes designed to prevent
corruption, which means they are slow, specification-driven, and biased toward
contractors who are good at winning contracts. The resulting projects are
frequently delivered years late and occasionally not at all; the literature on
large federal IT failures is extensive and bipartisan.

Salaries are the other half. A government agency cannot pay what the corridor
pays, cannot grant equity, and cannot hire on the timescale a competitive market
requires. The people capable of building these systems are, with rare
exceptions, not available to it.

So the state rents the capability, and renting a capability is different from
buying a product in one respect that matters: the capability does not accumulate
inside the institution. When the contract ends the knowledge leaves, which makes
the next contract more likely and the dependency deeper.

This is the mechanism that produced the university-contractor arrangement in
1945, the research park in 1951, and the platform provision of public
infrastructure in 2000. Public institutions that cannot compete for talent end
up buying it, and buying it means the capability lives somewhere else
permanently.
