# Episode 6: The Park

## The Eight

**Mountain View. September 1957.**

Eight men resigned on the same day: Julius Blank, Victor Grinich, Jean Hoerni, Eugene Kleiner, Jay Last, Gordon Moore, Robert Noyce, and Sheldon Roberts. Shockley called them traitors, and the name stuck as the Traitorous Eight, which is the sort of label a group only accepts if it rather likes it.

They were a team Shockley later tarred as “the traitorous eight” (Harris 2023, ch. 3.2).

## The mechanics

They did not intend to found a company. They intended to move together to an existing employer, and Eugene Kleiner's father's contact at the New York investment bank Hayden, Stone was asked to help find one. The letter reached Arthur Rock, a young banker there, who came out to California, met them, and concluded that they should start a company rather than join one.

Rock then approached a long list of established firms — the number usually given is around thirty — asking whether any would back a new semiconductor venture run by eight men who had never managed anything. Almost all declined. Sherman Fairchild's Fairchild Camera and Instrument agreed, with an option to buy the whole company for a fixed sum if it succeeded.

Fairchild Semiconductor was incorporated in October 1957 in Mountain View.

The departure is the method, not a failure of it. The Valley reproduces by spinoff: talent exits bad leadership, carries the knowledge out in its heads, and starts again a few miles away.

## Why California made it possible

Three features of the place made it repeatable.

The first is legal. California does not enforce non-compete agreements in employment contracts, a position that goes back to the state's original civil code in 1872 and has held ever since. An engineer in Massachusetts who left a firm to found a competitor could expect litigation. An engineer in California could not. Route 128 around Boston had comparable talent, comparable universities, and comparable federal money in 1957, and it did not produce the same fan-out — and the enforceability of non-competes is among the more persuasive explanations for the difference.

The second is geographic. The Peninsula is dense. A new firm could recruit from an old one without anyone changing schools or selling a house, which lowers the personal cost of leaving to almost nothing.

The third is financial, and it barely existed yet. Rock's involvement in the Fairchild financing is the beginning of a form of capital designed to fund exactly this kind of departure.

## What they carried

They carried process knowledge, culture, and a shared assumption that a semiconductor company should be flat, engineering-led, and generous with equity. Jean Hoerni would develop the planar process that made integrated circuits manufacturable. Robert Noyce would see how to put multiple devices on one chip. Gordon Moore would later plot the curve that coordinated an industry's investment for fifty years.

None of that was visible in September 1957. What was visible was eight men walking out of a building Shockley had placed in Mountain View because his mother lived nearby.

## The military customer waiting

The chapter keeps the substrate visible, because the early market was not consumer electronics and was not close to it.

Integrated circuits were expensive and their first large customers were programmes for which cost mattered less than weight and reliability: the Minuteman missile guidance system and the Apollo Guidance Computer. Federal purchasing in the early 1960s absorbed the great majority of American integrated circuit production and, by guaranteeing volume, drove the price down the learning curve to the point where commercial applications became viable.

The consumer semiconductor industry exists because the Defense Department and NASA bought the first several years of output at whatever it cost — including the Minuteman missile guidance system and the Apollo Guidance Computer as early bulk customers (`books/cardinal/ch/4-03-fairchild-platform-company.qmd`). That is a public subsidy of the most direct kind, and it does not appear in any account of the industry that begins with a garage.

## Fairchild as platform

Fairchild Camera and Instrument was not a semiconductor company. It was a camera company that bought an option on eight angry engineers. The structure — venture backing, equity for founders, parent corporation with a buyout clause — would be copied until it became the default way Silicon Valley started firms.

Spinoffs would leave Fairchild the way these eight had left Shockley. Intel would come from Noyce and Moore in 1968. Advanced Micro Devices from others. The region would acquire an industry rather than a company, and an industry with a shared culture that had propagated by fission from a single origin.

## Eight men leaving a building

Eight men leaving a building is the founding event of the industry. The spinoff is the method, and Fairchild is about to demonstrate it at scale.

Shockley stayed in Mountain View and continued to alienate everyone who remained. The Traitorous Eight became Fairchild Semiconductor. The university's industrial park collected rent from companies their descendants would found.

The betrayal Shockley named became the region's reproductive method. California law made exit cheap. Geography made exit local. Capital, when it arrived, made exit fundable.

The traitors won the name. The landlord won the lease. The Pentagon would win the first invoices.

## Fairchild Semiconductor

Fairchild Semiconductor was incorporated in October 1957 in Mountain View. Sherman Fairchild's camera company held an option to buy the whole venture for a fixed sum if it succeeded — a structure venture capital would copy until it became invisible.

Jean Hoerni's planar process made integrated circuits manufacturable. Robert Noyce's independent development of the integrated circuit put multiple devices on one chip. Gordon Moore would plot component density against time in 1965 and give the industry a coordination device masquerading as a law.

## Moore's observation

In 1965 Gordon Moore, then Fairchild's director of research and development, was asked by *Electronics* magazine to write about the future of integrated electronics. He plotted the number of components on a chip against time, found the count had been doubling roughly annually, and projected the trend forward a decade. It held, with a revision to roughly two years, for half a century.

Moore's law is usually described as a prediction. It is better understood as a statement about what the industry could achieve if it kept investing at a particular rate — a public promise that disciplined private spending.

## Spinoffs from spinoffs

Semiconductors went the other way for twenty years because the crucial asset kept walking out and because there was now a financial form designed to fund it when it did. Noyce and Gordon Moore resigned to found Intel in 1968, taking Andy Grove with them. Others left for Advanced Micro Devices and National Semiconductor. The region acquired an industry rather than a company, and an industry with a shared culture that had propagated by fission from a single origin.

## Fairchild's military shelf

Fairchild Semiconductor incorporated in October 1957 in Mountain View — same town, same talent pool, different cap table (`books/cardinal/ch/4-02-shockley-and-the-eight.qmd`). Its early customers were not hobbyists. They were programmes where weight and reliability mattered more than unit cost: Minuteman guidance, Apollo avionics, and the broader defence-and-space shelf that absorbed most American integrated-circuit output in the early 1960s (`books/cardinal/ch/4-03-fairchild-platform-company.qmd`).

Jean Hoerni's planar process and Robert Noyce's integrated circuit made the parts small enough and reliable enough for those buyers. Gordon Moore's 1965 article plotted density against time and gave investors a public promise to coordinate private spending. The Traitorous Eight left Shockley's ego and walked into a customer that already lived on the same map as Lockheed's shifts and SRI's contracts.

The betrayal Shockley named became the region's reproductive method. The Pentagon's purchase orders became the industry's learning curve. The university's ground leases became the rent line on the same balance sheet as the chip sales. Eight signatures in September 1957 connected all three threads before any trade journalist had coined a valley's name.
