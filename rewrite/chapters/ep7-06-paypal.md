How does a failed startup become a ruling class?

It was a joke about a real thing, and by naming it the magazine made it more so.
A network that has been told it is a network behaves like one.

PayPal was not, by the standards of what its alumni went on to build, a large
success.

Confinity was founded in 1998 by Max Levchin and Peter Thiel, initially to do
cryptography and payments on handheld devices, and it developed a way to send
money by email. X.com was founded by Elon Musk in 1999 as an online financial
services company. Both were in Palo Alto, both were chasing the same emerging
market in payments for online auctions, and in 2000 they merged.

The merged company then had a difficult year. There was a management fight; Musk
was removed as chief executive while he was on a plane, and Thiel took over.
Fraud losses were severe enough to threaten the business, and Levchin's team
built the detection systems that eventually contained them. The company depended
almost entirely on eBay for transaction volume, and eBay was building a
competitor.

PayPal went public in February 2002 and was acquired by eBay in October of that
year for roughly one and a half billion dollars in stock.

That is a good outcome and an unremarkable one. Several dozen companies of the
period did comparably well, and most of their alumni went on to ordinary
careers.

What made this one different is what the participants did with the proceeds and
with each other.

Musk put most of his share into SpaceX, founded in 2002, and into Tesla, where
he led the Series A in 2004 and became chairman. Thiel founded Palantir in 2003
and the hedge fund Clarium, and in 2004 made the first outside investment in
Facebook, half a million dollars for a stake that eventually returned around a
billion. Reid Hoffman, who had been chief operating officer, founded LinkedIn in
2002. Chad Hurley, Steve Chen, and Jawed Karim, who had worked at the company,
founded YouTube in 2005. Jeremy Stoppelman and Russel Simmons founded Yelp in
2004, with Levchin backing it. Levchin later founded Affirm. Roelof Botha became
a partner at Sequoia and then its senior steward. Keith Rabois invested and
operated across a dozen of the others. Thiel and others founded Founders Fund in
2005.

They invested in each other's companies, sat on each other's boards, recruited
from each other, and introduced each other to capital. Facebook's first outside
money came from this group. So did a substantial share of the early rounds of a
generation of companies.

Consider what the group actually held after 2002. They had capital, which is
useful and replaceable. They had shared operating experience of scaling a
business through fraud, regulation, and a near-death funding environment, which
is rarer. And they had a calibrated view of each other: they knew who was good
at what, who could be relied on, and who could be backed without diligence.

That last item is the valuable one. The fundamental problem in early-stage
investing is that there is no information — no revenue, no product, sometimes no
company. Everything turns on an assessment of people, and assessment is
expensive. A group that has already assessed each other under pressure can move
faster and with more conviction than anyone outside it, and speed and conviction
are what win competitive deals.

This is a credentialling system with no examination and no institution. Stanford had been built to issue exactly that kind of certificate. This is the same function performed by a shared employment
history and a photograph.

The group also holds, in substantial part, a coherent set of political
commitments.

Thiel has been explicit for two decades: a scepticism about the compatibility of
democracy and freedom as he defines them, a conviction that competition is
overrated and monopoly is the correct objective for a business, and a belief
that technological progress has stalled outside computing. His 2014 book states
the monopoly argument directly, and it is a coherent position rather than a
slip.

He funded a Senate campaign in 2022 that reached the vice presidency two years
later. Others in the group have funded candidates, founded political
organisations, and acquired media. Musk bought a communications platform in 2022
and subsequently took a role in the federal government.

What it records is that a
group of colleagues from one company's payroll in 2002 held, twenty years later,
positions in defence software, space launch, electric vehicles, social
networking, payments, venture capital, and national politics.

There is a reading of PayPal's founding that is easy to miss and that explains a
great deal about what its alumni did next.

Thiel has said repeatedly that the original ambition was not to build a
convenient way to pay for auction items. It was to create a currency outside the
control of governments — a means of moving value that monetary authorities could
not devalue or block. The payments business was what that ambition became when
it met regulation, the card networks, and the need for revenue.

The company that resulted was thoroughly conventional: it settled in dollars,
partnered with banks, complied with money transmission rules in fifty states,
and was eventually bought by an auction site. The founding intention survived as
disposition rather than as product.

That disposition — a conviction that existing institutions are obstacles rather
than frameworks, and that a sufficiently well-designed private system can route
around them — is visible in almost everything the group built afterwards. A
private launch company competing with a national space programme. A software
firm supplying capabilities that governments could not build themselves. A
currency movement several of them backed early. A social platform acquired and
reoriented by one of them on explicitly political grounds.

That is a good outcome and an unremarkable one. Several dozen companies of the period did comparably well.

 Musk put most of his share into SpaceX and Tesla. Thiel founded Palantir in 2003 and made the first outside investment in Facebook in 2004. Hoffman would build LinkedIn. Levchin would start Affirm. The address book became a governing class.

In 2007 *Fortune* photographed a group of former PayPal employees dressed as a crime family and ran the picture under the label the PayPal Mafia.

The young men walked out with cash and a network. The road had done its work again.

Nobody in the parking lot that afternoon knew which of them would sit in a rotunda twenty-three years later, behind a president and ahead of a cabinet.
