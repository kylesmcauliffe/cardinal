# Episode 2: The Toll

## The Summit

Late May–June 1867. Donner Summit, Sierra Nevada.

Snow still lay in the cuts above the tunnel faces. The Central Pacific had been driving granite for two winters, measuring progress in inches per day, blasting with black powder and nitroglycerine while men worked in cold that turned breath to ice on beards and jacket collars. The Summit Tunnel was the line’s throat: until it opened, the Sierra remained a wall between Sacramento and the interior West.

The men doing that work were, by this stage, overwhelmingly Chinese.

Crocker's construction superintendent, James Strobridge, had opposed hiring Chinese workers. Crocker overruled him — the labour supply was inadequate, white workers left for the mines constantly, and a trial group performed well. From 1865 the company recruited heavily, first in California and then directly from Guangdong (repo ch. 2-09).

They were paid less than white workers doing the same work, and unlike white workers they were not provided with board, which they organised themselves. The company did not systematically record their deaths, which is why every figure offered is an estimate and why the estimates vary so widely. Avalanches, explosions, rockfalls, and cold killed men whose names rarely entered the official story the Promontory photograph would later tell.

In late May, unbelievably and unacceptably, the Chinese went on strike. “This is the hardest blow we have had,” E. B. Crocker reported (Ambrose 2000, ch. 11).

The ledger in the project appendix fixes a date: 25 June 1867, several thousand men on the Sierra grade demanding pay parity and shorter tunnel hours (appendix-b-ledger.qmd). Ambrose places the walkout in late May; the company’s own later accounts blur the calendar. The strike’s length in days matters less than its length in leverage: long enough to frighten Crocker, short enough to break before bond deadlines slipped. Whether the walkout began in late May or consolidated in June, the fact on the ground was the same: labour that had been treated as replaceable stopped moving rock and rail at the moment the company needed it most.

Chang’s account of the walkout is quieter than a Hollywood strike. “The men who were working at that hour walked out of the tunnels and away from the tracks. The ones who were sleeping slept on…” (Chang 2019, ch. 7).

No speeches survive in the company record like Crocker’s complaint. The action was spatial: bodies leaving the tunnel mouth, tools set down, the grade going still while foremen counted who was missing.

The demands, as reconstructed in the secondary literature summarized in repo ch. 2-09, were concrete: wages equal to white workers doing the same work, shorter hours in the tunnels, and an end to whipping and to physical restraint when men tried to leave camp. By any measure it was one of the largest labour actions in the United States to that date.

[TK: Chang ch. 7 — Stanford warning to Huntington weeks before strike, exact wording.] The timeline notes Stanford relayed warning about Chinese unhappiness to Huntington before the walkout (timeline.md; Chang ch. 7). If the president in Sacramento knew trouble was coming and the men in the snow knew they had been underpaid for years, the strike was not a surprise. It was a bill presented after a long tab.

Charles Crocker broke it in about a week by cutting off the food supply.

That sentence is the whole politics of the summit in seven words. The company did not argue the merits on a square. It controlled rice and pork and flour in camps miles from any town. Men who refused to work could be starved back to the face faster than any court could hear them. They went back to work.

## After the strike

The tunnels did not stop because men had made a moral point. They stopped because granite was still there and bonds were still being issued per mile. Snowsheds would eventually cover miles of track on the western approach—timber tunnels above the rails so avalanches would pass over the line instead of burying it. Chinese crews continued to do the lethal share of that work, paid less, credited less, and absent from the photographs when the work was done.

Thirteen years later the United States passed a law barring Chinese labourers from entering the country. The men who had blasted through the Sierra would be told, by federal statute, that their presence had been a temporary convenience rather than a contribution.

The strike did not change the pay structure in any lasting way the record celebrates. It did reveal what the company would do when labour briefly held the only leverage available in a mountain camp: withhold food until the line moved again.

The hardest blow Crocker reported was not to the schedule alone. It was to the story the Associates needed—that the continent was being united by voluntary American effort. The walkout in the snow proved the summit was built by men the ceremony at Promontory would not name, and that when those men asked for parity, the answer came from the commissary, not the courts.

The tunnel faces waited. The snow did not negotiate. Neither did Crocker.

## The work before the walkout

To understand the strike, start with the work it interrupted.

The Summit Tunnel was driven through granite by hand, black powder, and nitroglycerine, at rates measured in inches per day through two of the worst winters on record (repo ch. 2-09). Men lived and labored in cuts where snow buried the grade so completely that crews dug tunnels through drifts simply to reach the face. Avalanches killed without warning. Explosions killed with warning men could not heed fast enough.

Strobridge ran construction on the ground. Crocker ran the decision to hire Chinese workers when white labour walked off to mines or simply would not stay at the wages offered. From 1865 recruitment extended to Guangdong. At peak construction the Central Pacific employed somewhere between ten and twelve thousand Chinese workers—constituting the large majority of its workforce (repo ch. 2-09).

They organized their own board because the company would not provide it. They were paid less than white workers doing the same work. When they died, the company did not systematically record their names. Historians still argue about totals. The absence of a ledger is itself a policy.

## The week Crocker won

Crocker’s response to the strike was not negotiation. It was logistics.

Food in a mountain camp is a weapon if one side controls the storehouse. Cutting off rice and pork and flour did not require a court order. It required foremen willing to enforce starvation until men returned to the tunnels. The company won in about a week (repo ch. 2-09; appendix-b-ledger.qmd).

Ambrose’s framing of the strike as “unbelievable and unacceptable” records the shock of men who believed the workforce was a commodity that would not organize (Ambrose 2000, ch. 11). Chang’s framing records the silence of the walkout itself—the men who were sleeping slept on—without the brass band of American labour history (Chang 2019, ch. 7). Read together, they describe the same event: a revolt large enough to terrify Crocker, and disciplined enough to leave the company’s narrative scrambling.

E. B. Crocker’s line—“the hardest blow we have had”—is the sound of that scramble. It is also an admission that the line’s hardest blows were not granite. They were human.

## What continued after

The strike ended. The tunnels did not.

Men went back to faces that still had to be blasted. Snowsheds would cover miles of track on the western approaches—wooden tunnels above the rails so avalanches could pass over the line instead of wiping it out each winter. Chinese crews continued to do the lethal share. Irish rail handlers worked beside them in smaller numbers. Pay inequality persisted.

On 28 April 1869 a Central Pacific crew—Chinese, with a handful of Irish rail handlers—would lay ten miles and fifty-six feet of track in a single day to win a bet with the Union Pacific (repo ch. 2-09). That record still stands. It is remembered as a feat of organization and speed. It is rarely remembered as the culmination of labour that had been starved back to work less than two years earlier.

The Promontory photograph would come eleven days later. The men who laid ten miles in a day would not fill the frame. The men who broke the strike with a commissary would.

Nobody in the snow in 1867 knew the date of the photograph. They knew the face of the tunnel, the weight of a drill, and the price of asking to be paid like the men beside them.

Crocker knew the price of saying no. He set it at a week without food.

## A photograph they would not take

There would be no lithograph of the strike. No champagne on the tunnel face. No telegraph message to tell the nation that Chinese workers had shut down one of the largest construction projects in North America until they were treated like men rather than replaceable parts.

There would only be Crocker’s letter calling it the hardest blow, and Chang’s image of men walking out while others slept on, and the company’s commissary closing like a door.

When the line was finished, promoters would prefer the ten-mile day—a spectacle of speed. They would prefer Promontory—a spectacle of union. They would not prefer the week in 1867 when the summit belonged to labour, briefly, until hunger returned it to capital.

That omission is as deliberate as the absence of Chinese faces in the Promontory photograph. The toll road required a story about voluntary American progress. The strike was evidence that progress had been extracted, inch by inch, from men who had signed up for wages and received a war instead.

The tunnel faces waited. The snow did not negotiate. Neither did Crocker—and when he won, the line moved again toward the bet Crocker would make with Durant, toward the spike Stanford would miss, toward the books Huntington would hide.

The summit was not a backdrop. It was the price of the photograph.

## Numbers without names

Between ten and twelve thousand Chinese workers at peak. Several thousand on strike. A week to break them. Two years later, ten miles and fifty-six feet of track in a day. These figures come from company papers, congressional testimony summarized in secondary accounts, and the project ledger that lists 25 June 1867 as a strike date (repo ch. 2-09; appendix-b-ledger.qmd).

What the numbers do not carry is names in proportion. The Central Pacific did not systematically record Chinese deaths. Estimates of fatalities on the Sierra range widely in later scholarship. The company preferred records that could be shown to bondholders: miles completed, subsidies claimed, photographs taken.

When historians reconstruct the strike, they reconstruct it from Crocker’s alarm, Chang’s image of men walking away, and the silence where payroll detail should be. That silence is not an archival accident. It is how a firm communicates which lives are countable.

Strobridge and Crocker would be named in every history. E. B. Crocker’s quote would survive because it served the company’s narrative of victimhood at the hands of ungrateful labour. The men who walked out would survive as a crowd.

The strike did not stop the tunnel. It marked the moral economy of the line: work extracted under unequal pay, discipline enforced by hunger, credit claimed by men who would later miss spikes on purpose for the cameras and still hear bells.

## Strobridge on the ground

Strobridge had opposed hiring Chinese workers until Crocker overruled him with evidence from a trial crew and the simple fact that white labour would not stay (repo ch. 2-09). After that, Strobridge ran camps where nitroglycerine and black powder turned granite into rubble inch by inch. He was the face foremen saw when shifts changed. Crocker was the voice that decided whether rice arrived.

When the strike came, Strobridge did not negotiate as an equal with thousands of men. He enforced Crocker’s commissary policy because that was the job: keep the tunnel moving, keep the bond miles counting, keep the mountain rate collecting on the western slope.

The company’s public story would later praise organization—the ten-mile day, the snowsheds, the completion photograph. The private story in 1867 was a week without food until men picked up tools again.

That is the summit’s legacy as surely as the tunnel opening: proof that the line’s owners would treat hunger as a management tool, and that Congress, having paid in land and bonds, would not intervene on the side of the men who made the line possible.

The tunnel faces waited. The snow did not negotiate. Neither did Crocker—and when the strike ended, the same faces returned to the same granite, for the same differential pay, until the wire at Promontory said the job was done.

## Snowsheds and credit

After the strike, the company did not reform pay. It engineered around the mountain.

Miles of timber snowsheds would cover the western approaches—wooden tunnels above the track so avalanches could pass overhead while trains ran underneath (repo ch. 2-08, Davis survey contrast). Chinese crews cut and lifted the timber. Irish handlers spiked rail. Bond miles accumulated at the higher mountain rate where geology reports placed the Sierra’s base.

Credit flowed to the railroad. Wages flowed to men at different tables. Death flowed to the camps without ledgers.

When E. B. Crocker called the strike the hardest blow, he meant hardest to the schedule and to the subsidy arithmetic. He did not mean hardest to conscience. The company’s response proved that.

The summit was where the public story of the transcontinental line—heroic engineering, national union—met the private story of who would be paid, fed, and named. The strike made the meeting visible for one week. Hunger made it invisible again.

The tunnel opened. The sheds went up. The ten-mile day approached. Promontory waited with its cameras and its missing spike.

The men who had walked out in June would not be invited to the platform in May. They had already been taught what the commissary could do.
