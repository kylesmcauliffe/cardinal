# Episode 6: The Park

## April Third

**Stanford. 3 April 1969.**

Seven hundred people filled Dinkelspiel Auditorium. The factions — campus SDS, an SRI coalition, others — reorganised into one body and took its name from the date. On the wall outside, a poster would soon read “Strike Against the Stanford Empire” (Harris 2023, Section III frontispiece).

Their demands were four. The first is the hinge of this chapter.

They wanted the trustees to impose stricter guidelines on SRI. They wanted an end to all classified research at the university and at SRI. They wanted an end to all chemical and biological warfare work and all counterinsurgency work at both institutions, whether classified or not. And they wanted a public meeting of the full board of trustees on the question.

Read the first demand again. They were asking the university to exercise more control over its research institute, not less. They wanted the institution to own what it owned.

Six days later, about four hundred students walked into the Applied Electronics Laboratory and stopped work.

## What they had been reading

The movement did not begin in 1969 and it did not begin with slogans. It began with people reading contracts.

Concern surfaced in 1965, when it became known on campus that the Stanford-owned Research Institute in Menlo Park held two contracts concerning chemical warfare. It intensified in 1967 when students learned that SRI had taken on work related to Southeast Asia, and picketing began outside the Menlo Park buildings that April.

The numbers they assembled were the strongest part of their case. Across the fifteen years from 1950 to 1965, only nineteen of five hundred and eighty-two doctoral and engineering theses in Stanford's electrical engineering department had been classified, more than half of those during the Korean War. By 1968, close to thirty-five per cent of the department's electronics research funding was classified work, concentrated in the Applied Electronics Laboratory.

Something had changed, and it was legible in the budget. The students were not imagining a transformation of their university; they had documented one.

## Nine days

At a quarter to eleven on the morning of 9 April 1969, about four hundred Stanford students walked into the Applied Electronics Laboratory and stopped work.

Roughly two million dollars of classified Defense Department research was under way in the building. The students stayed nine days.

They ran a child care centre in one laboratory. They set up a bookstore in another, and named two rooms for Eldridge Cleaver and Che Guevara. A high school auxiliary operated in the basement. They held continuous open meetings and voted on everything.

And they used the laboratory's own printshop as a press. They went through the material they found, and published it: a booklet called *The Goods on AEL*, and a newsletter called *Declassified* that ran throughout the occupation.

Students in a building they had occupied, printing the classified research they found there, on the equipment they found there. The administration did not call the police into the AEL. President Kenneth Pitzer sought an outcome that avoided the scenes at Columbia and Berkeley. Armed with emergency authority, he eventually threatened suspension, and the students left on 18 April. He then closed the laboratory to everybody, including its own staff, for a week.

## Winning on paper

The concessions came quickly.

Joseph Pettit, the dean of engineering, announced that the school would accept no further classified contracts and would wind down the ones it held. On 24 April the academic senate adopted guidelines that effectively banished classified research from the campus.

That is a comprehensive victory on the terms the movement had stated. Classified research at Stanford essentially ended, and it has not returned in the form it took in 1968.

## The restructuring

Then the trustees answered the SRI question, and the answer is why this chapter exists.

A joint study committee of students, faculty, and administrators had been appointed to consider the relationship. In April 1969 nine of its twelve members recommended that Stanford sell the institute. SRI's book value was around twenty-five million dollars and its actual worth was estimated at something closer to forty-five. Some committee members wanted restrictive covenants attached to any sale, prohibiting chemical weapons and counterinsurgency work. A minority argued for the opposite course: that Stanford should take control and redirect the institute, because divesting it would not address the substance at all.

In January 1970 the trustees set the terms. SRI would be sold to itself, paying one per cent of its gross operating revenues — which had been fifty-eight million dollars in 1969 — up to a total of twenty-five million.

The chemical warfare work did not stop. The counterinsurgency work did not stop. The classified contracts did not stop. It simply stopped being Stanford's.

Researchers left the AEL for SRI, taking eight hundred thousand dollars in contracts with them. The work moved four miles to a building nobody could hold a sit-in about, because it no longer belonged to anyone the students could petition.

## Tear gas

On 16 May 1969, students blocking traffic near the SRI building were dispersed by police after four hours. They were protesting the divestment. They had asked the university to take responsibility; it had taken its name off the door instead.

## Who was in the room

These are not the powerless; they are the children of the elite, objecting from inside. The people actually on the receiving end of counterinsurgency research were in Vietnam and had no vote in Dinkelspiel Auditorium. The chapter is stronger for conceding that the conscience of the machine is still the machine.

## The end turn

Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970, and the military work moves off-campus without slowing down.

The rebellion did not stop the machine. It made the machine look less like a weapons lab on university letterhead. The lease on Page Mill Road still collected rent. Lockheed still filled parking lots in Sunnyvale. Fairchild's chips still rode Minuteman budgets.

The poster said strike against an empire. The trustees answered with a corporate sale to itself. The classified fraction went to Menlo Park. The quad got quieter. The procurement office kept buying.

April third named a coalition. January 1970 named a price. The episode ends with the work continuing somewhere else, on land the students could not occupy, under a name Stanford could disclaim.

The park remains. The creek remains. The street signs are twelve feet up and lit at night.

## The manoeuvre

What the trustees performed in 1970 was not reform and was not refusal. It was reorganisation: a change in the legal ownership of an activity, leaving the activity untouched and removing the party that could be held responsible for it.

The Contract and Finance Company in the railroad era had put profits inside an entity with no public obligations. The students who occupied the Applied Electronics Laboratory asked Stanford to own what it owned. The trustees answered by ceasing to own SRI.

Researchers left the AEL for SRI, taking some eight hundred thousand dollars in contracts and three hundred thousand in overhead with them. SRI closed its annex in the industrial park, which removed a target. The work continued four miles away, under a name Stanford could disclaim.

## SRI's position

SRI's own institutional history characterises the students' demands as "usually unfounded" and its chemical warfare work as "almost exclusively defensive." That is an interested party describing itself. It belongs in the record as SRI's position, not as the facts.

## The tear gas, restated

On 16 May 1969 police used tear gas to disperse students blocking traffic near the SRI building. They dispersed after about four hours. They were protesting the divestment.

The students had asked the university to take responsibility for what it owned. The trustees had responded by ceasing to own it. The students, who understood perfectly well what had happened, were tear-gassed while objecting to a decision that was formally a concession to them.

## Honest accounting of the protesters

These were not the powerless. They were students at one of the most expensive private universities in the United States, most of them from comfortable backgrounds, many of them shielded from the draft by the deferment their enrolment provided. They were the elite objecting from inside it.

The people actually on the receiving end of counterinsurgency research were in Southeast Asia and had no representation in Dinkelspiel Auditorium. The movement's success or failure made no difference to them that anyone has been able to trace. The conscience of the machine is still the machine.

## End turn

Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970, and the military work moves off-campus without slowing down. The rebellion did not stop the procurement office. It moved the letterhead. The lease on Page Mill still collected rent. Lockheed still filled Sunnyvale. Fairchild's chips still rode federal budgets.

April third named a coalition. January 1970 named a price. The episode ends with work continuing somewhere students could not occupy, under a name the trustees could disclaim — while the park, the creek, and the federal money kept flowing.

## 1965 and 1967

Concern surfaced in 1965 when campus learned Stanford-owned SRI held two contracts concerning chemical warfare. It intensified in 1967 when students learned SRI had taken Southeast Asia-related work; picketing began outside Menlo Park buildings that April.

## Budget as evidence

The students documented a change; they did not imagine it.

## Inside the building

They ran child care in one lab, a bookstore in another, rooms named for Cleaver and Guevara, a high school auxiliary in the basement, continuous open meetings. They used the laboratory printshop to publish *The Goods on AEL* and *Declassified*. They removed classification by printing on the equipment that classification protected.

## Winning on paper

Dean Pettit announced no further classified contracts. The academic senate on 24 April banished classified research from campus — a comprehensive victory on stated terms. Classified research at Stanford essentially ended in the form it took in 1968.

## January 1970

Trustees set terms: SRI sold to itself, one per cent of gross operating revenues up to twenty-five million dollars against book value near twenty-five and worth estimated near forty-five. Chemical warfare work did not stop. Counterinsurgency work did not stop. What stopped was Stanford's ownership and therefore any obligation to answer.

## Poster

“Strike Against the Stanford Empire” (Harris 2023, Section III frontispiece) was a title card waiting on a wall. The empire answered by changing letterhead.

## End

Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970. Military work moves off-campus without slowing. The rebellion made the machine look less like a weapons lab on university stationery. The lease on Page Mill still collected rent. Lockheed still filled Sunnyvale. The street signs stayed twelve feet up, lit at night, naming the men who built the sorting, the relief, the leases, and the labs.

It was never a secret. It was only subliminal.

## Nine days inside

**Day one.** The doors close at 10:45 a.m. Roughly two million dollars of classified Defense work stops because four hundred students walk in. Open meetings begin. Someone starts sorting papers.

**Day two.** The child care centre opens in a lab bay. Parents rotate shifts on the quad lawn. *Declassified* issue one comes off the printshop press.

**Day three.** The bookstore shelves fill with donated paperbacks. Rooms receive names — Cleaver, Guevara — that make the administration wince in memos Pitzer has not yet signed.

**Day four.** High school students from Palo Alto arrive for the auxiliary programme in the basement. Faculty supporters drop food at the loading dock without entering, a compromise between solidarity and tenure.

**Day five.** Engineering dean Pettit's staff read budget lines aloud at an open mic. Thirty-five per cent classified funding in electronics is no longer an abstraction. It is a number on a handout.

**Day six.** *The Goods on AEL* circulates in stapled stacks. Journalists call. The university press office says negotiations continue.

**Day seven.** Suspension letters are drafted. Pitzer does not want Columbia's photographs. He wants the building empty before trustees meet.

**Day eight.** Votes on whether to hold or leave. The coalition holds. Outside, pickets continue at SRI in Menlo Park where Stanford still owns the institute students want controlled.

**Day nine.** Students leave 18 April under suspension threat. The laboratory closes to everyone for a week — occupiers and researchers alike. Victory on paper begins: no new classified contracts, senate guidelines on 24 April.

**After.** January 1970: SRI sold to itself. May 16: tear gas for protesters of divestment. Contracts walk to Menlo Park with eight hundred thousand dollars. The poster said empire. The trustees changed corporate structure. The work continued.

## The hinge restated

They wanted Stanford to exercise more control over SRI. Trustees ceased to own SRI. Chemical warfare research did not end. Counterinsurgency research did not end. Classification did not end. Only the obligation to answer ended.

That is the episode's end turn: Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970, and military work moves off-campus without slowing down. The rebellion did not stop the machine. It made the machine look less like a weapons lab on university letterhead.

The street signs are twelve feet up and lit at night.
