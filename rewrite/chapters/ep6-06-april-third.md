# Episode 6: The Park

## April Third

**Stanford. 3 April 1969.**

Seven hundred people filled Dinkelspiel Auditorium. The factions — a campus chapter of Students for a Democratic Society, an SRI coalition, and others — reorganised into one body and took its name from the date.

On the wall outside, a poster read “Strike Against the Stanford Empire” (Harris 2023, Section III frontispiece).

Their demands were four, and the first is the hinge of this chapter.

They wanted the trustees to impose stricter guidelines on the Stanford Research Institute. They wanted an end to all classified research at the university and at SRI. They wanted an end to all chemical and biological warfare work and all counterinsurgency work at both institutions, whether classified or not. And they wanted a public meeting of the full board of trustees on the question.

Read the first demand again. They were asking the university to exercise *more* control over its research institute, not less. They wanted the institution to own what it owned.

Six days later, about four hundred students walked into the Applied Electronics Laboratory and stopped work.

## What they had been reading

The movement did not begin in 1969 and it did not begin with slogans. It began with people reading contracts.

Concern surfaced in 1965, when it became known on campus that the Stanford-owned Research Institute in Menlo Park held two contracts concerning chemical warfare. It intensified in 1967 when students learned that SRI had taken on work related to Southeast Asia, and picketing began outside the Menlo Park buildings that April (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`).

The numbers they assembled were the strongest part of their case. Across the fifteen years from 1950 to 1965, only nineteen of five hundred and eighty-two doctoral and engineering theses in Stanford's electrical engineering department had been classified, more than half of those during the Korean War. By 1968, close to thirty-five per cent of the department's electronics research funding was classified work, concentrated in the Applied Electronics Laboratory.

Something had changed, and it was legible in the budget. The students were not imagining a transformation of their university; they had documented one.

## Nine days inside the AEL

At a quarter to eleven on the morning of 9 April 1969, about four hundred Stanford students walked into the Applied Electronics Laboratory and stopped work.

Roughly two million dollars of classified Defense Department research was under way in the building. The students stayed nine days.

They ran a child care centre in one laboratory. They set up a bookstore in another, and named two rooms for Eldridge Cleaver and Che Guevara. A high school auxiliary operated in the basement. They held continuous open meetings and voted on everything.

And they used the laboratory's own printshop as a press. They went through the material they found, and published it: a booklet called *The Goods on AEL*, and a newsletter called *Declassified* that ran throughout the occupation.

Students in a building they had occupied, printing the classified research they found there, on the equipment they found there. The administration did not call the police into the AEL immediately. President Kenneth Pitzer sought an outcome that avoided the scenes at Columbia and Berkeley. Armed with emergency authority, he eventually threatened suspension, and the students left on 18 April. He then closed the laboratory to everybody, including its own staff, for a week.

## Winning on paper

The concessions came quickly.

Joseph Pettit, the dean of engineering, announced that the school would accept no further classified contracts and would wind down the ones it held. On 24 April the academic senate adopted guidelines that effectively banished classified research from the campus.

That is a comprehensive victory on the terms the movement had stated. Classified research at Stanford essentially ended in the form it took in 1968, and it has not returned in that form.

## The restructuring

Then the trustees answered the SRI question, and the answer is why this chapter exists.

A joint study committee of students, faculty, and administrators had been appointed to consider the relationship. In April 1969 nine of its twelve members recommended that Stanford sell the institute. SRI's book value was around twenty-five million dollars and its actual worth was estimated at something closer to forty-five. Some committee members wanted restrictive covenants attached to any sale, prohibiting chemical weapons and counterinsurgency work. A minority argued for the opposite course: that Stanford should take control and redirect the institute, because divesting it would not address the substance at all (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`).

In January 1970 the trustees set the terms. SRI would be sold to itself, paying one per cent of its gross operating revenues — which had been fifty-eight million dollars in 1969 — up to a total of twenty-five million.

The chemical warfare work did not stop. The counterinsurgency work did not stop. The classified contracts did not stop. It simply stopped being Stanford's.

Researchers left the Applied Electronics Laboratory for SRI, taking eight hundred thousand dollars in contracts with them. The work moved four miles to a building nobody could hold a sit-in about, because it no longer belonged to anyone the students could petition.

## Tear gas

On 16 May 1969, students blocking traffic near the SRI building were dispersed by police after four hours. They were protesting the divestment. They had asked the university to take responsibility; it had taken its name off the door instead.

## Who was in the room

These are not the powerless; they are the children of the elite, objecting from inside. The people actually on the receiving end of counterinsurgency research were in Vietnam and had no vote in Dinkelspiel Auditorium. The chapter is stronger for conceding that the conscience of the machine is still the machine (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`).

## SRI's position

SRI's own institutional history characterises the students' demands as "usually unfounded" and its chemical warfare work as "almost exclusively defensive." That is an interested party describing itself. It belongs in the record as SRI's position, not as the facts.

## The manoeuvre

What the trustees performed in 1970 was not reform and was not refusal. It was reorganisation: a change in the legal ownership of an activity, leaving the activity untouched and removing the party that could be held responsible for it.

The Contract and Finance Company in the railroad era had put profits inside an entity with no public obligations. The students who occupied the Applied Electronics Laboratory asked Stanford to own what it owned. The trustees answered by ceasing to own SRI.

Pressure produced a change of corporate structure rather than a change of activity. The same move would recur in later decades whenever a platform needed to keep the revenue stream while shedding the obligation to answer for it. This episode is the local proof.

## The deep cut in the same building

Doug Engelbart's 1968 demonstration at SRI — the mouse, hypertext, windowed displays — is known as the Mother of All Demos (Fisher 2018, preface). Levine places the same institute's ARPA chemical-warfare contract work in Vietnam alongside funding for Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center: SRI was “doing ARPA contract work on chemical warfare in Vietnam” while Licklider's office funded augmentation (Levine 2018, ARPA chapter). <!-- bib: TODO levine-surveillance-valley -->

The Mother of All Demos and napalm research in the same institution is the irony the campus would soon try to name. The April Third Movement did not invent the contradiction. It printed the budget lines.

## The end turn

Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970, and the military work moves off-campus without slowing down.

The rebellion did not stop the machine. It made the machine look less like a weapons lab on university letterhead. The lease on Page Mill Road still collected rent. Lockheed still filled parking lots in Sunnyvale. Fairchild's chips still rode defence budgets.

The poster said strike against an empire. The trustees answered with a corporate sale to itself. The classified fraction went to Menlo Park. The quad got quieter. The procurement office kept buying.

April third named a coalition. January 1970 named a price. The episode ends with the work continuing somewhere else, on land the students could not occupy, under a name Stanford could disclaim — while the park, the creek, and the federal money kept flowing.

Sand Hill Road is still empty in 1970. Kleiner and Perkins have not yet opened an office there. The next episode begins when venture capital puts a door on university land that the rebels could not close.

## The poster as title card

Harris opens Section III with the April Third Movement poster — “Strike Against the Stanford Empire” — because the document is already a scene: ink on paper, archive box, ready-made title card (Harris 2023, Section III frontispiece). The empire the students named was not abstract. It was SRI ownership, classified AEL budgets, Lockheed payrolls down the freeway, ground rent on Page Mill, and trustees who would rather sell an institute than govern it.

The strike did not stop the empire. It forced a rename. January 1970 priced the rename at one per cent of gross revenues up to twenty-five million dollars. The work continued in Menlo Park. The quad got quieter. The procurement office kept buying.

That is Episode 6's end turn, stated without the street-sign coda that belongs to the book's close: Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970, and the military work moves off-campus without slowing down.

## The coalition's four demands, restated

The April Third Movement's platform was not vague. Stricter trustee guidelines for SRI. An end to classified research at Stanford and SRI. An end to chemical and biological warfare work and counterinsurgency work at both institutions, classified or not. A public trustees' meeting (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`).

The first demand assumed ownership. The trustees' 1970 answer assumed divestiture. Students wanted the university to steer the institute it had founded in 1946. The board chose to stop being steerable.

## Nine days as logistics

The occupation ran like the relief operations Hoover had organised in another century: inventory, allocation, open books. Child care in one bay so parents could stay. A bookstore in another so information could circulate without the administration's press office. Rooms renamed for figures the trustees hated because naming is a cheap form of possession. A high school auxiliary because teenagers on campus made the building harder to treat as a mere machine shop.

*The Goods on AEL* and *Declassified* turned the laboratory's printshop into the movement's publishing arm. Classification is a rule about who may read. The students read anyway, then stapled copies for anyone who would take them.

Pitzer's restraint was tactical. Columbia's photographs were fresh in every dean's mind. Suspension letters were slower than tear gas and, in April, more effective. The building emptied on 18 April. The laboratory closed to everyone for a week — occupiers and researchers alike — which was both punishment and admission that the students had seized something real.

## Faculty senate, 24 April

The academic senate's guidelines on 24 April effectively banished classified research from campus. Dean Pettit's school would accept no further classified contracts and would wind down existing ones. On paper, the movement won everything it asked of the university proper.

Paper is where Stanford often settles arguments it does not want to lose in public.

## January 1970 arithmetic

SRI reported fifty-eight million dollars in gross operating revenues in 1969. The trustees set a self-purchase price capped at twenty-five million, payable at one per cent of gross until paid (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`). Chemical warfare contracts did not appear on the quad anymore. They appeared in Menlo Park under a name Stanford could disclaim.

Researchers carried eight hundred thousand dollars in contracts out of the Applied Electronics Laboratory when they walked to SRI. Overhead followed. The chokepoint moved four miles. The work did not slow.

## Tear gas as answer

On 16 May 1969 students blocked traffic near SRI to protest divestment — not occupation, but objection to the trustees' solution. Police dispersed them after four hours. Tear gas is what governments use when argument has already failed and movement must continue anyway.

The students had asked for responsibility. The board had offered a sale to itself. The gas was the punctuation mark on a sentence the trustees thought closed.

## Conscience inside the machine

The occupiers were elite students at an elite university, many shielded from the draft by enrolment. The people targeted by counterinsurgency research had no seat in Dinkelspiel. Naming that asymmetry does not discredit the movement's courage. It locates it accurately on the map this book has been drawing since the creek chapter.

## What changed and what did not

Classified research on campus essentially ended in the form it took in 1968. Defence procurement in the county did not end. Lockheed still employed more people than Fairchild and HP combined for years. The industrial park still collected ground rent. SRI still did contract work for agencies the students could name but not vote out.

Stanford cuts SRI loose in 1970, and the military work moves off-campus without slowing down. The rebellion did not stop the machine. It made the machine look less like a weapons lab on university letterhead — while the leases, the missiles, and the sorting geography kept running.
