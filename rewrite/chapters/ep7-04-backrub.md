# Episode 7: The Road

## BackRub

**Stanford, California. January 1997.**

Larry Page had been crawling the web from Stanford's servers for months. He called the project BackRub. That's why Page called his system BackRub. (Levy 2011, ch. 1) <!-- bib: TODO -->

The crawl consumed bandwidth the computer science department noticed. The results were better than anything else on the market. Page and Sergey Brin were graduate students; Stanford owned the machines; and the question of who would own the algorithm was already forming in the office of technology licensing.

In January 1997 Page emailed a plan to Vinod Khosla, then at Kleiner Perkins, proposing that Excite buy BackRub for $1.6 million. "Excite's total outlay would be $1.6 million, including $300,000 to Stanford for the license." (Levy 2011, ch. 1) <!-- bib: TODO -->

Excite passed.

Stanford's cut sat inside the pitch like a line item on a grocery receipt. The university was not an afterthought. It was part of the price.

## The insight

Search engines in 1997 ranked pages primarily by the text on them, which meant anyone could rank highly by repeating a word. PageRank used a different signal: the structure of links between pages. A page linked to by many pages is probably useful, and a link from a page that is itself heavily linked counts for more than one from a page nobody reads. It is a recursive measure of authority, and it was much harder to fake because it depended on the behaviour of other people. <!-- kyle: ch/4-12 -->

The results were obviously better, and anyone who used the web in 1998 remembers the difference.

## Stanford's position

The university owned the patent.

PageRank was developed on federally funded research at Stanford — the Digital Libraries project, supported by the National Science Foundation among others — and under the Bayh–Dole arrangement the university took title. It licensed the patent to the company, exclusively, in exchange for a fee and shares. <!-- kyle: ch/4-12 -->

Stanford sold most of those shares a few years after the 2004 public offering, for a sum in the region of three hundred million dollars.

That transaction is the entire book in a single line item. Federal research funding produces an invention; a statute assigns the invention to the university; the university licenses it to a company founded by its own doctoral students; the company becomes one of the most valuable enterprises on earth; and the university collects a nine-figure return. <!-- kyle: ch/4-12 -->

Public money in, private monopoly out, institution enriched, elite reproduced. There is no step in that sequence that is hidden, illegal, or even unusual.

## The paper

The paper that described Google contains an appendix explaining why Google should not exist.

Sergey Brin and Larry Page published "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine" in 1998. Its final appendix argues that advertising-funded search engines are inherently biased toward the advertisers and away from the needs of consumers, that the bias is insidious because it is difficult for users to detect, and that these incentives mean a search engine of this kind will be crafted to serve the advertiser rather than the searcher. <!-- kyle: ch/4-12 -->

They concluded that it is crucial to have a competitive search engine that is transparent and in the academic realm.

Then they built the largest advertising-funded search engine in history.

They would need a garage, venture capital, and a business model before the appendix caught up with them.
