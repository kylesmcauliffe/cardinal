Does the mechanism work outside technology?

In 1962 a graduate student at the Stanford Graduate School of Business wrote a
paper for an entrepreneurship class. The assignment was to produce a business
plan for a small company. Almost everyone in the room wrote about electronics,
because it was 1962 on the San Francisco Peninsula and electronics was the
subject.

Phil Knight knew nothing about electronics. He was a middle-distance runner from
Oregon with an accounting degree, and he wrote about shoes.

The paper's title was its argument: could Japanese sports shoes do to German
sports shoes what Japanese cameras had done to German cameras? Frank
Shallenberger gave it an A. Sixty years later the company it became was among
the most valuable consumer brands on earth, and its founder's name is on a
building at the school where he wrote it.

Knight has told the origin repeatedly and consistently, and the detail that
matters is that he overheard it.

He had worked at the *Oregon Journal*, and the photographers there complained
that their expensive German cameras — Leica, Zeiss — were being undercut by
Japanese equipment that was nearly as good and dramatically cheaper. The
complaint was about their own profession. Knight extracted a general proposition
from it: when manufacturing capability relocates and brand loyalty lags behind,
there is a window, and whoever holds distribution rights during the window
collects.

That is not an insight about shoes or cameras. It is an insight about the timing
of a chokepoint, and it is the same insight Sam Brannan had about shovels.

He chose athletic shoes because he knew the sport, because German firms —
principally Adidas — dominated it, and because he knew nothing about
electronics. The most consequential business decision of his life was made by
process of elimination.

He acted on it with a directness that is close to comic.

Travelling in Japan in November 1962, he telephoned Onitsuka in Kobe, makers of
the Tiger running shoe, and asked for a meeting. He was received. Partway
through the discussion he was asked which company he represented.

He had no company. He invented one on the spot and called it Blue Ribbon Sports,
after the ribbons on the wall of his childhood bedroom. He left with
distribution rights for the western United States.

In January 1964 he and Bill Bowerman — his coach at Oregon, who had been cutting
up shoes and rebuilding them for years in search of something lighter — each put
in five hundred dollars and ordered three hundred pairs. The partnership was
sealed with a handshake. Knight sold the first shoes out of the boot of a car at
track meets, and kept his accounting job for years while doing it.

Nike's first full-time employee, hired in 1965, was Jeff Johnson, a runner
Knight had met through Stanford intramural track. The network again, doing
what networks had always done on the Peninsula: lowering the cost of finding someone you
can trust.

Here is why this chapter is in this book rather than in a business history.

As Nike developed, it did not build factories. It kept design, brand, and
distribution, and contracted out production entirely. That decision — made
initially because Blue Ribbon was a small importer with no capital, and then
retained deliberately as the company grew — turned out to be the durable source
of its margins.

The parts Nike kept cannot be copied. A design language, an athlete endorsement
portfolio, and shelf position in every sports retailer on earth are assets that
compound and that a competitor cannot buy. The parts Nike contracted out can be
copied by anyone with capital and a workforce, which is precisely why they are
low-margin and why they should belong to somebody else.

That is the fabless model, and Nike reached it in canvas and rubber before the
semiconductor industry named it in silicon. When chip designers in the 1980s and
1990s began separating design from fabrication for the same reasons, they were
rediscovering a structure that a shoe company had been running for a decade.

Own the specification and the shelf. Contract the making.

The model requires the factory to move, and this is the part the chapter must
not soften.

Onitsuka's Japanese production became expensive as the yen strengthened and
Japanese wages rose. Production went to South Korea and Taiwan in the 1970s and
early 1980s. As those economies developed, it went to Indonesia, then to China,
then to Vietnam, which is now the largest single source of Nike footwear.

Each move followed the same arithmetic and each was executed through contractors
rather than owned subsidiaries, which meant the conditions inside the factories
were legally somebody else's responsibility.

Through the 1990s that arrangement produced sustained reporting on wages,
overtime, and safety in contract plants in Indonesia and Vietnam, a consumer
boycott campaign, and a period in which the brand Nike had built so carefully
was the principal liability. Knight said publicly in 1998 that the Nike product
had become synonymous with slave wages, forced overtime, and arbitrary abuse,
and the company subsequently built a monitoring and disclosure apparatus,
published its supplier list, and became by some measures a leader in supply
chain transparency.

The arm's-length arrangement is not a
lapse in the model. It is the model. A company that owns no factories has no
employees in them, and every improvement in conditions is therefore a voluntary
undertaking rather than an obligation, revocable and dependent on continued
attention.

This is precisely the Contract and Finance Company. Put a
legal boundary between the activity and the party benefiting from it, and the
benefit flows across the boundary while the liability does not.

It is worth being precise about what Nike actually owns, because the answer
explains the margins.

A running shoe is not technically difficult. The materials are available, the
construction is well understood, and the factories that make Nike's shoes make
other companies' shoes on adjacent lines. There is genuine engineering in the
product and it is not the source of the pricing power.

What Nike owns is a position in the culture, and it acquired it by buying
attention. The endorsement model — signing athletes, associating the mark with
achievement, and advertising heavily enough that the association becomes
automatic — was not invented by Nike but was executed by Nike at a scale nobody
had attempted. The Michael Jordan contract in 1984 is the canonical instance: a
signature line that became a business larger than most footwear companies, built
on a person.

The mark itself was designed in 1971 by a graphic design student at Portland
State for a reported thirty-five dollars.

That is a chokepoint on attention, and it is the same asset that the earlier platforms will monetise directly. Nike got there first by paying for it rather
than by owning the channel, and the arrangement has held for forty years because
attention, unlike manufacturing capacity, cannot be relocated to a cheaper
jurisdiction.

In 2011 the Knight Management Center opened at the Stanford Graduate School of
Business — a complex of buildings funded principally by a hundred-million-dollar
gift from the man who had written a class paper in that school in 1962.

Later came Knight-Hennessy Scholars, endowed with a further very large gift and
run with the university's former president, funding graduate students across the
university on an explicitly leadership-focused model.

Trace the circuit. A credential produces an idea. The idea produces a company.
The company's margins depend on contract labour eight thousand miles away. The
proceeds return to endow the institution that issued the credential, which then
produces the next cohort, in buildings carrying the donor's name.

Leland Stanford did this with a railroad in 1885. Phil Knight did it with shoes
in 2011.

The mechanism is not about technology. It never was. It is about who holds the
part that cannot be copied, and where the proceeds go afterwards.

## Washington

Ronald Reagan was elected in November 1980, and a substantial part of the
intellectual apparatus of his administration came from a building on the
Stanford campus.

The Hoover Institution had spent the decades since Hoover's defeat in 1932 assembling
what a defeated president had begun: a permanent body of scholars developing a
particular set of economic and foreign policy arguments, sustained through a
period when those arguments were entirely out of fashion.

Milton Friedman joined Hoover in 1977. George Shultz was there before becoming
Secretary of State in 1982 and returned afterwards. The Institution supplied
personnel, position papers, and intellectual standing to an administration
committed to deregulation, tax reduction, and a harder line in the Cold War.

That is what Herbert Hoover built the archive for. He founded it in 1919 to
collect the documentary record of how power moves; it became, over sixty years,
an instrument for moving it. Hoover had understood in 1919 that whoever gathers the documents acquires interpretive power. This is the interest payment.

The policy changes of the early 1980s reached the Valley directly.

Capital gains tax rates had been cut in 1978 and were cut again in 1981, which
raised the after-tax return on exactly the kind of long-horizon equity
investment that venture capital makes. Combined with the 1979 ERISA clarification, the effect on the amount of capital available to
venture funds was transformative, and the industry grew by an order of magnitude
across a few years.

Deregulation in telecommunications, finance, and transportation opened markets
that technology firms would later occupy. Defence spending rose sharply, which
flowed to the defence corridor of Sunnyvale and Mountain View.

The administration brought a second Californian export to Washington, and it had
been tested in the state a decade earlier.

Reagan had been governor of California from 1967 to 1975, and the politics that
carried him to Sacramento were substantially about the universities: the
Berkeley Free Speech Movement, the campus unrest of the late 1960s, and a promise to restore order. In office he presided over the beginning of
the end of free tuition in the California public system, which had been a
founding principle of the 1960 Master Plan.

That is a durable shift and it inverted the arrangement Terman had built. The G.I. Bill and the Master Plan had made higher
education a public good financed collectively. From the 1970s the cost migrated
to the student, through fees and then through debt, on the argument that the
private returns to a degree were substantial and the beneficiary should pay.

The argument has force. It also converts a credential from something a society
provides into something an individual purchases, which changes who can obtain
one and what they must do afterwards to service the purchase.

Proposition 13, passed by California voters in 1978, capped property taxes and
required supermajorities for new taxes, permanently constraining the revenue
base of the state's schools and local government. It was the most influential
state ballot measure in modern American history and it was widely imitated.

So the same decade that vastly increased the flow of public research money into
a private university's patent portfolio also began withdrawing public support
from the system that had made California's technical workforce possible. Both
movements originated in California and both went national.

What assembled in California in these years is a coherent position, and the
chapter's obligation is to describe it without sneering, because most of the
people holding it hold it sincerely.

It runs roughly as follows. Markets allocate better than governments. Regulation
protects incumbents and suppresses innovation. Individual technical talent is
the scarce resource and should be rewarded accordingly. The people who build
valuable things deserve what they earn, and the mechanism that identifies them
is approximately fair.

Set against that the material in this act. The semiconductor industry's early
volume came from federal procurement. The network came from a defence agency.
The research came from federal grants routed through a university, on land
granted to a railroad. The capital came substantially from pension funds
admitted by a regulatory reinterpretation. The patents on which the biotech
industry was built came from federally funded work, assigned to universities by
statute.

The contradiction is not hypocrisy, and calling it hypocrisy would be the lazy
version. It is something more interesting: a genuine belief in market
allocation, held by people whose own position was produced by an unusually large
and sustained programme of public investment, who did not experience that
investment as such because it arrived through institutions — a university, a
research contract, a pension allocation — rather than as a cheque.

Public subsidy that arrives through an institution is invisible to the person it
benefits. That is the most durable finding in this book.

## Bechtel

Caspar Weinberger became Secretary of Defense in 1981. George Shultz became Secretary of State in 1982. Both men had been executives at Bechtel, the San Francisco engineering firm that had grown on public-works contracts the way the railroad had grown on land grants. Shultz — "the outsider and former academic" — had become president of Bechtel a year after joining the company. <!-- bib: TODO -->

Shultz told his 1982 nomination hearing that "Private interests have no business with nuclear weapons." <!-- bib: TODO -->

Bechtel had business with nuclear weapons. The line is worth holding in mind.

The firm's social world included the Bohemian Grove, the private campground in Monte Rio where California's corporate and political leadership met each summer. Nixon's Watergate tapes record him calling the Grove "the most faggy goddamned thing you could ever imagine," in a passage that also describes Bechtel courting Shultz there. <!-- bib: TODO -->

One detail, sourced once, is enough. The point is not the campground. The point is that the cabinet and the contractor drank from the same cup.

Shultz would later chair the board of the Hoover Institution on the Stanford campus. Weinberger would help steer defence spending back toward the firms that had trained them. The road, the university, the archive, and the cabinet were the same circuit, opened wider in 1981.
