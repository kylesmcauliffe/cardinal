The bottleneck in artificial intelligence is not the idea, the model, or the
data. It is the hardware.

Models are copyable, and are copied within months; capable open-weight models
follow the frontier closely. Data is abundant and its marginal value falls.
Talent is mobile and extremely well paid precisely because it can leave.

What is scarce is compute — the specialised processors required to train large
models, and behind them the small number of facilities capable of fabricating
the chips at all. Training a frontier model requires capital expenditure at a
scale that only a handful of organisations on earth can contemplate, and that
scale has been rising rather than falling.

Follow the scarcity, as this book has done since a tin route in the Late Bronze
Age. Everything else in this industry is competitive. This is not.

The company that designs most of the relevant hardware was founded in 1993 in
Santa Clara, and spent twenty-five years selling graphics processors to people
who played video games.

Its architecture turned out to suit the mathematics of neural networks
extraordinarily well, and its CUDA software platform — released in 2007, long
before there was a market for it — meant that by the time deep learning became
important, essentially all of the research tooling had been written against
Nvidia's hardware. That software ecosystem is a switching cost, and it is at
least as important as the silicon.

The chapter states market share figures with a date and a definition or not at
all, because accelerator share depends entirely on how the market is drawn —
whether in-house designs by the cloud providers are counted, whether inference
is separated from training, whether revenue or units are measured. The drafts of
this project carried a round number with no source, and it has been removed.

What is durable: through the 2020s a single firm supplied the great majority of
merchant accelerators used for frontier model training, its allocation decisions
determined which laboratories could train what, and its market capitalisation
rose to a level that placed it among the most valuable companies ever listed.

The Standard Oil comparison is apt in the specific respect that matters. A
single firm astride a passage that everyone downstream must use, with pricing
power over an entire industry, is the same structural position — and the same
position the Central Pacific held over California freight in Act I chapter 9.
It is not apt in others: Nvidia does not own the refineries, competition exists,
and the position depends on continued technical leadership rather than on
infrastructure that cannot be duplicated.

Fabrication, from chapter 2 of this act, is the deeper constraint. Nvidia

manufacturing is concentrated in Taiwan.

ChatGPT was released on 30 November 2022 and reached a very large user base

The frequently cited figure of a hundred million users within two months

The clearest available test of whether governance structures constrain this
class of organisation ran for five days.

OpenAI had been founded in 2015 as a non-profit, on the explicit premise that
the development of artificial general intelligence was too consequential to be
governed by commercial incentives. It later added a capped-profit subsidiary to
raise capital, with the non-profit board retaining control. The structure was
unusual and was defended as a safeguard.

On 17 November 2023 the board removed the chief executive. Within days,
substantially all of the company's employees signed a letter threatening to
resign, the largest investor made its position clear, and a competitor offered
to hire the entire staff. On 21 November the chief executive was reinstated and
most of the board resigned.

The board did exactly what its charter empowered it to do, and it lost in under
a week to a combination of employee equity interests and investor pressure.

Whatever one thinks of the merits, the structural finding is unambiguous: a
governance mechanism designed specifically to constrain commercial logic in the
most consequential technology of the era was tested once and failed. The chapter
does not need to argue this. It happened.

Is this a conspiracy, or is it gravity?

The Introduction made three predictions and said the book would be wrong if they
failed. This is where the verdicts are returned.

*California's great fortunes should cluster around control of bottlenecks rather
than around invention.*

The evidence across five acts is that they do, and consistently enough that the
exceptions are worth noting individually.

Sam Brannan bought the shovels; James Marshall found the gold and died poor. The
Big Four were merchants who owned the only line, not engineers; Theodore Judah,
who surveyed the route and drafted the legislation, was bought out and died at
thirty-seven. Microsoft licensed the operating system and the hardware makers
competed their margins to nothing. Douglas Engelbart demonstrated the interface
of the next fifty years in 1968 at a federally funded institute and captured
none of it. Xerox invented the graphical workstation and could not sell it.
Google's technical contribution was real, and the money came from the auction
rather than from PageRank. Nvidia's position rests as much on a software
ecosystem accumulated over fifteen years as on the chips.

The genuine counter-examples exist and should be stated. Hewlett-Packard, Intel,
Genentech, and Apple's hardware businesses were built substantially on products
rather than on positions, at least initially. The pattern is strong, not
universal.

**Verdict: supported.**

*Public investment should precede private capture rather than follow it.*

The land grants and thirty-year bonds of the Pacific Railway Act. The mission
system's imperial subsidy. Federal purchasing of the entire early output of the
integrated circuit industry. ARPANET. The National Science Foundation backbone,
transferred to commercial operators in 1995 without compensation. Federally
funded research assigned to universities by Bayh-Dole and licensed onward. The
1979 ERISA reinterpretation that admitted pension capital to venture funds. The
CHIPS Act.

In every case public money or public authority came first and private ownership
followed, and in most cases the transfer was accomplished through an instrument
rather than a payment.

**Verdict: supported, and more strongly than the first.**

*The institutions that produce the elite should be endowed by the elite they
produced.*

The Founding Grant of 1885, from a railroad fortune. Hoover's archive in 1919,
from a mining fortune, by a graduate of the first class. The Knight Management
Center in 2011, from a footwear fortune, by a man who wrote the company as a
class paper in the school the building houses. Named buildings, institutes,
professorships, and fellowship programmes across the campus, funded by graduates
whose companies the university helped create and in several cases holds equity
in.

**Verdict: supported.**

A book that only reports confirmations is a polemic. Here is where this one is
most exposed.

**The Santa Clara headnote.** Act I chapter 10 gives prominence to an episode
that specialists consider substantially overstated. Corporate personhood
developed over decades through decided cases, and the headnote did not create
it. The chapter concedes this and still uses the episode, and a reader is
entitled to think it is doing more work than the evidence supports.

**Selection versus causation.** The book's central structural claim is that the
corridor persists because chokepoints reproduce there. The competing explanation
is that talent and capital cluster for straightforwardly productive reasons —
agglomeration effects, thick labour markets, tacit knowledge — and that the
chokepoint reading is a moralised description of an ordinary economic
phenomenon. This is the strongest objection in existence to this book and it
cannot be fully answered, because the two explanations predict most of the same
observations.

**Single-source chapters.** Several chapters rest more heavily than is ideal on
one account, particularly in Act I and in the parts of Act III that depend on
institutional histories written by participants.

**Counterfactuals.** The book argues that secession chose the railroad route and
that the route created the Big Four and therefore Stanford. That chain is well
evidenced at each link and is still a chain, and chains of counterfactuals get
weaker with length.

Stated fairly: California's corridor produced the transistor, the integrated
circuit, the personal computer, the internet's commercialisation, modern
biotechnology, and the current generation of machine learning. Whatever the
distributional consequences, the productive achievement is real, enormous, and
has raised living standards worldwide. Talent clusters because clustering works,
capital follows returns, and describing this as chokepoint rent extraction is a
way of not saying that the place is unusually good at making things.

The answer this book gives is that both are true and they are not in tension.
Agglomeration is real and it explains why an industry concentrates somewhere. It
does not explain why the surplus accrues to the position rather than to the
invention, why public money precedes private capture with such regularity, or
why the institution that certifies the participants is endowed by them. Those
are distributional facts, and they are what the book is about.

The Introduction set this book against Max Weber, who argued in 1905 that
capitalism's animating spirit was Protestant asceticism — worldly discipline
read as evidence of divine election.

The point of the doctrine was never economic. Its function was moral: it made
prosperity legible as virtue, so that a man who had accumulated a great deal
could understand the accumulation as a sign rather than as an appetite. It
answered the question every durable system of inequality has to answer, which is
why the winner deserved to win.

California's version keeps the structure and inverts the content. Not thrift but
leverage. Not the monastery but the garage. Not election but merit — a story in
which the fortune is evidence of insight, nerve, and willingness to work in a
shed.

And the garage story does exactly the work the doctrine of election used to do.
It is sincerely held, it is frequently true at the level of the individual, and
it systematically obscures the thing this book has spent five acts documenting:
that the shed was near a university with unsellable land, that the university
was endowed by a railroad built with public money, that the customer for the
first products was the federal government, and that the professor who told them
to stay had a written plan.

So the answer to the Introduction's question is that California's spirit of
capitalism is chokepoint rent extraction wearing frontier merit as a costume.
Not because the people in it are frauds — most of them are not — but because a
system that requires an account of its own justice will generate one, and the
one it generates will be the flattering version.

Weber thought the spirit came first and the accumulation followed. In California
it runs the other way. The accumulation came first, and the spirit was assembled
afterwards to explain it.

There was no conspiracy because none was required, and this is the conclusion
the whole book has been arriving at.

Land, research funding, legitimacy, and network compound on each other without
anyone intending an outcome. A dean concentrates resources where federal money
is going. A landowner leases to tenants who will hire his graduates. An investor
funds the founder he already knows. A donor endows the school that certified
him. Each decision is individually rational, locally defensible, and entirely
unremarkable, and the aggregate is a machine that reproduces an elite across a
hundred and forty years.

That is a more troubling finding than a conspiracy, and it is the true one. A
conspiracy has participants who can be prosecuted, documents that can be
subpoenaed, and a decision that could have gone the other way. This has none of
those. It has gravity.

A programme.

The Voice Guide of this project forbids moralising, and there is a better reason
than style. Every remedy that suggests itself — antitrust, public ownership of
research outputs, a common carrier classification for platforms, taxation of
endowments, reclassification of contract workers — is a live political argument
on which reasonable people disagree, and none of them follows automatically from
the analysis.

What the analysis supports is narrower and more useful. If the mechanism is
structural rather than intentional, then remedies aimed at individuals will not
work, and remedies aimed at structures might. That is the whole of the advice.

The rest is the reader's, and the last two chapters are about learning to see
it.
