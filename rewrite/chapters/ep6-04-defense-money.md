# Episode 6: The Park

## Defense Money

**Sunnyvale. 1956.**

Lockheed Missiles and Space Division opened beside Moffett Field while Shockley was still hiring in Mountain View. The building went up fast — steel, concrete, parking lots sized for shift changes — and the job postings asked for engineers who could work under security clearance and who did not mind that their product would never appear in a store.

The largest employer in Santa Clara County for most of the 1960s and 1970s would be Lockheed. Almost nobody tells the story of Silicon Valley this way, and the omission is instructive. Lockheed made no consumer product, had one customer, and operated substantially in secret. It employed more people in the Valley than Hewlett-Packard and Fairchild combined for years, and it does not appear in the origin story at all, because the origin story is about garages and Lockheed was a procurement relationship (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

## Lockheed

Lockheed Missiles and Space Division opened in Sunnyvale in 1956, adjacent to Moffett Field, and grew very fast on the Polaris submarine-launched ballistic missile programme and on classified satellite reconnaissance work. Employment reached the tens of thousands. It was, by a wide margin, the region's dominant industrial enterprise (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

The figure is deliberately vague in the public record because much of the work was classified, but the scale is not ambiguous: tens of thousands of jobs in one division, more than HP and Fairchild combined for years, in a county that would later prefer to narrate itself through garages on Addison Avenue.

Polaris and the reconnaissance programmes were not consumer products. They were procurement relationships with one customer that tolerated secrecy. That customer paid for parking lots, shift work, and suppliers in instrument makers and semiconductor fabs before any Japanese radio mattered to the regional balance sheet.

## Fairchild's first customer

Fairchild Semiconductor's early revenue did not come from radios or televisions. It came from programmes where weight and reliability mattered more than unit cost: the Minuteman missile guidance system and the Apollo Guidance Computer, among other defence and aerospace programmes (`books/cardinal/ch/4-03-fairchild-platform-company.qmd`).

Federal purchasing in the early 1960s absorbed the great majority of American integrated-circuit production. By guaranteeing volume at prices commercial markets would not have paid, the Defense Department and NASA pulled chips down the learning curve until consumer applications became thinkable. The consumer semiconductor industry exists because the public bought the first years of output at whatever it cost. That subsidy rarely appears in garage mythology.

Shockley's recruits and Fairchild's spinoffs sit on the same map as Lockheed's shifts. The transistor arrived because a Nobel laureate wanted to live near his mother. The missile division arrived because the Pacific theatre had taught the federal government where to put logistics and labs. The chips connected the two stories in billing departments nobody put on postcards.

## SRI

The Stanford Research Institute had been founded by the university in 1946 as a non-profit to conduct applied research for industry and government.

The ownership relationship is worth stating precisely, because a later chapter turns on it entirely: SRI was established by Stanford's trustees, and Stanford owned it. It was located in Menlo Park, a few miles from the campus, and it grew through the 1950s and 1960s on contract research, a substantial and increasing proportion of it for military and intelligence agencies (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

By the late 1960s that work included research on chemical and biological warfare and on counterinsurgency techniques relevant to the war in Vietnam, and the university's ownership of the institute doing it was a matter of public record.

Levine's account of the same building makes the juxtaposition plain: SRI was “doing ARPA contract work on chemical warfare in Vietnam” while J. C. R. Licklider's successors funded Douglas Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center (Levine 2018, ARPA chapter). <!-- bib: TODO levine-surveillance-valley -->

The Mother of All Demos and chemical-warfare contracts under one institute's roof is the deep cut Episode 6 owes the record: federally funded interface dreams and federally funded weapons research, Stanford-owned, Menlo Park address, students unable to read either ledger until they occupied a laboratory on campus and printed what they found.

## Ames and the suppliers

NASA Ames Research Center sat at Moffett Field in Mountain View, inheriting an aeronautics establishment that dated to the 1930s — built for airships, repurposed for jets, tunnels, computing, and eventually space science (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

Around Lockheed, SRI, and Ames accumulated electronics firms, instrument makers, and the semiconductor companies from the previous chapters. HP's oscillators and Varian's klystrons fed radar and microwave budgets before living rooms mattered. None of them needed a consumer market in the 1960s to survive. They needed a customer with a procurement office and a tolerance for secrecy.

Follow the revenue across the region in 1965 and the picture is unambiguous. Defence and aerospace purchasing was the dominant source of demand. The consumer market for electronics existed but was small and was served largely by Japanese manufacturers. Silicon Valley was a military-industrial cluster with a university attached, and it was that for roughly a quarter of a century (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

## What the arrangement produced

The chapter should give the arrangement its due before turning on it, because the output was extraordinary.

Satellite reconnaissance, developed in secret at Lockheed and elsewhere, gave the United States and eventually the Soviet Union the ability to verify what the other side actually had — which made arms control agreements possible and removed a category of catastrophic misjudgment. The Corona programme's imagery established in the early 1960s that the missile gap the 1960 election had been fought over did not exist (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

Integrated circuits reached volume production because missile and space programmes bought them at any price. Computing, satellite navigation, digital signal processing, and the network itself all came out of the same channel.

None of this would have been funded by a private market on the timescales involved, because the returns were decades away and unappropriable. The case for the arrangement is strong. The chapter's argument is not that public research funding is a mistake. It is that this particular arrangement routed the benefits in a specific direction, and the direction was not chosen by anyone who could be voted out.

## ARPANET

The first message sent over the ARPANET went from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park on 29 October 1969. The sender typed L, then O, and the system crashed.

The internet's first word was "lo".

It was sent between two Defense Department contractors, over a network the Defense Department had commissioned, to a node at an institute owned by Stanford University (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

The Advanced Research Projects Agency had been created in 1958 in response to Sputnik, and its Information Processing Techniques Office funded computing research at universities and institutes across the country. Those installations had incompatible machines and no way to share resources, and connecting them was a practical problem worth money.

The first four nodes were UCLA, SRI, the University of California at Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah. The technical approach — packet switching, with no central control — had been developed independently by Paul Baran at RAND in Santa Monica and Donald Davies in Britain.

Baran's motivation at RAND had included survivability of communications under nuclear attack. The frequently repeated claim that the internet was designed to survive a nuclear war is an overstatement the people involved have spent decades correcting. Survivability was one strand in the intellectual origins of packet switching, and the ARPANET itself was built to share expensive computers between Defense contractors.

The chapter describes it as a procurement rather than as a revelation. Mythology prefers birth moments. The record prefers contract lines.

## Engelbart

The other thing happening at SRI in the 1960s was Douglas Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center, federally funded from the same sources as the institute's other contract work.

In December 1968, at a conference in San Francisco, Engelbart gave a demonstration that has been known ever since as the Mother of All Demos (Fisher 2018, preface). In ninety minutes he showed a live networked computer system with a mouse, a windowed display, hypertext links, real-time collaborative document editing, video conferencing, and version control. In 1968.

Almost none of it was commercialised by SRI. The people went on to Xerox PARC, a few miles away. The mouse patent was assigned to SRI, which licensed it to Apple years later for a modest sum (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

Federal money, a Stanford-owned institute, a working demonstration of the interface every subsequent computer would use, and no commercial capture whatsoever. The invention and the fortune are separated by fifteen years and two changes of ownership — a pattern worth remembering when the industry explains itself through garages.

The same institute, on Levine's account, was “doing ARPA contract work on chemical warfare in Vietnam” while Engelbart's center received augmentation funding (Levine 2018, ARPA chapter). The demo and the contracts share an address. Stanford owned the institute. Students would soon ask the university to answer for both.

## Classification and land

Classification covered the Lockheed work almost entirely, a growing share of SRI's contracts, and a substantial fraction of Stanford's own engineering research, including the Applied Electronics Laboratory, which by the late 1960s was the campus centre of classified work (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

There was also a specific local grievance about land. Stanford had leased large tracts of the industrial park to defence contractors, and the university's research budget had come to depend substantially on classified contracts. The institution that had been endowed to serve the children of California was, by the late 1960s, deriving a significant part of its income from work its own students were not permitted to read.

Terman and his generation regarded this as unremarkable — wartime practice continued into a cold war that showed no sign of ending. But the arrangement had a constituency it had not consulted, and that constituency was living on the campus in increasing numbers and had begun reading the budget.

## Budget lines the students will read

By 1968 close to thirty-five per cent of Stanford's electrical engineering electronics research funding was classified, concentrated in the Applied Electronics Laboratory (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`). Lockheed payrolls, SRI contracts, ground rent on Page Mill, and chip sales into guidance systems all drew on the same federal stream.

When students occupied the AEL in April 1969, they were not imagining a militarised university. They had documented a change in the budget. This chapter names the substrate so the occupation reads as response, not tantrum.

## The corridor on the map

By the late 1960s the map is a corridor: Stanford's park on Page Mill, HP and Varian as tenants, Fairchild and Shockley in Mountain View, Lockheed in Sunnyvale, SRI in Menlo Park, Ames at Moffett. Federal money runs through every pin.

The university collects rent, tuition, and overhead on work it often cannot describe to its own students. The semiconductor firms sell chips into guidance systems. The research institute tests ideas the Pentagon wants tested.

This is the substrate under the story about entrepreneurs. It was built before the traitorous eight incorporated, and it would outlast their argument with Shockley.

## The forgotten employer, restated

Hewlett-Packard and Fairchild matter in legend. Lockheed mattered in payroll — tens of thousands in Sunnyvale against hundreds in a garage on Addison Avenue, for years (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). Both were true on the same map. Only one fit the origin story the region preferred to tell about itself.

The defence corridor does not end in April 1969. It changes legal address when SRI is sold to itself and contracts walk from the quad to Menlo Park. The procurement office keeps buying. The internet's first word remains "lo," typed before the crash, between nodes the Defense Department commissioned on an institute Stanford still owned when the packet left UCLA.

In April 1969 several hundred students will walk into the Applied Electronics Laboratory and shut it down. Chapter 6's last file is what they do with what they read. This file is what was already on the map when they started reading.

## The park's defence face

The Stanford Industrial Park's early tenants were not consumer companies, and the chapter should keep Act II's substrate visible through the 1960s (`books/cardinal/ch/4-01-termans-garden.qmd`).

Varian made klystrons and microwave equipment, and its customers were military. Hewlett-Packard's instrument business depended heavily on defence and aerospace purchasing — the same oscillators that had sold to Walt Disney for *Fantasia* within two years found a customer base substantially military (`books/cardinal/ch/3-09-total-war-filter.qmd`). Lockheed's missile division arrived in Sunnyvale in 1956 on the same federal income stream that bought chips from Fairchild once the traitorous eight incorporated.

The private-sector face of the arrangement sat on a public-sector revenue line. Federal procurement paid for most of it, and the park was the mechanism by which that money became a real estate portfolio, a graduate programme, and a set of private fortunes on long ground leases the Founding Grant made possible.

## Act II's promise kept

Episode 5 ended with Terman returning from Harvard's Radio Research Laboratory with a plan: steeples of excellence, federal money, unsellable land leased to companies that hired Stanford graduates. Episode 6's Shockley and Fairchild chapters tell a founder story. This chapter tells the payroll story underneath it.

Lockheed's tens of thousands. SRI's contracts. Ames's tunnels. ARPANET's first packet to Menlo Park. Engelbart's demo on the same institute's books as chemical-warfare research, on Levine's account. The garage on Addison Avenue is real. It is not the largest building on the map.

## Grievance scheduled

Terman and his generation regarded classified university research as unremarkable — wartime practice extended into a cold war that showed no sign of ending (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). By the late 1960s students living on the campus in increasing numbers had begun reading the budget: nineteen classified engineering theses from 1950 to 1965 against thirty-five per cent classified electronics funding by 1968, concentrated in the Applied Electronics Laboratory (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`).

The corridor did not surprise them. It angered them because they could document it — Lockheed at one end of the county, Stanford-owned SRI at another, classified work on the quad between, ground rent from defence tenants paying the endowment while the Applied Electronics Laboratory's printshop still worked.

The defence money chapter ends where the April Third chapter begins: with numbers on hand and a building to walk into.

## SRI in 1946

When Stanford's trustees chartered the Stanford Research Institute in 1946, the point was applied research at scale — industry and government contracts a campus department could not carry alone (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). Menlo Park put the work a few miles from the quad, under the same ownership that would later make student protests a trustees' problem rather than a contractor's problem.

By the 1960s the institute was a major node in the defence corridor: ARPANET endpoint, Engelbart's augmentation lab, and — on Levine's account — ARPA chemical-warfare contract work in Vietnam alongside that augmentation funding (Levine 2018, ARPA chapter). Stanford owned the institute on paper. The Pentagon owned much of the revenue. The students owned the anger once they read the ledgers.

## Closing the garage myth, opening the payroll

Silicon Valley's origin story compresses a quarter-century of procurement into a garage photograph. The payroll record compresses differently: Lockheed employing more people than HP and Fairchild combined; SRI growing on contracts Stanford could not fully disclose; Fairchild's chips sold into Minuteman and Apollo before they sold into calculators (`books/cardinal/ch/4-03-fairchild-platform-company.qmd`; `books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

Both compressions are true at the scale they choose. This chapter chooses the payroll scale because Episode 6's last scene is students occupying a laboratory over classified defence electronics — not over a resistance-capacitance oscillator, but over the same federal stream that built Sunnyvale's parking lots and Menlo Park's nodes.

The internet's first word was "lo." The county's first word for decades was classified. The students in 1969 would try to make the second word public without stopping the first stream of money.
