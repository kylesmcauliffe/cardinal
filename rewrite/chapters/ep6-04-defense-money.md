# Episode 6: The Park

## Defense Money

**Sunnyvale. 1956.**

Lockheed Missiles and Space Division opened beside Moffett Field while Shockley was still hiring in Mountain View. The building went up fast — steel, concrete, parking lots sized for shift changes — and the job postings asked for engineers who could work under security clearance and who did not mind that their product would never appear in a store.

The largest employer in Santa Clara County for most of the 1960s and 1970s would be Lockheed. Almost nobody tells the story of Silicon Valley this way, and the omission is instructive. Lockheed made no consumer product, had one customer, and operated substantially in secret. It employed more people in the Valley than Hewlett-Packard and Fairchild combined for years, and it does not appear in the origin story at all, because the origin story is about garages and Lockheed was a procurement relationship.

## Polaris and after

Lockheed Missiles and Space Division opened in Sunnyvale in 1956, adjacent to Moffett Field, and grew very fast on the Polaris submarine-launched ballistic missile programme and on classified satellite reconnaissance work. Employment reached the tens of thousands. It was, by a wide margin, the region's dominant industrial enterprise.

The Minuteman missile guidance system and the Apollo Guidance Computer would become early bulk customers for Fairchild's integrated circuits. Federal purchasing in the early 1960s absorbed the great majority of American integrated circuit production and, by guaranteeing volume, drove the price down the learning curve to the point where commercial applications became viable.

Defence and aerospace purchasing was the dominant source of demand across the region in 1965. The consumer market for electronics existed but was small and was served largely by Japanese manufacturers. Silicon Valley was a military-industrial cluster with a university attached, and it was that for roughly a quarter of a century.

## SRI

The Stanford Research Institute had been founded by the university in 1946 as a non-profit to conduct applied research for industry and government.

The ownership relationship is worth stating precisely, because a later chapter turns on it entirely: SRI was established by Stanford's trustees, and Stanford owned it. It was located in Menlo Park, a few miles from the campus, and it grew through the 1950s and 1960s on contract research, a substantial and increasing proportion of it for military and intelligence agencies.

By the late 1960s that work included research on chemical and biological warfare and on counterinsurgency techniques relevant to the war in Vietnam, and the university's ownership of the institute doing it was a matter of public record.

## Ames and the suppliers

NASA Ames Research Center sat at Moffett Field in Mountain View, inheriting an aeronautics establishment that dated to the 1930s and adding wind tunnels, computing, and eventually space science.

Around these anchors accumulated the suppliers: electronics firms, instrument makers, and the semiconductor companies from the previous chapter, whose early volume came from missile and space programmes.

Follow the revenue across the region and the picture is unambiguous. The garage sits at the centre of the Valley's story about itself; the procurement office is what actually happened.

## ARPANET

The first message sent over the ARPANET went from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park on 29 October 1969. The sender typed L, then O, and the system crashed.

The internet's first word was "lo".

It was sent between two Defense Department contractors, over a network the Defense Department had commissioned, to a node at an institute owned by Stanford University.

The Advanced Research Projects Agency had been created in 1958 in response to Sputnik, and its Information Processing Techniques Office funded computing research at universities and institutes across the country. The first four nodes were UCLA, SRI, the University of California at Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

The chapter describes it as a procurement rather than as a revelation. Connecting expensive computers was a practical problem worth money.

## Engelbart

The other thing happening at SRI in the 1960s was Douglas Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center, federally funded from the same sources.

In December 1968, at a conference in San Francisco, Engelbart gave a demonstration that has been known ever since as the Mother of All Demos. In ninety minutes he showed a live networked computer system with a mouse, hypertext, windowed displays, and collaborative editing.

The interface ideas in that demo reappear later at Xerox PARC and then in a personal computer the public could buy. The demo is remembered as a birth moment. It was also a deliverable on a contract.

## Classification

Classification covered the Lockheed work almost entirely, a growing share of Stanford's electronics research, and much of what SRI did in Menlo Park. The public paid for the research and was not permitted to examine it.

Some of the people on the Stanford campus were beginning to work out what their university was building. In two chapters they will occupy a laboratory over it.

## The corridor

By the late 1960s the map is a corridor: Stanford's park on Page Mill, HP and Varian as tenants, Fairchild and Shockley in Mountain View, Lockheed in Sunnyvale, SRI in Menlo Park, Ames at Moffett. Federal money runs through every pin.

The university collects rent, tuition, and overhead on work it often cannot describe to its own students. The semiconductor firms sell chips into guidance systems. The research institute tests ideas the Pentagon wants tested.

This is the substrate under the story about entrepreneurs. It was built before the traitorous eight incorporated, and it would outlast their argument with Shockley.

The defence corridor does not end when students take a building in April 1969. It changes address.

## ARPANET as procurement

The technical approach — packet switching, with no central control — had been developed independently by Paul Baran at RAND in Santa Monica and Donald Davies in Britain. Baran's motivation at RAND had included survivability of communications under nuclear attack. The frequently repeated claim that the internet was designed to survive a nuclear war is an overstatement the people involved have spent decades correcting. Survivability was one strand in the intellectual origins of packet switching, and the ARPANET itself was built to share expensive computers.

## Shift change, Sunnyvale, 1962

Cars pour out of a parking lot sized for a small city. Badge holders do not discuss the work. Minuteman guidance needs chips Fairchild fabricates. SRI nodes wait for ARPANET packets. Engelbart's team upstairs from a contract drawer students cannot read. Stanford owns SRI on paper in 1962. Students have not yet read the chemical warfare clauses. They will. The Applied Electronics Laboratory is filling with classified electronics funding. Thirty-five per cent classified by 1968. In 1962 the slope is visible only if you read budgets.

Moffett Field blimps are gone; jets and tunnels remain. Ames wind tunnels test what Lockheed sells. HP on Page Mill sells instruments into the same budgets. Varian's klystrons feed radar chains. The university cashes ground rent and tuition and overhead. The freeway fills. East Palo Alto watches headlights from the other side of the water.

This is Silicon Valley in the years the origin story skips. No consumer hero. One customer. Secrecy as default. The internet's first word will be "lo" because a UCLA student types two letters before crash. The node in Menlo Park belongs to an institute Stanford still owns. The chapter ends pointing at students who will walk through a door in 1969 because they read this map in numbers.
