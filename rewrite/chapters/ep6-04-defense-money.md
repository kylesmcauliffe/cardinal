# Episode 6: The Park

## Defense Money

**Sunnyvale. 1956.**

Lockheed Missiles and Space Division opened beside Moffett Field while Shockley was still hiring in Mountain View. The building went up fast — steel, concrete, parking lots sized for shift changes — and the job postings asked for engineers who could work under security clearance and who did not mind that their product would never appear in a store.

The largest employer in Santa Clara County for most of the 1960s and 1970s would be Lockheed. Almost nobody tells the story of Silicon Valley this way, and the omission is instructive. Lockheed made no consumer product, had one customer, and operated substantially in secret. It employed more people in the Valley than Hewlett-Packard and Fairchild combined for years, and it does not appear in the origin story at all, because the origin story is about garages and Lockheed was a procurement relationship.

## Polaris and after

Lockheed Missiles and Space Division opened in Sunnyvale in 1956, adjacent to Moffett Field, and grew very fast on the Polaris submarine-launched ballistic missile programme and on classified satellite reconnaissance work. Employment reached the tens of thousands. It was, by a wide margin, the region's dominant industrial enterprise.

[TK: Lockheed Sunnyvale employment figures — primary source beyond Harris ch. 3.1]

The Minuteman missile guidance system and the Apollo Guidance Computer would become early bulk customers for Fairchild's integrated circuits. Federal purchasing in the early 1960s absorbed the great majority of American integrated circuit production and, by guaranteeing volume, drove the price down the learning curve to the point where commercial applications became viable.

Defence and aerospace purchasing was the dominant source of demand across the region in 1965. The consumer market for electronics existed but was small and was served largely by Japanese manufacturers. Silicon Valley was a military-industrial cluster with a university attached, and it was that for roughly a quarter of a century.

## SRI

The Stanford Research Institute had been founded by the university in 1946 as a non-profit to conduct applied research for industry and government.

The ownership relationship is worth stating precisely, because a later chapter turns on it entirely: SRI was established by Stanford's trustees, and Stanford owned it. It was located in Menlo Park, a few miles from the campus, and it grew through the 1950s and 1960s on contract research, a substantial and increasing proportion of it for military and intelligence agencies.

By the late 1960s that work included research on chemical and biological warfare and on counterinsurgency techniques relevant to the war in Vietnam, and the university's ownership of the institute doing it was a matter of public record.

## Ames and the suppliers

NASA Ames Research Center sat at Moffett Field in Mountain View, inheriting an aeronautics establishment that dated to the 1930s and adding wind tunnels, computing, and eventually space science.

Around these anchors accumulated the suppliers: electronics firms, instrument makers, and the semiconductor companies from the previous chapter, whose early volume came from missile and space programmes.

Follow the revenue across the region and the picture is unambiguous. The garage sits at the centre of the Valley's story about itself; the procurement office is what actually happened.

## ARPANET

The first message sent over the ARPANET went from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute in Menlo Park on 29 October 1969. The sender typed L, then O, and the system crashed.

The internet's first word was "lo".

It was sent between two Defense Department contractors, over a network the Defense Department had commissioned, to a node at an institute owned by Stanford University.

The Advanced Research Projects Agency had been created in 1958 in response to Sputnik, and its Information Processing Techniques Office funded computing research at universities and institutes across the country. The first four nodes were UCLA, SRI, the University of California at Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

The chapter describes it as a procurement rather than as a revelation. Connecting expensive computers was a practical problem worth money.

## Engelbart

The other thing happening at SRI in the 1960s was Douglas Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center, federally funded from the same sources.

In December 1968, at a conference in San Francisco, Engelbart gave a demonstration that has been known ever since as the Mother of All Demos. In ninety minutes he showed a live networked computer system with a mouse, hypertext, windowed displays, and collaborative editing.

The interface ideas in that demo reappear later at Xerox PARC and then in a personal computer the public could buy. The demo is remembered as a birth moment. It was also a deliverable on a contract.

## Classification

Classification covered the Lockheed work almost entirely, a growing share of Stanford's electronics research, and much of what SRI did in Menlo Park. The public paid for the research and was not permitted to examine it.

Some of the people on the Stanford campus were beginning to work out what their university was building. In two chapters they will occupy a laboratory over it.

## The corridor

By the late 1960s the map is a corridor: Stanford's park on Page Mill, HP and Varian as tenants, Fairchild and Shockley in Mountain View, Lockheed in Sunnyvale, SRI in Menlo Park, Ames at Moffett. Federal money runs through every pin.

The university collects rent, tuition, and overhead on work it often cannot describe to its own students. The semiconductor firms sell chips into guidance systems. The research institute tests ideas the Pentagon wants tested.

This is the substrate under the story about entrepreneurs. It was built before the traitorous eight incorporated, and it would outlast their argument with Shockley.

The defence corridor does not end when students take a building in April 1969. It changes address.

## Fairchild's first customer

Fairchild Semiconductor's early revenue did not come from radios or televisions. It came from programmes where weight and reliability mattered more than unit cost: missile guidance and spaceflight (`books/cardinal/ch/4-03-fairchild-platform-company.qmd`). [TK: Minuteman program detail from Leslie, *The Cold War and American Science*]

Federal purchasing in the early 1960s absorbed the great majority of American integrated-circuit production. By guaranteeing volume at prices commercial markets would not have paid, the Defense Department and NASA pulled chips down the learning curve until consumer applications became thinkable. The consumer semiconductor industry exists because the public bought the first years of output at whatever it cost. That subsidy rarely appears in garage mythology.

## Satellite reconnaissance

Satellite reconnaissance, developed in secret at Lockheed and elsewhere, gave the same customer another reason to fund electronics at scale without advertising the product (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). Classification covered most Lockheed work, a growing share of Stanford's electronics research, and much of what SRI did in Menlo Park. The public paid and could not examine.

## Packet switching without mythology

The technical approach behind ARPANET — packet switching without central control — had been developed independently by Paul Baran at RAND and Donald Davies in Britain. Baran's work at RAND included survivability of communications under nuclear attack. The claim that the internet was designed solely to survive nuclear war is an overstatement the participants have spent decades correcting. Survivability was one strand in the origins; the ARPANET itself was built to share expensive computers between Defense contractors (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`).

## Engelbart as deliverable

Douglas Engelbart's 1968 demonstration is known as the Mother of All Demos (Fisher 2018, preface). In ninety minutes he showed a mouse, hypertext, windowed displays, and collaborative editing on a live system. The interface ideas reappear at Xerox PARC and then in personal computers the public could buy. The demo is remembered as a birth moment. It was also a deliverable on a federal contract at the same institute that held Stanford ownership while doing work students would soon protest.

## Budget lines the students will read

By 1968 close to thirty-five per cent of Stanford's electrical engineering electronics research funding was classified, concentrated in the Applied Electronics Laboratory (`books/cardinal/ch/4-07-april-third-movement.qmd`). Lockheed payrolls, SRI contracts, ground rent on Page Mill, and chip sales into guidance systems all drew on the same federal stream.

When students occupied the AEL in April 1969, they were not imagining a militarised university. They had documented a change in the budget. This chapter names the substrate so the occupation reads as response, not tantrum.

## The forgotten employer, restated

Hewlett-Packard and Fairchild matter in legend. Lockheed mattered in payroll — tens of thousands in Sunnyvale against hundreds in a garage on Addison Avenue, for years (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). Both were true on the same map. Only one fit the origin story the region preferred to tell about itself.

The defence corridor does not end in April 1969. It changes legal address when SRI is sold to itself and contracts walk from the quad to Menlo Park. The procurement office keeps buying. The internet's first word remains "lo," typed before the crash, between nodes the Defense Department commissioned on an institute Stanford still owned when the packet left UCLA.

## Moffett Field's inheritance

Moffett Field in Sunnyvale had been built for airships in the 1930s and became an aeronautical research centre. NASA Ames Research Center inherited that establishment — wind tunnels, computing, space science — on the same corridor as Lockheed's missile division (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). Geography that had been logistical for the Pacific war remained logistical for missiles and satellites.

## Suppliers, not heroes

Around Lockheed, SRI, and Ames accumulated electronics firms, instrument makers, and the semiconductor companies from the previous chapters. None of them needed a consumer market in the 1960s to survive. They needed a customer with a procurement office and a tolerance for secrecy. HP's oscillators, Varian's klystrons, Fairchild's chips — each could be traced to federal budgets before they could be traced to living rooms.

## Stanford ownership as liability, foreshadowed

SRI's founding in 1946 as a Stanford-owned nonprofit in Menlo Park was convenient for contract research and inconvenient for accountability (`books/cardinal/ch/4-04-defense-corridor.qmd`). By the late 1960s the institute's work included chemical and biological warfare research and counterinsurgency techniques relevant to Vietnam — on the public record, under a parent university students could petition.

Classification made the arrangement opaque to citizens while keeping it legible to agencies. The Applied Electronics Laboratory on campus concentrated classified electronics funding by 1968. The corridor was not only geographic. It was a chain of ownership and clearance levels that would break when students published what they found inside the AEL printshop.

## Corrective summary

Act II promised to put the procurement office at the centre of the story instead of the garage. This chapter keeps that promise for the 1956–1969 window: Lockheed as county payroll leader, SRI as Stanford-owned contractor, ARPANET as Defense procurement, Engelbart as federal deliverable, Fairchild's chips as missile and space inputs. The garage on Addison Avenue still matters. It matters the way a storefront matters on a street paid for by railroad bonds — real, formative, and not the whole ledger.
