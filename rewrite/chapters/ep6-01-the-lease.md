# Episode 6: The Park

## The Lease

**Stanford Industrial Park. 1953.**

The signature line was on the last page of a ground lease measured in decades. Varian Associates — Russell and Sigurd Varian, William Hansen's klystron work behind them — would put research and development on university land along Page Mill Road. Frederick Terman was on Varian's board and among its investors. “The first lease at the Stanford Industrial Park was granted to Varian’s R & D department in 1953—no surprise, because Terman was an investor and board…” (Harris 2023, ch. 3.1).

The ink dried. Rent would flow to a university that could not sell the dirt beneath the buildings.

That is the cold open: not a garage, not a transistor, but a landlord selecting a tenant.

---

**Palo Alto. 1885.**

The Founding Grant of 1885 forbids the university to sell its land.

Leland Stanford wrote that clause, or had it written, to protect his son's memorial from future trustees who might liquidate it. It is a grieving father's provision, and for sixty-six years it functioned exactly as a dead hand provision usually does: it locked up an asset and prevented anyone from doing anything useful with it.

Then somebody noticed that a university which cannot sell land can still lease it.

## The problem, restated as an opportunity

By the late 1940s Stanford had eight thousand acres of the San Francisco Peninsula and a chronic shortage of money. The endowment was adequate rather than large. The land was used for grazing, agriculture, and a golf course, and generated very little.

Terman and the university's business manager, Alf Brandin, worked out the implications. Long-term ground leases would produce income without violating the Grant. And the tenants could be chosen.

That last point is the one that matters. A landlord who selects tenants on the basis of strategic fit rather than rent is exercising industrial policy over a region.

## 1951

The Stanford Industrial Park — later the Research Park — opened in 1951 along Page Mill Road, the first university-owned industrial park in the United States. Varian Associates was the first tenant, for the reasons the previous episode described: it was founded by Stanford researchers on Stanford technology, and it was already entangled with the institution.

Eastman Kodak took space. General Electric came. Hewlett-Packard moved from its various rented premises to the park in 1956 and stayed, which meant the company that had started in the garage at 367 Addison Avenue became a tenant of the university whose professor had told it to stay in California.

The leases were long — fifty-one years and then ninety-nine — and the terms included restrictions on what could be built and who could occupy it. The university was not merely collecting rent. It was zoning.

Hansen Way runs from Page Mill Road to El Camino Real, through what is now the Stanford Research Park. It is named for William Webster Hansen, the physicist whose klystron and microwave work seeded Varian Associates and the defense-electronics industry that filled the park. He died of beryllium exposure. The road is a lumber track that became an industrial one, named for a man the federal contracts killed, running through land the university was forbidden to sell.

## Four roles at once

Here is what makes the arrangement different in kind from any other university-industry relationship then in existence. Stanford occupied four distinct positions with respect to the firms on its land, simultaneously.

It was their **landlord**, on long leases at terms it set, on land it could not sell and therefore had no incentive to develop for any other purpose.

It was their **talent supply**, producing the engineers they hired and, through the Honors Cooperative Program from 1954, continuing to educate those engineers after they were hired. Employees of member companies could take graduate courses part-time, with their employers paying the fees. A university that educates a company's staff for the duration of their careers is not a supplier; it is infrastructure.

It was their **research partner**, through sponsored research, consulting relationships, and faculty who sat on their boards or founded them.

And it was their **legitimacy anchor**. A young company on Stanford land, staffed by Stanford graduates, doing work adjacent to Stanford research, was legible to federal contracting officers and to investors in a way that an identical company in an industrial district thirty miles away was not.

No other institution in the country held all four. That combination is what the chapter means in calling the university an operating system for a regional economy: not a metaphor about influence, but a literal description of an entity supplying the land, the labour, the research, and the credentialling for an industry growing on top of it.

## Follow the revenue

The park's early tenants were not consumer companies and the chapter keeps the substrate visible.

Varian made klystrons and microwave equipment, and its customers were military. Hewlett-Packard's instrument business depended heavily on defence and aerospace purchasing. Lockheed's missile division would arrive in Sunnyvale in 1956.

The private-sector face of the arrangement sat on a public-sector income stream. Federal procurement paid for most of it, and the park was the channel by which that money became a real estate portfolio, a graduate programme, and a set of private fortunes.

## The loop

Federal money funds research in the fields Terman selected. The research produces technology and trained people. Faculty and graduates found companies to commercialise it. The companies lease land in the park, which pays the university. Their employees enrol in the cooperative programme, which pays the university. Their research needs sponsor further work, which pays the university and generates the next technology. Their success attracts the next generation of students and faculty, which strengthens the steeples, which attracts more federal money.

Every stage of that loop feeds the next, and the university takes a position at every stage.

## Who could live nearby

The workforce for all this had to live somewhere, and where it could live had been determined before any of it was built.

Palo Alto, Menlo Park, Atherton, and the surrounding towns operated restrictive covenants of the kind described in the previous episodes, and the federal underwriting maps graded the area accordingly. The covenants became judicially unenforceable in 1948 but remained in deeds and continued to be honoured informally, and the practices of local real estate boards and lenders sustained the pattern for decades afterwards.

East Palo Alto, immediately across the freeway from the university and the park, became substantially Black in the same decades, with a very different tax base, different schools, and no access to the housing appreciation happening a mile away (Harris 2023, ch. 3.1).

The industrial park's labour force was filtered by residence before it was filtered by hiring. The boundary is still visible from the road.

## The operating system is running

By 1955 the machine had been assembled and switched on. Everything in the rest of this episode is an application built on top of it: semiconductors, then defence corridors, then a student occupation in a laboratory Terman had helped fill with classified work.

The first application arrives the following year, when a Nobel laureate moves to Mountain View to be near his mother.

The lease in 1953 was the signature. The park was already open. The future was a tenant list on university paper, paid in rent on land a railroad baron's will had locked in place half a century earlier.

## Varian's sequence

Russell and Sigurd Varian, with William Hansen, had developed the klystron at Stanford before the war under an arrangement that gave the university royalty share. Varian Associates was founded in 1948. The 1953 lease put R&D on university land with Terman on the board and among investors — “no surprise,” as Harris puts it, because the insider signature was the point (Harris 2023, ch. 3.1).

## HP arrives

Hewlett-Packard moved from rented premises to the park in 1956. The company that began in the garage at 367 Addison Avenue became a long-term tenant of the institution whose professor had told it to stay in California. The garage myth and the lease myth are the same story told at different scales: stay on the Peninsula, build where Terman wanted the talent, pay the university for the ground.

## Who could not lease

The workforce had to live somewhere. Restrictive covenants and FHA maps sorted who could buy within reach of Page Mill Road. East Palo Alto, across the freeway, became substantially Black with a different tax base and different schools (Harris 2023, ch. 3.1). The park's labour market was filtered by residence before hiring.

By 1955 the operating system was running. The next chapter is a Nobel laureate moving to Mountain View because his mother lived there — contingency sitting atop a lease that could not have been signed if the Founding Grant had allowed a sale.

## The other university

Berkeley in 1951 was the stronger institution by most measures. It had the Radiation Laboratory, the Manhattan Project pedigree, more Nobel laureates, and larger federal research funding. If you had been asked in 1951 which Bay Area university would anchor a technology industry, the answer would have been obvious and wrong.

Berkeley did not build an industrial park, and could not have. It is a public university, embedded in a city, on a constrained site, governed by regents appointed through a political process and accountable to a state legislature. It could not have leased public land to private companies on preferential terms without a fight it would have lost, and its faculty consulting and spin-out arrangements were more restricted.

Stanford was private, governed by a self-perpetuating board answerable to nobody, and sitting on eight thousand contiguous acres that could not be sold and therefore had to be used.

The Founding Grant's insulation from politics, written in 1885 to protect a memorial, turned out sixty-six years later to be the decisive competitive advantage in a business nobody had imagined. Berkeley did the better science for decades and Stanford captured the industry, and the difference is a governance structure (`books/cardinal/ch/4-01-termans-garden.qmd`).

## Terman as provost

Terman became provost in 1955 and ran the university's academic strategy for a decade. The industrial park expanded. By the mid-1960s the region had a name for itself as a technology centre, and by 1971 a trade journalist would supply the label that stuck.

The cold open's signature in 1953 on Varian's lease was not the beginning. It was the visible moment after the park opened in 1951 — insider board membership made predictable what the Founding Grant made possible. Land that could not be sold had to be leased. Terman chose who leased it.

## Alf Brandin

Terman and the university's business manager, Alf Brandin, worked out the implications of long-term ground leases: income without violating the Grant, and tenants selected for strategic fit. A landlord who picks companies is running industrial policy over a region, not collecting rent alone.

Fifty-one-year leases, then ninety-nine. Restrictions on what could be built and who could occupy space. Eastman Kodak, General Electric, HP in 1956 — each tenancy another loop in the federal-money circuit described in the previous episode.

## 1953 again

When Varian's R&D department signed in 1953, Terman was investor and board member (Harris 2023, ch. 3.1). The first lease at the Stanford Industrial Park was granted to Varian's R & D department — no surprise, because Terman was on the board and among the investors. The sentence is Harris's; the fact is the episode's cold open.

The through-line of Episode 6 is land that cannot be sold leased to the future. The signature line in 1953 is the future arriving on Hansen Way, on dirt the grant locked, under terms the university wrote.

## Night on Page Mill, 1954

A security guard walks the edge of a construction pad where Varian's new footprint meets Kodak's loading dock. The leases run fifty-one years, then ninety-nine. The university's business office in the morning will record another ground-rent check. In the afternoon a graduate student from the Honors Cooperative Programme will sit in a Hewlett-Packard conference room after her shift, taking a microwave course her employer pays for. Her professor consulted for Varian last month. Her thesis advisor sits on a board. She does not yet know the word ecosystem. She knows she cannot afford Palo Alto on an technician's wage unless she lives with three roommates south of campus.

That is four roles in one evening: landlord, talent supply, research partner, legitimacy anchor. No other American university held all four in 1954. Berkeley had Lawrence's lab and political regents. Stanford had eight thousand acres it could not sell and a provost who had spent the war in Cambridge learning how federal money moved.

The cold open's pen in 1953 merely made visible what 1951's park opening began. Terman on Varian's board. Varian's klystron royalties paying the university. Varian's lease paying again. The loop closed before Shockley hired Noyce. The loop would survive Shockley, Fairchild, SRI's sale, and every poster on the quad.

Land that cannot be sold must be leased. The future signs the last page of the ground lease and calls it research and development.
