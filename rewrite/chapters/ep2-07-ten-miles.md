# Episode 2: The Toll

## Ten Miles

April 1869.

Charles Crocker offered a bet of $10,000 to Durant, saying that the CP would lay ten miles of track in one day (Ambrose 2000, ch. 16).

## The subsidy, and where it went

The public contribution was enormous. Land grants of alternate sections along
the right of way, doubled in 1864 to twenty sections per mile — which is to say
twenty square miles of land for every mile of track. Government bonds at six per
cent, issued per mile, graduated by terrain: sixteen thousand dollars a mile
across the flat, thirty-two thousand across the high plains, and forty-eight
thousand a mile in the mountains.

That last figure produced an immediate and characteristic manoeuvre. Where the
mountains began was a question of geology, and geology was a matter of expert
opinion, and the Central Pacific obtained an opinion placing the base of the
Sierra Nevada considerably further west than any geologist unconnected to the
company would have put it. Lincoln accepted it. The company collected the
mountain rate across some twenty-odd miles of what was, by any ordinary
description, valley floor.

The larger method was the construction company. The Central Pacific
contracted the building of its line to the Contract and Finance Company, which
was owned by the same four men. The railroad — a corporation with public
obligations, public credit, and eventually public shareholders — paid the
construction firm, which was private and theirs, at prices they set on both
sides of the transaction.

The profit was captured at the point of transfer. The railroad could show modest
returns while the men controlling it became extraordinarily rich, and the
accounts of the Contract and Finance Company were destroyed before a
congressional investigation could examine them.

Keep this manoeuvre in mind. Later, a university under pressure over its
military research will divest the research institute to itself, and the
structure of the transaction will be recognisable.

## The workforce

Crocker's construction superintendent, James Strobridge, opposed hiring Chinese
workers. Crocker overruled him — the labour supply was inadequate, white workers
left for the mines constantly, and a trial group performed well. From 1865 the
company recruited heavily, first in California and then directly from Guangdong.

They were paid less than white workers doing the same work, and unlike white
workers they were not provided with board, which they organised themselves. The
work was lethal. The Summit Tunnel was driven through granite by hand and with
black powder and nitroglycerine, at a rate measured in inches a day, through two
of the worst winters on record. Men were killed by explosions, rockfalls,
avalanches, and cold. The company did not systematically record their deaths,
which is why every figure offered is an estimate and why the estimates vary so
widely.

In June 1867 several thousand Chinese workers in the Sierra struck. Their
demands were an increase in pay to match white workers, a reduction in the
working day in the tunnels, and an end to the practice of whipping and of
restraining workers from leaving. It was, by any measure, one of the largest
labour actions in the United States to that date.

Crocker broke it in about a week by cutting off the food supply.

They went back to work. Two years later they finished the line, and on 28 April
1869 a Central Pacific crew — Chinese, with a handful of Irish rail handlers —
laid ten miles and fifty-six feet of track in a single day to win a bet with the
Union Pacific. It has never been beaten.

Thirteen years later the United States passed a law barring Chinese labourers
from entering the country. The next chapters describe how.

## What the monopoly then did

With the line complete, the Central Pacific and its successor the Southern
Pacific held the only rail connection between California and the east, and
proceeded to price it.

Rates were set not by cost but by what a shipper could bear — a practice the
company defended openly as charging what the traffic will bear. A grower in the
Central Valley had one way to reach a market. A town that cooperated got a
station; a town that did not got a line routed around it and ceased to exist.
The company acquired control of the shipping and the ferries as well, so that
alternatives to its rails led to its docks.

Whoever owns the rails sets the prices. The earlier chapters established the pattern and this
chapter supplies the American address.

Four men now held a fortune built on federal land, federal credit, and a
workforce paid a differential that was never made up. One of them was about to
convert his share of it into something that could not be regulated, taxed, or
broken up.

## Both chairs

Stanford was elected Governor of California in 1861 and served from January 1862
to December 1863 — precisely the period in which the Pacific Railway Act passed,
the Central Pacific was organised, and California committed public support to
the enterprise.

He was simultaneously president of the Central Pacific Railroad.

As governor he advocated the railroad as a public necessity, which it arguably
was. He signed legislation providing state and county assistance to it. He
presided over the groundbreaking in Sacramento in January 1863 in both
capacities, and nobody at the ceremony appears to have regarded the arrangement
as remarkable.

There was no conflict-of-interest framework to violate. The roles were simply
held at the same time by the same man, and the disclosure was total: everyone
knew. This is worth dwelling on, because it is the cleanest illustration in the
book of a point the Voice Guide insists on. Nothing here is concealed. The
method does not depend on secrecy. It depends on a structural arrangement
being visible and unremarkable.
