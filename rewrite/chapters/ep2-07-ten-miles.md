# Episode 2: The Toll

## Ten Miles

28 April 1869. Utah desert, Central Pacific grade.

Charles Crocker offered a bet of ten thousand dollars to Thomas Durant of the Union Pacific, saying the Central Pacific would lay ten miles of track in one day.

<!-- kyle: moments/Ambrose ch 16 -->

Durant ran the eastern company the way the Associates ran the western one: as a race for subsidies, land, and credit. The bet was not a sporting gesture. It was a public-relations weapon in a contest over which line could claim the continent’s attention while both hauled iron toward Promontory. Ten miles in a day had never been done. Crocker intended to do it anyway.

Ten thousand dollars was a fortune to a rail hand and a line item to a man who would invoice the work through a construction company he co-owned. The wager made the newspapers. The invoices made the fortune. Both depended on Chinese crews who had already struck once and been starved back to the grade.

Organizing such a day required the same brutality the Sierra had taught. Rails, ties, and spikes had to be staged for miles. Teams had to be fed before dawn and moved in relays. Chinese crews who had been paid less than white workers, who had struck in 1867 and been starved back to work, would shoulder the heaviest share again. The record—ten miles and fifty-six feet—still stands. It is remembered as triumph. It is also the arithmetic of a company that had learned to treat men as movable parts in a wager between millionaires.

Think of what ten miles means for a body.

A mile of track is ties, plates, spikes, and rail lifted, aligned, and driven home by crews working in relays. Ten miles in a single day meant staging material far ahead of the railhead, feeding men before light, and keeping the line of wagons and handlers moving without the usual stop at sundown. It meant Chinese crews who had built the Sierra grade doing again, in desert heat instead of snow, what the company had trained them to do: move iron faster than anyone believed possible.

Durant watched from the Union Pacific side of the contest. Crocker collected on the bet. The record still stands—not because the day was humane, but because it was organized with military precision for a single purpose: to prove the Central Pacific could outwork its rival in public.

They finished the line two weeks later. The photograph at Promontory would not show them.

## The subsidy, and where it went

The public contribution that made the wager possible was enormous.

Land grants of alternate sections along the right of way, doubled in 1864 to twenty sections per mile—twenty square miles of land for every mile of track. Government bonds at six per cent, issued per mile, graduated by terrain: sixteen thousand dollars a mile across the flat, thirty-two thousand across the high plains, and forty-eight thousand a mile in the mountains.

<!-- kyle: ch/2-09-railroads-first-monopoly -->

Where the mountains began was a question of geology, and geology was a matter of expert opinion. The Central Pacific obtained an opinion placing the base of the Sierra Nevada considerably further west than any geologist unconnected to the company would have put it. Lincoln accepted it. The company collected the mountain rate across some twenty-odd miles of what was, by any ordinary description, valley floor.

The larger method was the construction company. The Central Pacific contracted the building of its line to the Contract and Finance Company, which was owned by the same four men. The railroad—a corporation with public obligations, public credit, and eventually public shareholders—paid the construction firm, which was private and theirs, at prices they set on both sides of the transaction.

The profit was captured at the point of transfer. The railroad could show modest returns while the men controlling it became extraordinarily rich, and the accounts of the Contract and Finance Company were destroyed before a congressional investigation could examine them.

Collis Huntington feared that investigators would demand to see those books.

<!-- kyle: moments/Rayner ch 9 -->

The fear is instructive. The line was not complete when Huntington began worrying about ledgers in Washington. The ceremony at Promontory was still days away. The Associates already knew which numbers they did not want read in a committee room.

## Both chairs, briefly

Leland Stanford had been Governor of California from January 1862 to December 1863 while also serving as president of the Central Pacific. He advocated the railroad as a public necessity, signed state assistance, and presided over the Sacramento groundbreaking in both capacities. There was no conflict-of-interest framework to violate. Everyone knew. The arrangement was visible and unremarkable—which is its own kind of power.

Stanford would drive the ceremonial spike at Promontory and miss. The public would cheer anyway. The telegraph would report “done!”. The public subsidy would remain in private hands.

## What the monopoly then did

With the line complete, the Central Pacific and its successor the Southern Pacific held the only rail connection between California and the east, and proceeded to price it.

Rates were set not by cost but by what a shipper could bear—a practice the company defended openly as charging what the traffic will bear. A grower in the Central Valley had one way to reach a market. A town that cooperated got a station; a town that did not got a line routed around it and ceased to exist. The company acquired control of shipping and ferries as well, so that alternatives to its rails led to its docks.

Four men now held a fortune built on federal land, federal credit, and a workforce paid a differential that was never made up. One of them was about to convert his share into something that could not be regulated, taxed, or broken up—a university on rancho land that could not be sold.

That conversion belongs to a later episode. The toll road belongs to this one.

## The bells and the books

The nation heard bells on 10 May 1869. Editors called it binding. Engineers shook hands for the camera. Stanford’s mallet struck rail and the wire closed.

The Contract and Finance Company books were already being kept where no passenger would look. Huntington’s Washington work ensured that the margin stayed on the private side of the transfer while the risk stayed on the public side of the charter.

The Chinese workers who laid ten miles in a day were not in the photograph. The men who starved a strike in the Sierra were not on the platform. The Americans who died on the isthmus in 1849 were not remembered at all.

The episode ends on that split: a public story told in spikes and bells, and a private story told in ledgers that would burn before investigators could read them.

The border had moved nine days after gold in the previous generation. In this one, the continent was “joined” while the toll was installed. The next episode would ask what a monopoly does when it owns the only way through—and whom it chooses to blame when people notice.

## Epilogue at the wire

On 10 May 1869 the telegraph operator closed the circuit. Bells rang in cities that would never see the Summit Tunnel or the isthmus mud. Listeners heard “done!” and understood completion.

Completion was the wrong word. Transfer was the right one.

Public land had been granted. Public bonds had been issued. Private construction companies had captured the margin. Labour that had struck in the snow had been broken at the commissary. Labour that had laid ten miles in a day had been excluded from the frame. Labour that had died on the isthmus had been folded into a merchant’s letter home.

The Associates would price the line. They would buy legislatures. They would acquire ferries and wharves until shippers could not escape their rates. One of them would build a university that could not sell its land, turning a railroad fortune into an institution that would outlast any antitrust suit.

But that is forward. At the wire, only the sound travelled instantly. The books moved more slowly, and some of them did not move at all—they were destroyed before anyone outside the four merchants could read the margin.

The nation celebrated. The toll was open. The ledgers were already elsewhere.

## What “done” left out

Read the Pacific Railway Act as a price list and Promontory as a receipt.

The Act paid in land checkerboarded along the route—acres that would become towns, farms, and eventually suburban real estate without the railroad ever needing to haul another tie. It paid in bonds graded by terrain, with mountain miles worth more than valley miles, and with the definition of “mountain” negotiable by experts on the company payroll.

The Contract and Finance Company sat between the public railroad and the private fortunes. It invoiced the work. It set the prices. It destroyed its books when investigators drew near.

The Chinese workforce that made the ten-mile day possible had been imported when white labour would not stay, paid less when it did, struck when the terms became unbearable, and starved back to the grade when the company refused to bargain.

The isthmus deaths Huntington recorded in 1849 were not on the platform either. They were part of the same supply chain: bodies moving toward California, money moving toward whoever owned the bottleneck.

“Done!” was a telegraph word. It was not an accounting word. The accounting continued in rooms Huntington controlled, on paper Hopkins kept, and in courts the Associates would learn to treat as another line item.

When the Wilson Committee and other investigators later pressed for the construction company’s records, they would find fire and absence where ledgers should have been. By then the monopoly would already own the only way out of the Central Valley. By then the bells would already be memory.

The episode ends where it began: a ceremony visible from coast to coast, and a set of books kept where passengers never went. The toll was not the spike. The toll was everything that happened after the wire said done.

## Stanford’s two hats

Stanford had been elected governor in 1861 and served through 1863 while serving as president of the Central Pacific. He signed state assistance. He spoke of the railroad as necessity. He stood at the Sacramento groundbreaking in January 1863 wearing both offices without embarrassment, because no law forbade it and no constituency powerful enough to stop the line wanted to try.

At Promontory he wore the face the East wanted: benefactor, statesman, man of the spike. The mallet missed. The wire closed. The public heard completion.

The private side was Hopkins’s ledgers, Huntington’s fear of committees, Crocker’s bets, and a construction company that existed to invoice the railroad at prices the same four men set. That side did not miss. It collected.

## The grower at the station

Picture a wheat grower in the Central Valley after 1869. One railroad. One rate set by what the traffic will bear. One ferry connection if the company owns the dock. Cooperation meant a siding; resistance meant rerouting until a town dried up.

That is the toll in daily life—not a spike in Utah, but a invoice in Sacramento for moving crop to market. The public had paid to build the line. The Associates charged to use it.

The bells rang for binding. The grower would pay for passage.

## Durant’s view

Thomas Durant had his own subsidies to protect on the Union Pacific side of the bet. The contest over ten miles in a day was not friendship. It was rivalry between companies that both lived on federal land and federal bonds, each trying to prove it deserved the next tranche of credit.

When Crocker’s crews set the record, Durant paid the wager and lost face. The Central Pacific gained prestige it would convert into political cover when rate-setting angered shippers. Prestige is cheaper than rebates.

The bet mattered because it was public. The books mattered because they were private. Huntington understood both currencies. Hopkins kept both ledgers. Stanford provided the photograph.

## One sentence on fire

The accounts of the Contract and Finance Company were destroyed before a congressional investigation could examine them. Rayner’s account of Huntington’s fear that the Wilson Committee would demand those books is the shadow of that fire in Washington correspondence.

Fire is convenient when margin lives on the construction side and modesty lives on the railroad side. The public saw ties and spikes. The investigators saw smoke.

The episode closes on that split: bells for the public, ashes for the committee, and a single rail line charging what the traffic will bear for decades to come.

Collis Huntington would spend the post-Promontory years making sure the committee never got a clean read of the margin. Leland Stanford would spend his turning part of the fortune into stone and campus on rancho land. Charles Crocker would spend his defending rates. Mark Hopkins would spend his in ledgers that looked modest next to the construction invoices.

The Chinese workers who laid ten miles in April and vanished from the photograph in May would spend theirs in exclusion law and erasure—a cost the episode records here so the ceremony cannot pretend it was the whole story.

The toll was open. The books were already elsewhere. The wire had said done.

## Both chairs

Stanford was elected Governor of California in 1861 and served from January 1862 to December 1863 — precisely the period in which the Pacific Railway Act passed, the Central Pacific was organised, and California committed public support to the enterprise. He was simultaneously president of the Central Pacific Railroad.

<!-- kyle: ch/2-09-railroads-first-monopoly -->

As governor he advocated the railroad as a public necessity. He signed legislation providing state and county assistance. He presided over the Sacramento groundbreaking in January 1863 in both capacities, and nobody at the ceremony appears to have regarded the arrangement as remarkable. The roles were simply held at the same time by the same man, and the disclosure was total: everyone knew.

Nothing here depended on secrecy. It depended on a structural arrangement being visible and unremarkable. That is how public subsidy becomes private fortune without a scandal—openly, while bells still ring for the spike.
