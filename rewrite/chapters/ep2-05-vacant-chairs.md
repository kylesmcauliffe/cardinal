# Episode 2: The Toll

## Vacant Chairs

1853–1862. Washington.

On 21 January 1861, days before leaving the Senate to help lead a rebellion,
Jefferson Davis spoke in favour of a transcontinental railroad.

"I have thought it an achievement worthy of our age and of our people," he said,
"to couple with bonds of iron the people of the Pacific with the valley of the
Mississippi" — and to show that even snow-capped mountains intervening could not
divide them.

He was about to divide the country. And his departure is the reason the railroad
was finally built, on a route he had spent eight years opposing.

This winter in Washington would decide the railroad's route. Everything that followed — the
Big Four, their fortunes, the university one of them founded, the corridor that
university anchors — rests on a decision that was made in Washington by
subtraction.

## The surveys

On 3 March 1853 Congress appropriated a hundred and fifty thousand dollars for
the War Department to determine the most practicable and economical route for a
railroad from the Mississippi to the Pacific. The work was given to the Corps of
Topographical Engineers, under the direction of the Secretary of War.

The Secretary of War was Jefferson Davis.

Four principal corridors were surveyed: along the 47th and 49th parallels in the
north, the 37th to 39th through the central Rockies, the 35th across the
southwest, and the 32nd along the southern border. Parties went into the field
within two months. The results were published as a twelve-volume report, one of
the great documents of nineteenth-century American science, full of geology,
botany, ethnography, and superb lithographs.

As a decision procedure it failed completely. Every field commander reported
having found the most practicable and economical route to the Pacific. Stevens
made the case for the north, Whipple for the 35th parallel, Beckwith for the
central line, Parke and Pope for the south. Congress, having spent two years and
a considerable sum, was exactly where it had started.

## Davis's conclusion

Davis was not in doubt, and the chapter gives his case its full strength because
the case was substantially sound.

He reported for the 32nd parallel, and the engineering arguments were real. It
was the shortest of the routes, at under seventeen hundred miles. It was
estimated as the cheapest, at something in the region of seventy-two million
dollars. It ran at low elevations through country with mild winters, avoiding
the snow that would eventually require the Central Pacific to build miles of
timber snowsheds over its track. It passed through territory that was already
organised.

On the merits available in 1855, a competent engineer with no sectional interest
could have reached the same conclusion. That is what makes the chapter work. The
southern route was not obviously wrong. It was obviously southern, and everyone
in Washington understood that a railroad determines where population, commerce,
and political weight accumulate for the next century.

Davis was from Mississippi. He had served in both houses for that state. He
would become president of the Confederacy, and he continued to advocate a
southern railroad after secession.

## Gadsden

The clearest evidence that this was never merely an engineering question is
James Gadsden.

Gadsden was a South Carolinian, a former army officer, a plantation owner, and
president of the South Carolina Railroad. He had advocated secession in 1850. He
wanted San Diego as the Pacific terminus of a southern line, and he had earlier
supported a proposal to divide California into two states, with the southern
half open to slavery.

In 1853 he was appointed minister to Mexico, and he negotiated the purchase of a
strip of territory south of the Gila River — the Gadsden Purchase — which
resolved the one significant obstacle to the 32nd-parallel route, a section that
would otherwise have run through Mexican territory.

The United States bought land from a foreign country in order to make a
particular railroad route feasible, and the man who negotiated it wanted that
route because it would carry a slave economy to the Pacific. The chapter states
this plainly and lets it stand.

## Eight years of nothing

And then nothing happened, for eight years.

Both major parties put a Pacific railroad in their platforms in 1856 and again
in 1860. Bills were introduced. None passed. Northern members would not fund a
southern route; southern members would not fund a northern one; and neither side
would accept a central compromise that gave the other an advantage in the
territories.

The deadlock was not technical and could not be resolved technically, which is
why the twelve volumes of survey data changed nothing. A survey can establish
grades and rainfall. It cannot resolve a distributive question, because the
parties to a distributive question are not disagreeing about facts.

## The chairs empty

Then, in the winter of 1860 and 1861, the southern members left.

The arithmetic of the chamber changed completely and at once. With no prospect
of a southern route being adopted, and nobody present to oppose a central one,
Congress found that it had very little difficulty agreeing terms. As the
National Park Service's own history of the line puts it, senators and
representatives had little trouble settling on an acceptable bill once the
opposition was gone.

Theodore Judah spent the winter of 1861 and 1862 in Washington lobbying for it.
Judah was the engineer who had surveyed a practicable line over the Sierra
Nevada, and who had persuaded four Sacramento merchants to incorporate the
Central Pacific Railroad of California in June 1861 to build it. He was
relentless, well prepared, and personally responsible for a great deal of the
bill's technical content.

Lincoln supported it, on two grounds that were both about the war: a
transcontinental line was a military asset, and binding the Pacific coast to the
Union was a strategic necessity at a moment when the Union's continued existence
was not assured.

The bill moved through Congress in May and June of 1862. Lincoln signed it on 1
July.

## What the Act did

The Pacific Railway Act authorised two companies — the Union Pacific building
west from the Missouri River at Council Bluffs, and the Central Pacific building
east from the navigable waters of the Sacramento River — to construct a railroad
and telegraph line, and it paid them in two currencies.

The first was land: alternate sections along the right of way, in the
checkerboard pattern described in the opening episodes chapter 8, subsequently doubled by
amendment. The second was credit: thirty-year government bonds at six per cent,
issued per mile of track completed, at rates that varied with terrain and were
highest in the mountains.

The Union Pacific was chartered by the federal government directly — the first
corporation so chartered since the Second Bank of the United States.

One correction is worth making because a reader checking the most obvious source
will hit it. The National Archives' summary page for the Act states that it
designated the 32nd parallel as the initial transcontinental route. That is
contradicted by the statute printed on the same page, which specifies Council
Bluffs to Sacramento, and by the Senate's own historical account. Cite the
statute.

## What the vacancy purchased

Four merchants in Sacramento — a grocer, a hardware dealer, a dry-goods
merchant, and another hardware dealer — now held a federal charter, a land
grant, and a bond subsidy for the western half of the most valuable
infrastructure project in American history.

They had not invented anything. They had not surveyed the route; Judah had done
that, and they bought him out. What they had was position, at the moment the
chairs emptied.

The route was chosen by subtraction. The war fought to preserve the Union built
the machine that would govern California, and handed it to private hands on the
way through.
