# Episode 2: The Toll

## Vacant Chairs

1 July 1862. Washington.

The Senate chamber in midsummer heat held a different geography than it had held three years earlier. The desks that had belonged to members from the seceded states stood empty—not removed, not reassigned, simply vacant in a room where every vote now landed on one side of a war. The opposition that had blocked a central transcontinental route for nearly a decade was not present to block it anymore.

The Pacific Railway Act would be signed that day. Four Sacramento merchants who sold groceries and hardware would receive a federal charter, a checkerboard of public land along the right of way, and thirty-year government bonds paid per mile of track. The engineer who had surveyed the Sierra route would not be in the photograph when the line was finished. He was already dead of yellow fever in New York. The men who bought him out would be in every picture that mattered.

## Davis speaks

On 21 January 1861, days before leaving the Senate to help lead a rebellion, Jefferson Davis spoke in favour of a transcontinental railroad.

“I have thought it an achievement worthy of our age and of our people,” he said, “to couple with bonds of iron the people of the Pacific with the valley of the Mississippi” — and to show that even snow-capped mountains intervening could not divide them.

He was about to divide the country. And his departure is the reason the railroad was finally built, on a route he had spent eight years opposing.

The irony is too neat for fiction, which is why the record is useful. Davis had spent the 1850s arguing that the southern route was the practicable one. He would spend the early 1860s trying to destroy the Union that would eventually fund a line nowhere near his preferred terminus. His January speech is the last public embrace of a railroad as national glue from a man who was preparing to break the nation in two.

## The surveys

On 3 March 1853 Congress appropriated a hundred and fifty thousand dollars for the War Department to determine the most practicable and economical route for a railroad from the Mississippi to the Pacific. The work was given to the Corps of Topographical Engineers, under the direction of the Secretary of War.

The Secretary of War was Jefferson Davis.

Four principal corridors were surveyed: along the 47th and 49th parallels in the north, the 37th to 39th through the central Rockies, the 35th across the southwest, and the 32nd along the southern border. Parties went into the field within two months. The results were published as a twelve-volume report, one of the great documents of nineteenth-century American science, full of geology, botany, ethnography, and superb lithographs.

As a decision procedure it failed completely. Every field commander reported having found the most practicable and economical route to the Pacific. Stevens made the case for the north, Whipple for the 35th parallel, Beckwith for the central line, Parke and Pope for the south. Congress, having spent two years and a considerable sum, was exactly where it had started.

The surveys mapped more than grades. They mapped peoples whose territories lay across every corridor, reduced in the reports to obstacles, resources, and climate tables. A distributive fight wore the costume of a technical one.

## Davis's conclusion

Davis was not in doubt, and his case deserves its full strength because the case was substantially sound.

He reported for the 32nd parallel, and the engineering arguments were real. It was the shortest of the routes, at under seventeen hundred miles. It was estimated as the cheapest, at something in the region of seventy-two million dollars. It ran at low elevations through country with mild winters, avoiding the snow that would eventually require the Central Pacific to build miles of timber snowsheds over its track. It passed through territory that was already organised.

On the merits available in 1855, a competent engineer with no sectional interest could have reached the same conclusion. The southern route was not obviously wrong. It was obviously southern, and everyone in Washington understood that a railroad determines where population, commerce, and political weight accumulate for the next century.

Davis was from Mississippi. He had served in both houses for that state. He would become president of the Confederacy, and he continued to advocate a southern railroad after secession.

## Gadsden

The clearest evidence that this was never merely an engineering question is James Gadsden.

Gadsden was a South Carolinian, a former army officer, a plantation owner, and president of the South Carolina Railroad. He had advocated secession in 1850. He wanted San Diego as the Pacific terminus of a southern line, and he had earlier supported a proposal to divide California into two states, with the southern half open to slavery.

In 1853 he was appointed minister to Mexico, and he negotiated the purchase of a strip of territory south of the Gila River — the Gadsden Purchase — which resolved the one significant obstacle to the 32nd-parallel route, a section that would otherwise have run through Mexican territory.

The United States bought land from a foreign country in order to make a particular railroad route feasible, and the man who negotiated it wanted that route because it would carry a slave economy to the Pacific.

## Eight years of nothing

And then nothing happened, for eight years.

Both major parties put a Pacific railroad in their platforms in 1856 and again in 1860. Bills were introduced. None passed. Northern members would not fund a southern route; southern members would not fund a northern one; and neither side would accept a central compromise that gave the other an advantage in the territories.

The deadlock was not technical and could not be resolved technically. A survey can establish grades and rainfall. It cannot resolve a distributive question, because the parties to a distributive question are not disagreeing about facts.

## The chairs empty

Then, in the winter of 1860 and 1861, the southern members left.

The arithmetic of the chamber changed completely and at once. With no prospect of a southern route being adopted, and nobody present to oppose a central one, Congress found that it had very little difficulty agreeing terms. As the National Park Service's own history of the line puts it, senators and representatives had little trouble settling on an acceptable bill once the opposition was gone.

Theodore Judah spent the winter of 1861 and 1862 in Washington lobbying for it.

Judah was the engineer who had surveyed a practicable line over the Sierra Nevada, and who had persuaded four Sacramento merchants to incorporate the Central Pacific Railroad of California in June 1861 to build it. He was relentless, well prepared, and personally responsible for a great deal of the bill's technical content.

Lincoln supported it, on two grounds that were both about the war: a transcontinental line was a military asset, and binding the Pacific coast to the Union was a strategic necessity at a moment when the Union's continued existence was not assured.

The bill moved through Congress in May and June of 1862. Lincoln signed it on 1 July.

## What the Act did

The Pacific Railway Act authorised two companies — the Union Pacific building west from the Missouri River at Council Bluffs, and the Central Pacific building east from the navigable waters of the Sacramento River — to construct a railroad and telegraph line, and it paid them in two currencies.

The first was land: alternate sections along the right of way, in a checkerboard pattern, subsequently doubled by amendment. The second was credit: thirty-year government bonds at six per cent, issued per mile of track completed, at rates that varied with terrain and were highest in the mountains.

The Union Pacific was chartered by the federal government directly — the first corporation so chartered since the Second Bank of the United States.

One correction is worth making because a reader checking the most obvious source will hit it. The National Archives' summary page for the Act states that it designated the 32nd parallel as the initial transcontinental route. That is contradicted by the statute printed on the same page, which specifies Council Bluffs to Sacramento, and by the Senate's own historical account.

## What the vacancy purchased

Four merchants in Sacramento — a grocer, a hardware dealer, a dry-goods merchant, and another hardware dealer — now held a federal charter, a land grant, and a bond subsidy for the western half of the most valuable infrastructure project in American history.

They had not invented anything. They had not surveyed the route; Judah had done that, and they bought him out. What they had was position, at the moment the chairs emptied.

The route was chosen by subtraction. The war fought to preserve the Union built the machine that would govern California, and handed it to private hands on the way through.

Nobody in the empty desks voted on that July afternoon. Their absence was the vote that counted.

## Judah in the snowless city

Washington in the winter of 1861–62 was a city of mud streets and military trains, preparing for a war that had already begun. Theodore Judah was there without the polish of a lobbyist born to the capital. He was an engineer with maps, profiles, and the kind of patience that came from having surveyed a route over the Sierra and then having to persuade merchants—and now congressmen—that the line was possible if someone would pay for it.

He had incorporated the Central Pacific with four Sacramento businessmen in June 1861. He believed in the central route because he had walked it on paper and in the field. He was also, by the winter of his lobbying, already entangled with men who saw the railroad as a vehicle for personal fortune rather than a public work. That fight would kill his leverage before yellow fever killed him. For the moment, he needed statutes.

Lincoln’s support mattered for reasons that had little to do with California commerce. A transcontinental line was a military asset: troops, materiel, and messages could cross the interior faster than a voyage around the Horn. Binding the Pacific coast to the Union mattered at a moment when the Union’s continued existence was not assured. California had entered the Union as a free state in 1850, part of a sectional bargain. Keeping it tied to Washington was not an abstraction in 1862. It was strategy.

Judah supplied the technical scaffolding—the grades, the passes, the practicable alignment. The empty southern desks supplied the votes.

## Land and bonds, made human

The Act’s two currencies sound abstract until they are measured against a body of work.

Land grants in alternate sections created a checkerboard of public and railroad acreage along the right of way—later doubled to twenty sections per mile, which is to say twenty square miles of land for every mile of track laid. Government bonds at six per cent paid sixteen thousand dollars per mile on the flat, thirty-two thousand across the high plains, and forty-eight thousand in the mountains.

Those mountain miles would become a battlefield of geology reports. The Central Pacific would obtain an opinion placing the base of the Sierra Nevada farther west than independent geologists would have drawn it, collecting the higher rate across valley floor that any traveller would call valley. Lincoln accepted the opinion. The men laying track in snow would not be consulted.

The Union Pacific became the first corporation chartered directly by the federal government since the Second Bank of the United States. The Central Pacific was a California corporation that would behave, for the next decade, like a state within a state—setting rates, acquiring ferries, deciding which towns lived and which withered when the line passed them by.

## The road not taken

James Gadsden’s southern corridor did not disappear because it was absurd. It disappeared because the men who wanted it left the chamber.

The Gadsden Purchase bought the strip that made the 32nd-parallel survey legible on paper. The proposal to split California into two states—opening the southern half to slavery—shows how closely railroad politics tracked the slavery politics that emptied the desks. A reader who treats the Pacific Railway Act as a triumph of engineering alone will miss that the engineering had been available for years. What changed in 1862 was the seating chart.

Indigenous nations across the surveyed corridors appear in the expedition reports as ethnography and climate tables, not as parties to the decision. Their land was mapped as terrain to be crossed. The Act would cross it with iron whether or not anyone in those nations had consented.

## Stevens, Whipple, Beckwith, Parke, Pope

Name the commanders because the failure of the survey was personal as well as political. Stevens argued the north. Whipple argued the 35th parallel. Beckwith argued the central line. Parke and Pope argued the south. Each man had walked ground, measured grades, and returned to Washington with the certainty only fieldwork breeds—that his route was the one.

Congress received twelve volumes of science and zero consensus. The lithographs were beautiful. The botany was meticulous. The ethnography treated peoples as data. None of it answered the question Congress actually needed to settle: which section of the union would benefit from the next century of commerce.

Davis answered for the South. Secession removed his answer from the chamber. Judah answered for the Sierra. The Associates answered for themselves.

When Lincoln signed the Act on 1 July 1862, the empty desks were still empty. The route was no longer in doubt. The only doubt left was how much public money would flow into private hands before the spike was driven—and whether anyone at Promontory would miss.

## July heat

The signing itself was quiet compared with the war news that summer. Lincoln’s hand moved across a page authorizing two companies to bind the continent with iron and wire. Judah’s maps were in drawers and briefcases. The Associates’ incorporations were already filed in California. The Chinese workers who would die in the snow were not yet recruited under that name. The isthmus was already a memory for men like Huntington, who had learned to charge for passage long before they charged for freight.

The Act did not mention Promontory. It did not mention a golden spike. It mentioned land, bonds, and a right of way. Everything else—the bets, the strikes, the burned books, the photograph—would be built on top of those two currencies like track on ties.

The vacant chairs made that possible. The toll would follow.
