# Episode 1: The Ledger

## Baptismal Register

1777–1800s. Mission Santa Clara and the coast chain.

<!-- kyle: ch/2-01-mission-system -->

The street runs from El Camino Real to Serra Street, across the front of the
campus, and the sign says Galvez.

It is named for José de Gálvez (1720–1787), inspector general of New Spain — a
Bourbon administrator, not a priest. In 1767 he carried out the royal decree
expelling the Jesuits from the Spanish empire. The next year, under Carlos III,
he went to Baja California and appointed the two men who would actually walk
north: Gaspar de Portolá as military commander, and Junípero Serra, a
Franciscan, as religious leader of the Alta California expedition. The missions
were Gálvez's idea. Serra executed them. David Starr Jordan, the university's
first president, spent years naming the campus after the Spanish and Mexican
officials he wanted students to remember; Galvez Street is that decision, still
in force, at the front door.

That is the first artifact. The second is a line in a book those men caused to
be kept.

The entry gives a date, a Spanish name, a note of parentage, and the name of the
priest who performed the rite. What it does not give is the name the person had
the day before.

Mission baptismal registers are the founding documents of California, and they
are administrative records rather than spiritual ones. They were kept because
the system required them: a baptism created an obligation, assigned a location,
and produced a person who could be counted, assigned to work, pursued if he
left, and accounted for at the end of the year. The registers were reviewed by
superiors. They were the basis of reporting to Mexico City and to Madrid.

California's first form of power was the power to write people down. The campus
still writes the man who ordered it done.

## A system, not a settlement

Spain came north from Baja California in 1769 and did not send settlers, because
it did not have any to send. What it sent instead was a system with three parts.

The presidio supplied force: a garrison, usually small, frequently unpaid, and
chronically short of everything. The pueblo supplied provisions, in theory,
though the civilian towns established at San José and Los Angeles remained tiny
for decades. And the mission supplied labour and record, which made it the
component that actually did the work.

Twenty-one missions were eventually founded along the coast, linked by the road
that came to be called El Camino Real. Each was an agricultural enterprise —
grain, cattle, hides, tallow, wine — run by two or three Franciscan priests
using Indigenous labour, and each maintained registers of baptisms, marriages,
and burials that in many cases survive.

The strategic purpose was never conversion alone. Spain was in California
because Russian fur traders were moving down the Pacific coast and British ships
were in the north Pacific, and the empire had a claim it had not occupied.
Occupation required people, people required an economy, and the fastest
available route to both was to convert the existing population into a resident
agricultural workforce. The theological project and the imperial one arrived on
the same ships and were not, at the level of policy, easily separable.

## 320 Dolores Street

Mission San Francisco de Asís was founded in June 1776, a few days before a
document was signed on the other coast asserting that governments derive their
just powers from the consent of the governed.

The building is still there, at 320 Dolores Street. It is the oldest intact
structure in San Francisco. It survived the earthquake of 1906 when almost
nothing around it did, and it is worth walking into, because the scale is
instructive: it is small, thick-walled, and plain, and it looks exactly like
what it was, which is an administrative outpost with a chapel in it.

The Presidio was established a few weeks later at the tip of the peninsula,
guarding the entrance to the bay. Force and record, sited within a few miles of
each other, and between them the whole apparatus of Spanish California.

What Spain was actually claiming was the harbour. The Golden Gate is a narrow
entrance to one of the largest protected anchorages in the world, and the empire
that held it would control access to everything behind it. The mission and the
presidio are instrumentation around a passage, and the passage is the
reason either of them exists.

## What the labour actually was

The mission economy ran on Indigenous labour that was not free to leave.

Once baptised, a neophyte was considered subject to mission discipline.
Departure without permission was treated as flight, and soldiers were sent to
retrieve those who left. Work was assigned, rations were distributed, and
punishment for infractions was corporal and administered by the priests. Housing
was communal and, for unmarried women and girls, locked at night.

Contemporary observers noticed. Visitors from other European powers, including
French and Russian expeditions with no particular sympathy for Indigenous
Californians, recorded the arrangement in terms ranging from approving to
appalled. The Franciscans defended it in their own correspondence, which means
the terms of the debate are preserved and the arrangement is not in dispute.

The demographic outcome is the central fact of the period. Scholarly estimates
of the Indigenous population of California before contact vary substantially —
the most cited reconstructions put it somewhere in the range of three hundred
thousand, with a wide margin — and the population within the mission districts
fell catastrophically over the six decades of the mission system, principally
through introduced disease compounded by concentrated living conditions,
malnutrition, and disrupted subsistence. The figures differ by source and by
method, and the disagreement is worth naming rather than resolving with a single
round number.

Death rates inside the missions exceeded birth rates for most of the period,
which meant the system required continuous recruitment simply to maintain its
workforce. That is not an incidental cruelty. It is a structural property of the
arrangement, and it is visible in the missions' own registers, because the
missions counted burials too.

## The archive against the archivist

Which produces the strangest feature of this chapter's evidence.

Almost everything known in detail about Indigenous experience in the mission
period comes from records kept by the institution responsible. The registers,
the annual reports, the correspondence with superiors, the responses to official
questionnaires: all of it produced by Franciscans, for Franciscan and royal
purposes.

And it documents the resistance anyway. It records flight, and the expeditions
sent to recover the people who fled. It records revolts — at San Diego in 1775,
at Santa Barbara and elsewhere in 1824 — with names, causes, and outcomes. It
records the burials. An institution that counts everything ends up counting the
evidence against itself, which is a recurring piece of good fortune for the
historian and a recurring problem for the institution.

The registers are still consulted. Descendant communities, including the Muwekma
Ohlone, use them to establish lineage, which means a set of documents created to
convert people into an administrable population is now used by their descendants
to demonstrate that they were there. The instrument has outlived its purpose and
been turned around.

## Before markets

California in 1821 had no significant commerce, no meaningful currency, almost
no settlers, and a governing apparatus of a few hundred soldiers and a few dozen
priests. By any economic measure it was among the least developed places claimed
by any European power.

It also had a complete land system, a controlled labour force, and fifty years
of records.

Power began here before markets did, and it began as paperwork. Everything that follows — the ranchos, the American land commission, the railroad's grants,
the university's endowment — is a transfer of assets that the mission system had
already identified, mapped, and staffed.
